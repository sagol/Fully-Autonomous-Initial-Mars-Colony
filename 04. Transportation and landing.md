# 4. Transportation and landing

*Last reviewed: September 28, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, January 2036.* The first cargo ship of Wave 1 comes out of the pink sky tail first, three engines lit, a pillar of exhaust scouring the plain. Nobody on Earth watches live; the images arrive minutes after the ship has settled. Nine days later a second ship lands two kilometers to the east, aiming at the beacon the first one set down. It carries the reactor. Within a week three heavy rovers, hitched in tandem, are towing it at walking pace along a graded route toward the reactor field 2 km away. On Earth, the launch teams are already refurbishing tanker ships for the 2037 window.

[Chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md) picks the ground: flat, rock-poor plain over shallow ice in Arcadia Planitia. Every tonne the colony uses there before its first crew arrives must be launched from Earth, pushed onto a path to Mars, slowed from about 6 km/s at the top of the Martian atmosphere, and set down upright within reach of the tonne before it.

In brief: a Starship-class ship holds 1.7–2.7 times the propellant it needs to deliver 100 t to Mars in any window from 2033 to 2043. Cargo ships are therefore only partly refilled in Earth orbit, with 9 tanker flights each from 2035 to 2043, enough for the worst day of each launch period, and the landed mass is set by the lander and its entry, descent, and landing (EDL) system. Refueling in orbit, a fast launch cadence, and heavy landings on Mars are all undemonstrated as of September 2026. The crew's way home is a small Mars ascent vehicle (MAV) to Mars orbit, where an Earth return vehicle (ERV) waits, keeping its return propellant cold with its own cryocoolers.

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) fixes the campaign. An optional pathfinder flies in 2033, and three cargo waves (11 landers, 770 t) land in January 2036, October 2038, and September 2040. In September 2042 an ERV reaches Mars orbit and a support lander brings the surface half of the food reserve and spares; a crew of four departs in November 2043 with a way home, and a committed food flight follows in December 2045. Each ship is planned at ~100 t landed ([the reference vehicle](#the-reference-vehicle-starship-as-of-september-2026) gives the basis), and this chapter derives the tanker flights each ship needs.

## What any cargo lander must do

Any lander qualifies that can do four things.

First, it must reach low Earth orbit (LEO) and leave it with the window's departure energy, which for a heavy lander means refueling in orbit. That energy is C3, the square of the speed left after escaping Earth, in km²/s².

Second, it must survive entry at 5.5–6.6 km/s, slow down in an atmosphere with less than 1% of Earth's surface pressure, and land propulsively. NASA's human-scale lander studies (26–36 t landed) already rule out supersonic parachutes at this mass [18].

Third, it must land within a few hundred meters of a target without digging a crater under itself or blasting rocks into the ships already there.

Fourth, it must get its cargo to the ground: a 15 t reactor in a cargo bay tens of meters up is useless until something lowers it.

NASA's Design Reference Architecture 5.0 (DRA 5.0) sized cargo landers for about 40 t of payload [25], and a 2015 NASA study put the smallest lander able to support human missions at 43 t at Mars entry, sized by the MAV [22]. Landers of that class would need 20–40 landings for [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s 770 t [22], [25]. Because the plan is written in landed tonnes, any mix of landers that delivers the tonnage in the right windows closes the budget.

## The reference vehicle: Starship as of September 2026

Starship is the only vehicle in development sized to land about 100 t of cargo on Mars, and with its dry mass and landing propellant such a ship weighs about 300 t at entry (worked example below). SpaceX's current Starship page gives the V3 ship a propellant capacity of 1,600 t and more than 100 t to orbit in a fully reusable configuration [2]. The page gives no payload to the Martian surface, only several hundred tonnes of cargo carried to Mars after refilling in orbit [2]. This book's ~100 t landed per ship comes from a NASA-led architecture study and earlier SpaceX statements [12]. [Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) adopts it as a planning assumption, and nothing in this chapter derives it.

Waves 2 and 3 plan only 60–70 t of cargo per lander, leaving 30–40 t as margin for lander mass growth, EDL margin in the first heavy landings, and dispersions in the 2037 entry.

Both stages use Raptor 3, a full-flow staged-combustion methane and oxygen engine: 250–280 tf (2.45–2.75 MN) of sea-level thrust and 350 s specific impulse (Isp) by SpaceX's figures [2], [3], and about 380 s for the vacuum variant of Raptor 2 [4]. SpaceX chose methane and oxygen partly because both can be made on Mars [12].

As of September 2026 no ship has reached orbit, and none has been recovered and flown again, although SpaceX has caught and reflown boosters [6]. Starship had flown 13 integrated test flights by July 2026 [28]. V3 debuted on Flight 12 on May 22, 2026; Flights 12 and 13 flew suborbital paths as planned, and both boosters were lost [5], [6], [28]. Flight 14, the first attempt to reach orbit, is set for no earlier than September 28, 2026 [7], [29].

In February 2026 Elon Musk announced that SpaceX's priority is now a city on the Moon, with work toward a Mars city to start in about five to seven years [8], so the Mars version has no funded schedule. The plan still targets 2035. That window pairs a moderate departure energy with the gentlest arrival of the early windows (next section), and a refueled Starship that lands on the Moon is most of what a Mars cargo lander needs.

> Assumption: A Mars-configured V3-class cargo ship has a dry mass of 130 t, with 160 t used as the pessimistic case. SpaceX has not published V3 dry mass; the Mars version needs a larger heat shield, landing legs, and cargo-handling gear. Both figures are this book's planning values, not derived from any V3 source. The 130 t sits close to the ratio of SpaceX's own published Mars ship designs, about 0.077 t of dry mass per tonne of propellant: 150 t for 1,950 t in 2016 [64], and in 2017 "about 85 tons" for 1,100 t, on a design that said 75 t "but inevitably there will be mass growth" [65]. At V3's 1,600 t that ratio gives about 124 t (this book's calculation). The pessimistic case carries 160 t through the propellant and tanker counts below.

## Launch windows: only 2037 is hard at both ends

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) lists every window from 2031 to 2048 from NASA's *Interplanetary Mission Design Handbook* [1]. The 2037 and 2039 windows are the most expensive in the plan, with minimum C3 of 14.84 and 12.17 km²/s² against 7.78 in 2033 and 10.19 in 2035 [1]. In 2035 the best path is a fast Type I transfer (less than 180° around the Sun); in 2037 and 2039 it is a slower Type II transfer [1]. Departure declination, in the table below, is the outbound direction's angle north or south of Earth's equator, which sets the lowest orbit inclination for a single departure burn.

A lander must also survive arrival. The handbook gives the Mars arrival hyperbolic excess speed (v∞) for each minimum-energy trajectory [1]. That is the lander's speed relative to Mars before the planet's gravity pulls it in. Combining it in quadrature with Mars's escape speed at the entry interface (about 4.9 km/s at 125 km altitude), as the square root of the sum of their squares, gives the entry speed.

The cheap 2033 window has the fastest arrival of the set. The pathfinder, whose job is to prove EDL, may therefore prefer the Type I alternative, which arrives slower but needs its own orbit plane for its −53° declination, or accept a harder entry than the waves will see. The 2039 arrival is as gentle as 2035's.

> Assumption: The handbook's minimum-energy launch periods span 3–6 weeks around the optimum [1]. Ships in one wave depart on different days over about four weeks, two on either side of the optimum, and every ship carries propellant for the worst day of those four weeks, when C3 is up to about 1.5 km²/s² above the minimum (this book's trajectory calculation). Justification: only one ship per wave can leave on the optimum day, and at the minimum C3 the 2035 and 2043 ships sit within 2 t of needing another tanker.

Arrivals spread as well. In the Type II windows a ship that leaves after the optimum arrives later and faster: a 2037 ship leaving two weeks late lands in mid-November 2038, at the end of the conjunction moratorium, and a late 2033 ship would enter at about 6.7 km/s. The pathfinder and the ERV therefore leave in the two weeks up to the optimum, the 2045/46 food flight earlier still ([below](#return-windows-and-stay-length)), and each wave's time-critical cargo, such as its reactor, rides the earliest ships. Across the four weeks the waves' entry speeds stay within 5.5–6.3 km/s. This book dates each wave by its optimum arrival; cargo on the earliest ships lands up to about six weeks sooner, which only adds margin.

## Refueling in Earth orbit

A Starship reaches LEO with little propellant left and must be refilled by tanker Starships, the idea behind NASA's lunar Human Landing System with its tanker, depot, and lander variants [26].

### Ship-to-ship transfer has never flown

The only in-space propellant transfer so far was between two tanks inside one ship, on Flight 3 in March 2024 [10]. NASA's Office of Inspector General (OIG) reported on March 10, 2026, that the large-scale ship-to-ship demonstration, planned for March 2025, had slipped 12 months to March 2026. The report quoted the program's view of cryogenic transfer as "one of the most significant technical challenges facing the provider" [9]. That date has passed without a flight. [Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) assumes the transfer is demonstrated before 2033, because the optional pathfinder's own eight tanker flights begin about mid-March 2033, and that refueling is routine by the Wave 1 gate in March 2035 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)).

The OIG report describes SpaceX's lunar plan as a depot followed by more than 10 tankers, one about every 6 days, from Starbase in Texas and Kennedy Space Center in Florida [9]; in November 2023 NASA officials put the whole lunar campaign at launches in the "high teens" [11]. Neither is a Mars figure, and no published source gives a Mars-specific count, so this chapter derives its own.

### Each Mars ship is its own depot

Tankers fill each Mars-bound ship directly. A separate depot, as in the lunar plan [9], adds a transfer per ship and helps only if multimonth storage works. Staging from a high elliptical orbit would raise the total tanker count. The plan uses neither, which leaves two questions: the orbit where the ship waits, and how much propellant it loses while waiting.

> Assumption: Filling ships orbit at 28.5–33° inclination and 300–400 km altitude. Justification: tankers from Starbase (about 26°N) and Kennedy (28.5°N) share an orbit only at 28.5° or more. That orbit also keeps about 120 m/s more of Earth's rotation than the space station's 51.6°. A single departure burn needs at least the departure declination, 6–22° for cargo and 31.2° for the crew [1]. Earth's bulge swings the plane about 7° per day, so it lines up with the departure direction only twice in each turn of about 50 days, at uneven intervals that run from under a week to over six weeks with the departure's declination (this chapter's calculation), and each filling campaign ends on one of those dates.

A ship whose filling runs past its alignment date can still leave a day late by folding the plane change into its departure burn. At each window's optimum that costs about 6–33 t, boil-off included, within the slack under its ninth tanker plus its 5% reserve in every window from 2033 to 2043; two days late costs up to about 105 t and fits only in 2035, 2039, 2041, and 2043 (this chapter's calculation). Otherwise it waits for its next alignment. Filled in the plane whose next alignment comes first, a ship waits about 4–24 days and loses the 5 t a day of boil-off below, and the wait costs at most one more tanker in 2033–2039 and, using the 5% reserve, none in 2041 and 2043; in the other plane a 2035 ship would wait about a month and need up to two. A ship planned late in its launch period may find no second alignment before the period closes. The tanker counts below do not carry the extra tanker ([R08](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r08)).

> Assumption: The tanker counts stay at nine per ship. A ship that misses its alignment waits for the next one and takes one more tanker, flown on demand, and a second miss loses its window. The alternative is a tenth tanker in every ship's plan. Justification: in this chapter's model of a four-ship campaign, a planned tenth tanker adds three or four launches to each wave and in no case modeled lowers the chance of losing a window by more than about 1.5 ships in 100, while raising it in most, because windows are lost when a launch failure grounds the fleet, and a tenth tanker cannot fly then either. The campaign keeps room for the extra flights instead: pads for at least about 0.8 launches a day, such as four turning in five days or five in six, which absorbs scrubs, and about 1.2, such as five turning in four days, if the campaign is also to recover from a stand-down of about a week (below); a fleet of about 12 tankers; and leave to fly tankers up to a week ahead of their slots when a stand-down threatens, at up to about 35 t more boil-off per ship, which the slack covers in 2035, 2039, 2041, and 2043 and the 5% reserve in 2037.

> Hypothesis: Weather and technical scrubs alone will not cost a ship its window, but a launch failure will if it grounds the fleet for more than about a week in the last weeks before a ship's alignment. Basis: at the Eastern Range, 38% of countdowns from 1988 to early 2017 were scrubbed, 18% for weather and 19% for customer or range issues [58]. In this chapter's day-by-day model of a Wave 2 or 3 campaign, those rates cost no window when the pads offer about 0.8 launches a day, while three pads turning in five days or four in six lose ships even without failures. With one launch in 200 failing and a 16-day stand-down, as long as Falcon 9's in July 2024 [59], about 7–12% of ships and 17–20% of waves lose a window with pads for 0.8–1.25 launches a day. With pads for about 1.2 a day, a stand-down of about a week, or tankers flying up to a week early with ships leaving up to a day late, brings that to about 0.5–1% of ships and 1–3% of waves; with pads for 0.8 a day the same measures bring it only to about 2–8% of ships and 7–19% of waves, because the pads cannot clear the backlog in time (this chapter's calculation). The weather record carries over to Florida pads, which the model grounds together on a bad day, but it averages the whole year, while Waves 2 and 3 fill from late July to October, mostly in the warm season when Florida's afternoon thunderstorms most often break the lightning launch rules [61]. At an assumed 25–30% of days lost to weather instead of 18%, four pads turning in five days lose up to about 2% of ships to scrubs alone, and five turning in six almost none (this chapter's calculation). The technical rate comes from a mostly expendable fleet and could be lower for a reusable tanker. Test: the tanker records of the lunar landing campaigns in the early 2030s (scrub rate, pad turnaround, stand-down after any failure) and the pathfinder's eight-tanker fill, read at G1 in March 2035; stand-downs longer than a week would call for pads nearer 1.2 launches a day together with each wave's filling starting about a week earlier.

A fully fueled Starship in LEO with a sunshield absorbs roughly 33 kWt of heat, by one independent estimate [13]; if all of it boils propellant (213 kJ/kg for oxygen, 510 kJ/kg for methane), the ship loses about 10 t per day, about 0.6% of a full load. NASA depot studies with multilayer insulation found boil-off well under 0.05% per day [31], so insulation decides the rate. Removing the heat actively would take about 0.5 MW of electric power on one ship [13], thousands of times the ~100 W any flight cryocooler had reached by 2021 ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)).

> Assumption: A Mars ship being filled loses an average of 5 t/day of propellant over a 30-day filling campaign, 150 t in total, with passive insulation, a sunshield, and subcooled loading (propellant chilled below its boiling point, so it absorbs heat before it starts to boil). No active cooling is assumed. This is the largest uncertainty in the tanker count below.

Boil-off does not stop at departure. After the departure burn a cargo ship carries only its landing propellant, and that has to last a coast of 6 to 13 months.

> Assumption: On the coast to Mars the landing propellant boils off at 0.05% per day, the upper end of the NASA depot figure above [31], in both the base and the pessimistic case. Over the 196–418 days of the 2033–2043 transfers, launch periods included, that is 9–19% of the landing propellant, 7–16 t per ship in the base case, so each ship leaves Earth carrying the extra. The ERV's capture and return propellant crosses the same coast and pays the same loss; its multiyear hold at Mars is actively cooled ([below](#the-earth-return-vehicle), [R26](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r26)).

## The rocket equation: propellant never limits the payload

Propellant needs come from the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, Δv = g₀·Isp·ln(m₀/m₁), applied in two burns. The trans-Mars injection (TMI) burn leaves a 350 km circular orbit, adds 0.1 km/s for finite-burn losses and course corrections, and uses 375 s (a mix of vacuum and sea-level Raptors). The Mars landing burn is 0.9 km/s at 350 s, carried all the way from Earth, with the coast boil-off above. The atmosphere does the rest of the slowing.

```text
TMI delta-v   = sqrt(C3 + 2*mu_E/r) - sqrt(mu_E/r),  r = 6,728 km,  mu_E = 398,600 km^3/s^2
Landed mass   = dry + payload
At Mars entry = landed * exp(900 / (9.80665 * 350))            = landed * 1.300
After TMI     = landed + (at_entry - landed) / (1 - coast loss),   coast loss = 1 - 0.9995^days
At LEO depart = after_TMI * exp((TMI + 100) / (9.80665 * 375))
Propellant    = at_LEO_depart - landed    (must be <= 1,600 t)

Example, 2035, dry 130 t, payload 100 t, 196-day coast:
  TMI = 3.65 km/s  ->  ratio exp(3,750 / 3,677) = 2.77
  at entry = 230 * 1.300 = 299 t;  landing propellant 69 t, so 76.1 t before a 9.3% coast loss
  after TMI = 230 + 76.1 = 306.1 t;  at departure = 306.1 * 2.77 = 848 t
  propellant = 848 - 230 = 618 t
```

The pessimistic case raises the dry mass to 160 t, lowers TMI Isp to 365 s, and raises the landing burn to 1.2 km/s.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2033 | 3.54 | 600 | 802 | 483 / 359 |
| 2035 | 3.65 | 618 | 823 | 466 / 346 |
| 2037 | 3.85 | 689 | 924 | 404 / 290 |

The ship's 1,600 t is 1.7–2.7 times the propellant these cases need. With full tanks, even the pessimistic ship could carry 290–359 t through to landing, nearly three times the ~100 t planning figure. The limit is the lander: its structure, entry mass, and landing burn.

A ship therefore needs only 600–700 t in the base case and 800–940 t in the pessimistic case, even on the worst day of its launch period, and the spare capacity can buy a faster transit (useful for the crew ship), cover boil-off, or brake a fast arrival before entry.

The annual totals fit existing environmental approvals: 25 Starship launches per year from Starbase [14], 44 from Kennedy's Launch Complex 39A [15], and 76 from Cape Canaveral's Space Launch Complex 37 [16], 145 in all, of which a wave uses at most about a third. But 145 per year averages only 2.8 per week, about 60% of the 4.7 per week that Waves 2 and 3 need for eight weeks in the base case, and about half the 5.6–6.1 of the pessimistic case. Against the Eastern Range's scrub rates, Waves 2 and 3 need pads for at least about 0.8 launches a day across Texas and Florida, four turning in five days or five in six, and about 1.2 a day, five turning in four days, to recover from a stand-down of about a week; three pads, or four turning in six days or more, lose ships to scrubs alone ([above](#each-mars-ship-is-its-own-depot); this chapter's calculation). A tanker refurbishes and reflies every 14 days, about 4 flights per campaign, so those waves need a fleet of 9–12 tankers, about 12 to leave room for the extra flights. Ship reuse has not been demonstrated as of September 2026 [6]; without it, every tanker flight consumes a new ship and the campaign cost multiplies.

Every Mars-bound ship except the ERV stays there: 14–15 in all (11 cargo landers, one or two support landers, the crew ship, and the 2045/46 food flight), or 15–16 if the optional pathfinder flies.

## Entry, descent, and landing

EDL is the least demonstrated part of the plan. Nothing heavier than about 1 t has landed on Mars; the record is Perseverance at 1,025 kg [18]. Heavy landers must use supersonic retropropulsion (SRP): firing engines into the oncoming supersonic flow to slow down and steer. NASA treats SRP as the enabling capability for landing human-scale payloads on Mars [18].

### Supersonic retropropulsion

NASA studied SRP with SpaceX from 2014 to 2017, using Falcon 9 first-stage reentries, which by July 2017 had flown several burns in Mars-relevant conditions [17]. The team concluded that the remaining challenge is flight systems engineering for a specific Mars vehicle, not new technology [17], and NASA Langley continues subscale ground tests and model validation [18]. All of this evidence comes from Earth; Mars's thin carbon dioxide atmosphere, its winds, and the plume's interaction with a large vehicle body are modeled only.

> Assumption: The reference ship enters Mars's atmosphere directly, without aerocapture, then descends belly-first and lands on its sea-level engines, with a total propulsive Δv of about 0.9 km/s. That figure is this book's planning value, not a published one; the pessimistic case uses 1.2 km/s ([above](#the-rocket-equation-propellant-never-limits-the-payload)). Ballistic capture and skip-entry trajectories are left for later study.

### Landing within 100 m

Perseverance's terrain-relative navigation, which matches descent images to an onboard map, could move its landing point by up to 600 m to avoid hazards, and NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory estimated it raised the chance of a safe landing from about 85% to about 99% [21], a result proven only at the 1 t scale. Colony landers must also land close enough for rovers to tow 15 t loads to the work sites and far enough apart that one plume does not damage another ship or the base. To land inside that band, the plan adds radio beacons on each prepared pad.

> Assumption: 99.7% of landings touch down within 100 m of the pad beacon, using terrain-relative navigation plus the beacons. For a two-dimensional scatter that means about 29 m standard deviation per axis; a 3σ circle of 100 m would hold only 98.9%. The remaining 0.3% per landing leaves about a 3% chance that one of the ten or eleven landings aimed at prepared sites (Wave 2's and Wave 3's eight cargo ships, the support landers, and the crew ship) ends outside one; Wave 1 lands on unprepared ground ([R14](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r14)). Pads sit about 1 km apart, the estimated reach of landing ejecta, with berms between them. Wave 1's three ships land on bare ground, where debris may carry about 2 km, so they touch down at least 2 km apart, this book's working assumption ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). The landing field lies at least 2 km from the habitat, the reactor field, and the wells, with berms on the line of sight ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md), [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). Chapter 8 prepares each site to this accuracy, so its site size rests on the 100 m figure.

That figure is unqualified. Its only evidence is terrain-relative navigation on a 1 t lander without beacons, and it is sensitive: at 40 m of scatter per axis about 4% of landings would fall outside 100 m, and at 50 m about 14% (this chapter's calculation). Gate G2 therefore reads the touchdown errors of Wave 1's ships against their aim points before Wave 2 is committed, and every later landing adds to the record ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)).

### Why pads come first

NASA's plume-surface interaction research lists the hazards of a landing engine: dust clouds that blind sensors, high-energy ejecta, and craters under the lander. Even NASA's small InSight lander, which touched down in 2018, dug pits on Mars [19]. Models and vacuum-chamber tests to predict these effects are in progress [19], [20]. NASA's analyses put the highest ground pressure under a plume on Mars, where the thin air keeps it narrow, rather than on Earth or the Moon [19], yet no published model gives the ground pressure, crater, or burn time of a Starship-class landing there. Scaled from the largest published cases, the one Raptor that carries the ship near touchdown presses tens of kilopascals on a patch 5–8 m wide, up to about 0.7 MPa under the nozzle, and digs deeper than the 0.3–0.8 m of loose soil over the site's ice ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)); the first flight data on a Raptor plume meeting the ground will come from SpaceX's uncrewed lunar demonstration, which is to carry plume instruments and relight its Raptors on the surface [9]. A Starship-class lander's three sea-level Raptors produce 7–8 MN of thrust, roughly 2,000 times the 3.6 kN of InSight's 12 descent thrusters combined [30]. A NASA-led study of Starship on Mars warns that without landing pads, the exhaust could dig holes under a lander, melt subsurface ice, and tip it over [12]. Ice raises a second hazard: on Starship's first launch the exhaust vaporized groundwater under the pad and threw debris at about 90 m/s, and a Mars landing on ground holding more than about 2% water by weight could do the same [38], enough to carry stones about 2 km in Mars gravity ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). With 5% water the same estimate reaches 125–140 m/s [38], enough for 3.6–5.1 km (this book's calculation), but it assumes all the water flashes to vapor at once; chapter 8 sets out this book's hypothesis that on bare ice the crater stops at the ice and plans for about 90 m/s as the upper case.

Prepared pads remove those hazards after the first wave. Wave 1 lands on unprepared ground and carries the pad kit and robots to build the first pads before Wave 2 arrives in October 2038. Chapter 8 covers how pads are built from local regolith.

### The 2033 pathfinder

The optional pathfinder exists to retire the EDL risk before Wave 1 commits three ships and a reactor. One heavy lander in the 2033 window would demonstrate SRP at Mars scale, measure the plume's effect on the actual site, and land survey robots that map the future pad field.

> Assumption: Wave 1 proceeds in 2035 whether or not the pathfinder flies, as long as some 100 t-class lander has landed on Mars before gate G1 in March 2035: the pathfinder, or another program's heavy lander, such as a SpaceX Mars flight in the 2031 or 2033 window. If none has, the 2035 flight becomes the EDL test and Wave 1 moves to 2037 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). Ships on direct entry cannot hold in orbit to wait for the first landing. Without the pathfinder, launch spacing puts arrivals days apart: the reactor rides the second ship, critical spares are split across ships, and the first touchdown's data update the aim points of the ships still in flight.

## Surface logistics: the first days after landing

> Assumption: Each cargo lander carries its own offload system (a crane or cargo elevator rated at 20 t) and a ramp or platform for rovers. The 20 t is a planning choice that covers a reactor, about 15 t and up to about 19.5 t with its growth allowance ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)). [Chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md) specifies the rovers and the transport trailer.

The first days after a Wave 1 landing follow a fixed sequence, driven by power and by what can fail first.

1. Safe the vehicle: vent or close off propellant, set the thermal state, and confirm the lander is upright and stable.
2. Establish the communications link through the Mars Relay Network and the lander's own antenna ([chapter 10](10.%20Communications.md)).
3. Deploy the first solar panels and batteries so offload does not drain the lander.
4. Lower the rovers and the trailer, then survey and grade the tow route to the reactor field.
5. Lift the reactor, the most valuable single item, onto its trailer and tow it at least 2 km to the reactor field ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md) describes the delivery).
6. Offload the rest in priority order: water and oxygen equipment ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)), construction robots, and the pad kit.

Empty landers stay useful as tank farms, equipment shelters, and scrap metal ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). Nearly nine years after the first of them lands, the same field receives the crew ship, the first arrival that must also leave.

### The crew vehicle and crew lander

> Assumption: The reference crew vehicle is a Starship-class ship, refilled in LEO, that departs in the November 2043 window, enters directly, and lands on a prepared pad at the colony. After landing it stays as a surface asset (backup habitat, storm shelter) and is not refueled.

No ship lands on the crew pad before the crew does, because every ship stays where it lands. [Chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md) builds the pad new in 2041–2042, finished by about July 2042, sintered out to 50 m with the same recipe as the landing-site cores. By the crew gate in August 2043, nine or ten landings will have used prepared sites: Wave 2's and Wave 3's eight cargo ships and the one or two support landers. The three Wave 1 ships land on bare ground, so their craters calibrate the plume models but test nothing about the recipe. With about 29 m of scatter per axis, only about one landing in five touches down on a 20 m core, so the eight cargo landings put a ship and its plume directly on the recipe only about 1.7 times on average; the other landings test it under a plume from a few tens of meters away. That record is thin, so the required support lander sets down in September 2042 on a site built exactly like the crew pad, sintered out to 50 m ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)): a full-scale test of the recipe a year before the crew gate, with about three chances in four of touching down on sintered ground. Counting it, the recipe carries a ship directly about 2.5 times before the crew gate (about 2.7 if a second support lander uses a hybrid site), with about a 3% chance of never (this chapter's calculation). Its site survey, the cores' record, the recipe's qualification tests, and inspection of the finished pad through the February 2043 conjunction and about nine months of the November 2042–September 2043 dust season, the part before the August decision, are the evidence that crew gate condition 4 reads ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#crew-gate)). The crew ship's unused tank capacity can shorten the trip and cut the crew's cruise dose of about 1.84 mSv/day ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md)), up to the speed its lander can enter at. A 240-day Type I transfer enters at about 6.5–6.7 km/s, the top of the landers' envelope; a faster one must brake before entry, which takes about 1,100 t of propellant and 14 tankers for a 200-day trip and more than the 1,600 t tank holds for 180 days (this chapter's calculation).

The fallback is NASA's DRA 5.0 model: a transit habitat waits in Mars orbit while the crew uses a separate lander, merging crew vehicle and ERV [25].

That saves about 7 t of Wave 3 mass and tests the full plant, but it puts methane production on the path to the crew gate, with about 15 months of margin at the design dry mass.

That margin holds only while the MAV stays near its design mass. Every added tonne of dry mass adds 5–9 t of liftoff mass by the NASA study above [27], and at the 2015 design's propellant share of liftoff mass (29.7 of 39.1 t, about three quarters) that is about 3.8–6.8 t more propellant per load, 0.9–1.6 t of it methane, or 70–130 more days of plant time at 85% availability (this book's calculation). At the high end the 12 t case does not close. The plant finishes load 2 by the decision only while the dry mass stays below about 11 t at the high end of the range (about 12.5 t at the low end); a heavier vehicle needs part of its methane from Earth, sized for the larger load, or a later crew window.

> Assumption: If the methane plant fails, or runs too slowly to finish load 2 by August 2042, the 2041 support lander carries methane for one MAV load from Earth: about 8.2 t at launch, so that 7.0 t survives a coast of 316–320 days at 0.05% a day, in an insulated, cooled tank of about 20 m³, together roughly 10 t (this book's estimate) in place of 10 t of that flight's spares. ### The Earth return vehicle

> Assumption: The ERV is a Starship-class ship with a crew cabin and 20 t of return provisions, launched in the October 2041 window, captured into a 1-sol orbit, and holding the propellant for trans-Earth injection. The 20 t is a planning allocation for the cabin and the return food ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)), not a sized manifest, carried on the 130 t dry ship above. The crew's Earth-entry capsule, about 9–12 t at the Orion size used below, rides on top of those 150 t, for a return mass of about 160 t. Justification: the capsule is a known item with a known mass, and the saving that might pay for it is not. NASA's Orion crew module weighs 10.4 t at launch and 9.3 t after landing [66], and DRA 5.0 booked its Mars return capsule as an item of its own, 10 t, with 4 t more for a service module to aim the entry [25]; this ERV aims the entry itself, so its capsule needs none. The 20 t has no room for a capsule: scaled to four people and a 235-day return, NASA's transit-habitat figures give some 16–26 t for such a cabin with its food (this book's scaling of [25] and [67]). The capsule might still fit within 150 t in all, because a ship that never lands carries no heat shield, flaps, landing legs, or cargo gear. In a NASA human Mars lander design [68] the landing legs come to about 4% of the landed mass and the entry heat shield to about 8% of the mass at entry, which on a ship landing 230 t would be some 30 t, three times the capsule (this book's reading and estimate). That design is a 65 t lander on an inflatable heat shield, though, not a 50 m hull covered in tiles, so the figure gives an order of magnitude at most; this book found no published figure for those masses on a Mars Starship, and the ERV adds parts of its own, such as the capsule's berth and the docking port for the MAV. The plan therefore counts the capsule and leaves that possible saving to cover the ERV's own additions. If the ERV's mass statement shows its hull, additions included, lighter than the lander's 130 t by at least the capsule's mass, the capsule fits within the 150 t and each ERV needs one tanker fewer (this book's calculation).

The table's Δv values assume a Mars departure v∞ of 3.0–3.5 km/s, above the ~2.7 km/s of the planned early November 2045 departure, to cover off-optimal return dates, and a propulsive capture for the 2041 arrival (v∞ 2.49 km/s [1], up to 2.53 km/s for a departure in the two weeks before the optimum). Like every other ship, the ERV loses 0.05% a day of its Mars-side propellant on the 10-month coast, about 24 t. With that loss, the worst day of its launch period, the capsule, and the cooling equipment below, the rocket equation gives about 740 t in LEO, 10 tankers, and about 75 t held at Mars. In the pessimistic case the ship is 160 t dry, which puts its return mass at about 190 t once the 20 t of cabin and provisions, the capsule, and the cooling equipment are added, and every burn runs at 365 s; it then needs about 930 t and 12 tankers.

> Assumption: The ERV holds its return propellant at zero boil-off with cryocoolers on its own solar arrays. By this book's estimate, about 75 t of propellant in insulated tanks sized for it, about 90 m³, absorbs about 0.1–0.2 kW of heat in Mars orbit: the coast rate of 0.05% a day on 70 t corresponds to about 0.11 kW, and the upper figure doubles it for Mars's infrared glow and the orbit's eclipses. The capsule's mass adds the last 5 t, but they enlarge the tanks' area, and so the heat, by only about 4–5% (this book's calculation). Held in the ship's nearly empty main tanks, it would absorb more. At [chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)'s 7–25 W of input per watt of cooling, that takes about 1–6 kWe, which about 50 m² of arrays supply even when Mars is farthest from the Sun. Two coolers of about 0.1 t each (one a spare; chapter 6), the arrays, radiators, and tank-wall tubing come to about 1 t, added to the return mass in the figures above. The spare covers a failure only if either cooler can carry the whole load. At the lower estimate one can; the upper, about 0.2 kW at 90 K, is more than the 90 K unit's 120 W or so on the ground (chapter 6) and more than the 170 W projected for its flight version [47], so that case needs a larger unit or a third cooler, about 0.1 t more within the 1 t. No cryocooler of this size has flown, and the 90 K unit has run 31 days [48]. Small space coolers have run for 20 years, and the two flown turbo-Brayton designs, Hubble's NICMOS cooler and three freezer units on the space station, failed once in about 350,000 unit-hours, about 2.5% a year per unit [49]. At that rate two coolers either of which carries the load keep the propellant cold through the hold about 99 times in 100, and two that must both run about 82 times; with only one failure on record the rate could be four times higher, and then the figures are 92 and 52 (this book's analysis of [49]). Counting also a fault that stops every unit of one design at once, at the one-in-ten share of failures that NASA's risk-assessment guide gives such common causes when a design has no record of its own [55], the first figure falls to about 98, and 90 at the higher rate (this book's calculation). The cooling system therefore still needs qualifying for the hold: a multiyear life test of the flight design, which would have to start by about 2038 to have three years behind it when G4 decides in July 2041, and redundant coolers shown to take over from each other, a gap NASA says must close before crewed use [50].

> Assumption: For a cooler failure at the upper heat estimate, this book keeps the two coolers above. The evidence favors four of the 90 K units now in test, any two of which carry about 0.22 kW, with crew gate condition 4 read as enough coolers working to lose one more and still carry the heat, three of the four at the upper estimate, the least failure tolerance NASA's human-rating standard allows against catastrophic events [56]. Justification: at the flown rate, counting common causes, two coolers under condition 4's every-cooler rule hold the crew a window about 9 times in 100, from launch shocks and a year of running before the crew leaves, and at the upper estimate a failure after launch leaves the crew on Mars about 4 or 5 times in 100; four coolers under the one-failure rule cut these to about 1.5 and 0.3 in 100, and at every rate the record allows they hold the crew least often of the kits compared here (this book's calculation). They add about 0.2 t and 1 kWe, which the ~1 t kit and its arrays already allow for (this book's calculation), and theirs is the only design of this size being built and tested. A third cooler does about as well at the lower heat estimate, but at the upper one all three must work, which holds the crew about 13–14 times in 100. Two larger units of about 250 W, which a NASA Glenn study sized at about 65 kg and 2 kWe each [57], would leave the crew on Mars about as rarely as four small ones but hold it as often as two, and nobody has built or tested one. Switch: if a ground test of a flight-like tank and its insulation, with a thermal model of the orbit, holds the heat near 0.11 kW, three coolers are enough; if the life test reads a failure rate near the flight record's 90% bound when gate G4 reads it in July 2041, each ERV flies the four, which also all but removes the stay to about 2050 ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#the-waiver-needs-allowances-above-the-cases)); if the life test finds a fault that every unit shares, more units of one design do not help, and the design must change first.

If the cooling fails, whether both coolers stop or, at the upper heat estimate, either one, the heat that leaks in boils the propellant off at a steady rate, about 35–60 kg a day for the 0.1–0.2 kW above; a tank that has lost part of its load still takes in almost as much heat as a full one. The planned November 2045 departure needs about 46 t plus 5–8 t for the plane alignment described below, so the ERV can lose at most about 21–24 t: its cooling has to last until 0.9–1.8 years before departure, about February to December 2044 depending on the heat leak. The later departure, which needs 59–71 t with its alignment, can lose only 4–15 t, so its cooling has to last until 0.2–1.1 years before departure (this book's calculation). At the upper heat estimate a failure any time before the crew lands therefore leaves too little for either return; at the lower one, a failure before about February to March 2044, early in the outbound cruise, does. Crew gate condition 4 requires every cooler working at the launch decision, and a failure between the decision and launch holds the crew. A failure during the outbound cruise or early in the stay turns the mission into the missed-window case: the crew stays on the surface, the committed 2045/46 food flight lands food and spares ([below](#return-windows-and-stay-length)), and a replacement ERV, decided as soon as the failure is seen, flies in the same window to wait for the December 2047 departure. It leaves early in that window, in December 2045 like the food flight, for reasons set out after the heat-shield options below.

The ERV's orbit must also line up with an ascent 3.2 years after its capture. The 2015 MAV's 5.27 km/s is for a co-planar ascent from 30°N into a 30° orbit, with the return vehicle "positioned appropriately" [22]. From 42°N the ERV's orbit has to be inclined at least 42°, which costs the MAV about 30 m/s of the help it gets from Mars's rotation, and its plane has to lie over the site on the ascent date; the trans-Earth burn also needs the orbit's periapsis about 128° behind the November 2045 departure direction. The MAV cannot pay for more. The 2015 design carries about 0.3 t of reserve propellant, worth about 80 m/s, and the latitude already takes 30–40 m/s of it (this chapter's calculation from [22]), so the ERV has to bring its plane to the MAV.

Mars's equatorial bulge, which its gravity coefficient J2 = 1.96 × 10⁻³ measures [37], turns a 1-sol orbit a long way in 3.2 years. At inclinations of 30–60° its plane swings about 160–270° around Mars's axis over the hold, and its periapsis turns about 40–430° within the plane. The Sun's pull and the rest of Mars's gravity field move the periapsis altitude between about 190 and 290 km and the period between about 24.4 and 25.1 hours, which trims of a few m/s correct (this chapter's calculation). Plane changes are cheap near apoapsis, where the ERV moves at only about 0.45 km/s: about 80 m/s and 5 t of propellant for every 10°. Even so, holding a plane the MAV can reach for the whole hold would cost 800–1,300 m/s, more than the ERV carries. The drift is predictable, so the ERV instead aims its 2042 approach, which costs almost nothing, so that the drift itself carries the periapsis to where the departure needs it. In this chapter's calculation the best such orbit holds at about 31°. A few days before the ascent the ERV turns its plane about 13° around the line of apsides at apoapsis, for about 100 m/s, into a plane of about 44° that the MAV reaches directly, and the trans-Earth burn leaves from that plane. For departures between late October and mid-November 2045 the alignment costs about 100–130 m/s, about 5–8 t of the 29 t held beyond the planned departure's need; aimed instead for the later departure of November 16–22 (below), it costs about 160–170 m/s, about 10 t of the roughly 20 t that departure leaves. This calculation tilted the MAV's plane to about 50° for that departure, which takes about 55 m/s of its 80 m/s reserve; the second calculation below finds an orbit at the same cost that keeps it at about 42°. DRA 5.0's addendum books a similar 100 m/s for orbit maintenance in the same 1-sol orbit [33].

These figures come from averaged J2 rates checked by a numerical propagation, and a second calculation with different tools confirms them. It uses the Orekit flight-dynamics library [51], NASA Goddard's GMM-3 gravity field to degree 20 [52], the Sun and Jupiter, sunlight pressure, and an optimizer free to place every burn, and finds about 95–115 m/s for the planned departure, 95–125 m/s from a week before it to ten days after, and about 160–165 m/s for the later one, with the MAV's plane at about 42° and the periapsis between about 210 and 300 km (this book's calculation). NASA's studies of the same orbit work the same way: DRA 5.0's second addendum recommends choosing the orbit's orientation after capture so that the drift carries it to its departure alignment, and books 157 m/s for a 20° turn at apoapsis [53]; NASA Langley's optimizations of stays of about a year found about 150 m/s for prograde orbits, and about 0.24–0.37 km/s when the landing site lies at 42° and the arrival periapsis must pass over it [54].

The aim of the 2042 capture matters more than any force the first calculation left out. An approach plane turned 1° from its aim, a miss of about 140 km in the aim point, adds 20–50 m/s by the departure if left alone, or about 10 m/s if a plane turn in the first days after capture corrects it; the drift magnifies small changes, and 1° of inclination moves the periapsis about 12° over the hold. Leaving Earth up to two weeks early in the 2041 launch period adds about 5–10 m/s. A missed return window costs more: held to a departure around the turn of 2048, the orbit aimed for 2045 needs about 210–300 m/s at that window's better dates, about 55–59 t of the 75 t in all, provided the cooling has kept the whole load for more than five years (this book's calculation).

DRA 5.0 put Earth entry speed on a nominal Mars return at up to 12 km/s, against 11 km/s for a lunar return, and recommended 12 km/s as a limit; earlier studies allowed up to 14 km/s [25]. Its addendum limited its own return trajectories to 13–13.5 km/s, called 13–14 km/s the reasonable upper limit, and named a braking burn by the capsule's service module as one way to reach 12 km/s, without sizing it [33]. The planned return does not stay under 12 km/s. By this book's calculation on JPL's DE441 ephemeris [32], 220–235-day returns leaving Mars between October 25 and November 12, 2045, enter Earth's atmosphere at 12.1–13.7 km/s, the range the book's simpler two-body model gave. These are inertial speeds; an entry heading east meets the atmosphere up to about 0.5 km/s slower, 0.4 km/s on Apollo's uncrewed test at lunar-return speed [34]. Braking to 12 km/s before entry would take up to about 3.4 km/s, over 240 t of propellant for the ~160 t return mass, and the ~75 t kept at Mars includes none of it: the 29 t beyond the planned departure's need buys only about 0.6 km/s. Leaving Mars two to three weeks later, about November 16–22, on a 230–240-day return keeps entry at 11.9–12.1 km/s with a departure v∞ of 2.9–3.2 km/s, inside the ERV's sizing. The stay grows to about 426–432 days, the crew reaches Earth in July 2046, and the departure takes 50–60 t of the 75 t, leaving 15–25 t for the alignment above and for margin (this book's calculation).

No crew has entered at either speed. Apollo's command module was designed for a lunar return at about 11.1 km/s [34], Orion comes back from the Moon at about 11 km/s [35], and the fastest Earth entry of any returning spacecraft when it flew, Stardust's 46 kg sample capsule in 2006, was 12.9 km/s on a heat shield of phenolic impregnated carbon ablator (PICA) [36]. DRA 5.0 found that no off-the-shelf ablator existed for a Mars-return Orion, even at 12 km/s [25]. Either option therefore needs a crew heat shield qualified above anything flown: to about 12 km/s for the later departure, to about 14 km/s for the planned one. The plan has not chosen between them, but it does not fly the ERV without the choice: gate G4 requires the return trajectory chosen and the capsule qualified for its entry speed before the ERV leaves, and crew gate condition 4 reads the result ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md), [R41](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r41)).

The two options differ in how far past tested hardware the shield must go. For an Orion-size capsule of 9–12 t entering at 5–8 g, this book's calculation, using Sutton and Graves's convective relation [39] and NASA's radiative-heating results for a 5 m nose radius [40], puts peak heating with NASA's usual margins at about 1,200–2,300 W/cm² at 12 km/s and 2,700–5,400 W/cm² at 14 km/s; DRA 5.0's own assessment gave about 1,450–2,100 and 2,550–4,550 W/cm² [25]. Some 80–90% of that heat is radiation from the gas ahead of the shield, at under 0.7 atm. NASA's 2009 assessment put the limit of Avcoat, Orion's material, at about 1,000 W/cm² [41], and PICA has been tested to 1,800 W/cm² as a single piece, which can be made at most 1.3 m across; tiled PICA is unproven at such heating [42]. NASA's woven carbon phenolics go further: the Heatshield for Extreme Entry Environment Technology (HEEET) reached technology readiness level 6 up to 3,600 W/cm² in 2019 [43], and two flight-ready 1.25 m shields of its single-piece derivative, 3MDCP, which performs predictably to about 3,700 W/cm², were finished for the Mars Sample Return capsule before that program ended [44]. A 5 m shield of either must be tiled, and the seams between tiles are the hardest part of the design [43]. No ground facility reproduces the entry. NASA's largest arc jet reaches 4.5 kW/cm² only on 5 cm models, with gas at no more than 28 MJ/kg against 62–88 MJ/kg in flight, and its lasers add up to 0.4 kW/cm² of radiation across a 15 cm sample, against 0.7–3.3 kW/cm² in flight [45] (this book's calculation). Ground tests can also miss what flight shows: Orion's Avcoat, tested in arc jets for its skip entry before flight, cracked and lost charred material in several places on Artemis I [46]. Apollo closed the same gap in flight, with a subscale probe at lunar-return speed and an uncrewed Apollo 4 that fired its own engine to reach that speed [34]. Qualification under G4 criterion 9 therefore means arc-jet and combined-heating tests of tiles and seams, and an uncrewed entry at the chosen speed; from an orbit reaching the Moon's distance, a stage adding about 1.0–1.3 km/s near Earth gives 12 km/s, and 3.0–3.7 km/s gives 14 km/s (this book's calculation). A new heat-shield material takes about ten years to develop and qualify [41], so either option needs the work under way by the early 2030s. The 12 km/s option stays within the heat fluxes woven shields have been tested to and needs about a third of the test flight's boost.

The choice is less stark than two options suggest, because on these returns the entry speed follows mostly from the day the capsule reaches Earth. Every return from the planned window that arrives on or after about June 23, 2046, enters at no more than 12.5 km/s. A crew leaving between October 31 and November 12, 2045, on a return of 223–235 days, inside the planned 220–235, arrives that day after a mission of about 950 days; the departure takes 42–49 t of the ERV's 75 t and leaves 19–27 t after the orbit alignment, and chapter 12's case B dose is 1,102–1,121 mSv, all within the planned figures (this book's calculation on DE441 [32]). At 12.5 km/s the peak heating with NASA's margins is about 1,600–3,000 W/cm², 82–87% of it radiation, under the 3,600–3,700 W/cm² that woven carbon phenolic has been tested to [43], [44], and the test entry needs a stage of about 1.6 km/s. A capsule rated for 12.5 km/s can also leave on almost any day from late October to late November on a return long enough, nearly the same span of dates a 14 km/s capsule allows (this book's calculation).

> Assumption: The crew capsule is qualified for about 12.5 km/s, and the crew leaves in the planned window on a return that reaches Earth about June 23, 2046, entering no faster. The alternatives are a capsule for about 14 km/s and the later departure of November 16–22. Justification: it keeps the planned dates, the ERV's margin, and the dose of the planned return, and it holds the heating inside the fluxes woven shields have been tested to; a 14 km/s capsule's heating reaches beyond any tested shield and its test entry needs a 3.0–3.7 km/s stage, and the later departure cuts the ERV's spare to about 4–14 t (this book's calculation). The later departure stays the fallback if the shield qualifies only near 12 km/s, and the shortest returns of the window open only if a 14 km/s shield qualifies.

> Hypothesis: A tiled woven carbon phenolic shield for a 5 m capsule can be qualified for about 12.5 km/s by gate G4 in July 2041. Basis: HEEET, a tiled system, reached readiness level 6 up to 3,600 W/cm² on a 1 m test unit, with arc-jet tests showing that its joints must stay under about 0.25 mm [43]; two 1.25 m single-piece 3MDCP shields were built for about 3,700 W/cm² [44]; and NASA's shock-tube measurements at 9.5–15.5 km/s narrowed the parametric uncertainty in the air's own radiation to about +9% and −6% [60]. Counting ablation products and the models' structure, though, the same study still puts the total uncertainty of radiative heating at 12–13 km/s at about +51–53% [60], about the same as NASA's 1.5 margin on radiative heating, which that uncertainty therefore uses up. What does not carry over is scale and combined heating: at 12.5 km/s a seam takes about 1,300–2,600 W/cm² of radiation, three to six times what NASA's laser-augmented arc jet delivers over 15 cm [45]. Test: arc-jet and laser-augmented tests of tiles and seams up to about 3,700 W/cm², then an uncrewed entry at 12.5 km/s from lunar distance with a stage of about 1.6 km/s, as Apollo 4 flew lunar-return speed before any crew [34], all reported at G4; seams that fail below about 3,000 W/cm² move the plan to the later departure.

A replacement ERV, flown after a cooling failure like those above, meets a harder arrival than the first. A ship leaving on the window's minimum-energy dates and paths, January 8–22, 2046, reaches Mars at 4.4–5.1 km/s ([below](#return-windows-and-stay-length)), and an ERV must shed that speed with its engines: a capture of about 1.9–2.4 km/s into the 1-sol orbit, against 0.82 km/s in 2042. On those paths a replacement would need about 1,220 t in LEO and 15 tankers, and in the pessimistic case about 1,550 t and 18, 96–98% of the 1,600 t tank; even the cheapest paths those dates allow, arriving at about 3.9–4.3 km/s, need about 1,020 t and 13 tankers (15–16 pessimistic). A braking burn far out, which a lander would need on those paths, would only add to this: slowing by 0.8 km/s far from Mars and then capturing takes 2.65 km/s where the capture alone takes 2.41, because a burn does the most where the ship moves fastest, at periapsis (this book's calculation on DE441 [32]). The same ship leaving about December 4–18, 2045 needs only about 0.1 km/s more to leave Earth, reaches Mars about late September 2046 at about 3.3 km/s, and captures for 1.2–1.3 km/s: about 830 t and 11 tankers, or about 1,040 t and 13 in the pessimistic case (this book's calculation). NASA's DRA 5.0 Addendum gives its own propulsively captured cargo in this window the same path, leaving December 11, 2045, arriving at 3.28 km/s, and capturing into the 1-sol orbit for 1.24 km/s [33].

The capsule needs no new rating. As in 2045, the entry speed of the December 2047 returns follows the day the capsule reaches Earth, and a capsule qualified for about 12.5 km/s covers the window: every departure from late November 2047 to late January 2048 has a return of 186–243 days that enters at 12.5 km/s or less, with a departure v∞ of 2.4–2.8 km/s. Leaving about December 20, 2047, a 220-day return reaches Earth about July 27, 2048 at about 12.5 km/s, and a 235-day return enters at 12.0 km/s (this book's calculation on DE441 [32], checked with a second Lambert solver). The short hold is the harder part. Captured only about 15 months before its departure, the replacement cannot let Mars's bulge turn its orbit into line as the first ERV does over 3.2 years: by the same method the cheapest alignment for the two departures tried costs about 0.8–0.9 km/s, more than the ship carries (this book's calculation).

> Hypothesis: A replacement ERV captured in late September 2046 can line its orbit up for a December 2047 departure for about 0.15–0.4 km/s. Basis: NASA Langley optimized almost this case, a 1-sol orbit with a 250 km periapsis reached on October 6, 2046 at 3.33 km/s and left on January 29, 2048. Raising the orbit on arrival and before departure and turning it near a distant apoapsis, in up to seven burns, cost about 0.15–0.3 km/s for prograde orbits and about 0.37 km/s with the constraints of a landing site at 42° [54]. Those constraints are stricter than the ERV's, because the MAV can meet any plane of 42–50°, so for these dates the analog gives a conservative figure; its departure, a month later than the replacement's, is the part that carries over least. Paid from the return propellant, 0.37 km/s on top of the ideal 0.8 km/s burn uses about 60 t of the 75 t held; the part spent at capture could add a tanker (this book's calculation). Test: the program's flight dynamics team optimizes the replacement's own arrival and departure, with the MAV's planes, before the first ERV leaves in October 2041, so that the contingency is sized when the flight is committed; a cost above about 0.6 km/s would need more propellant held or a later return.

> Assumption: A replacement ERV is a second ship of the first one's design, with a capsule of the same rating and a propulsive capture. After a cooling failure once the crew has launched, it leaves about December 4–18, 2045 and is captured about late September 2046: about 830 t in LEO and 11 tankers, 12 if part of its alignment is spent at capture (about 1,040 t and 13–15 in the pessimistic case). A failure seen after about October 2044, possible only at the upper heat estimate and about 1 time in 100 at the flown rate, leaves less than the 14 months that chapter 13's replacement support lander gets, too little to start a ship; the next crew's ERV, already being built for December 2045 (below), answers it, so the plan builds that ship to its date whatever the first ERV does. The return slips to about April 2050 only if the replacement's own cooling also falls short in its 15-month hold, about 2–3 missions in 1,000 at the flown rate and up to about 2 in 100 at that rate's 90% bound ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#schedule-logic-what-slips-and-by-how-much) sizes that stay; this book's calculation). After a failure before the crew launches, it flies in the November 2043 window, for the first ERV's 10 tankers (12 pessimistic), only if it can be readied in time, and otherwise also leaves in December 2045 (chapter 13). The alternatives are a second ERV planned in the November 2043 window, and aerocapture. Justification: the December departure costs the fewest tankers in the window by this book's calculation, and NASA's Addendum picks the same date [33]; the capsule and alignment figures above fit the first ERV's design. A second ERV planned in November 2043 would turn every hold before launch into a one-window slip and give a failure after launch a return vehicle already in orbit, but it adds a ship, a capsule, and 10 tankers to every mission, and it meets the same cooler rule, holding the crew again about 10 times in 100 with two coolers (this book's calculation). Under this book's working assumption the next crew flies in the 2048 window and its ERV is this same December 2045 ship ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#crew-and-after-20432048)); a replacement would take it, and the next crew would wait a window. A crew in the 2045/46 window would instead need an ERV in Mars orbit at its own decision in September 2045, one flown in November 2043. Aerocapture, which DRA 5.0 kept as an option for its 115 t cargo into the same 1-sol orbit, needing 19 m/s on average after the pass in its simulations [25], [33], would cut the replacement to about 8 tankers (9 pessimistic). No spacecraft has yet aerocaptured at any planet, though [62], and the one published study this book found of a Starship-class ship doing so at Mars is a first-order model with an assumed lift, which its author says only a full aerothermal simulation can confirm [63]. Switch: if the next crew flies in the 2045/46 window instead, its ERV flies in November 2043 and becomes the replacement. If the kit stays at two coolers read as every cooler working and the life test reads a failure rate near the flight record's 90% bound, about 23–37 missions in 100 would need a replacement, and a second ERV on every mission starts to pay; the four coolers the evidence favors (above) cut the need to about 2 in 100, from about 9–14 at the flown rate (this book's calculation).

### Return windows and stay length

The plan is a conjunction-class mission, a long stay that waits for the planets to line up again; DRA 5.0 used about 180 days each way and about 500 days on Mars [25].

> Assumption: With arrival on about September 16, 2044, the low-energy Mars departure falls in early November 2045, a surface stay of about 410 days. The return takes 220–235 days, reaching Earth about June 2046, 940–950 days after launch. By this book's trajectory calculation, staying 450 days would raise the departure v∞ from about 2.7 to about 3.5 km/s for a 220-day return. JPL's DE441 ephemeris [32] confirms these dates and speeds (this book's calculation).

Surface consumables are sized for about 1,000 days (chapter 2), more than twice the planned stay; food for the transits travels outside that reserve, outbound in the crew ship and for the return in the ERV. The ERV's food launches in October 2041 and is eaten 4.1–4.7 years after packing, so gate G4 checks a storage qualification for it, seven years long so that it also covers a return after a missed window ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)). A missed return window means waiting about 26 months, a surface stay of about 1,190 days, and the ERV would then hold its propellant for more than five years. The plants keep making oxygen and water, but the food reserve runs out after 1,000 days, in mid-June 2047, about 190 days short. The wait also outlasts the pharmacy's dates, which run through the planned return in mid-2046 ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md)), the suit seals and batteries, and the robots' spares, and it raises each crew member's dose to about 1,330–1,360 mSv in chapter 12's baseline case, which the waivers signed before launch must cover ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#the-waiver-needs-allowances-above-the-cases)).

> Assumption: A committed food flight in the 2045/46 window ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)) lands at least 2.6 t of shelf-stable food at the colony about October 2046 (below), whether or not the first crew leaves on time; if they do, it adds to the next crew's reserve. At chapter 9's 2.39 kg per person per day, that covers the 190-day gap with about 80 days of margin, and it lands about eight months before the reserve would run out. Greenhouse output, about a fifth of the calories ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)), adds margin and is not counted.

> Assumption: The same flight carries a pharmacy resupply and suit consumables (seals, batteries) for four crew, about 1 t together (this book's estimate), and at least 3.8 t of robot spares. With the 2.8 t left at the planned departure, those spares carry the fleet at [chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md#sizing-the-colonys-spares)'s floor rate to a December 2047 departure; the stock on hand runs out about the end of September 2046, up to about two weeks before the flight lands, a gap the robots bridge by idling their least-needed machines. If the first crew instead slips to the 2045/46 window, the flight also lands about 4.8 t of fresh surface food, so the crew does not eat the 2041 stock at about 5–6.2 years old ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)). The committed cargo, about 7–12 t, leaves most of the lander's ~100 t for expansion cargo, the next crew's ascent vehicle with its pad coolers and the surface half of that crew's food reserve, about 4.8 t, among it ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#crew-and-after-20432048)). Its manifest is fixed about early September 2045, three months before it leaves, with the same lead as the gates, and the flight goes whatever the colony reports (chapter 13).

The 2045/46 window's minimum-energy path arrives too fast for a lander. Leaving January 22, 2046, and arriving December 18, 2046, it reaches Mars at about 5.1 km/s [1], an entry at about 7.1 km/s, above the 6.6 km/s any lander in this plan is built for, and on JPL's DE441 ephemeris [32] each day's lowest-energy path from January 8 to 22 arrives at 4.4–5.1 km/s. Earlier departures arrive slower. Leaving about December 4–18, 2045, takes a C3 of 9.3–10.7 km²/s² instead of about 8.6 and a coast of 290–300 days, and the ship reaches Mars in late September to mid-October 2046 at 3.3–3.4 km/s and enters at 5.9–6.0 km/s, inside the 5.5–6.3 km/s of the waves, with no braking burn (this book's calculation). The handbook lists the same path among the window's energy minima, as its slowest Type II arrival: leaving December 3, 2045, at a C3 of 10.84 km²/s², it reaches Mars on September 21, 2046, at 3.26 km/s [1]. NASA's DRA 5.0 Addendum gives its own propulsively captured cargo in this window the same path, leaving December 11, 2045, and arriving at 3.28 km/s; its aerocaptured cargo, which sheds its arrival speed in the atmosphere, leaves on the minimum-energy date, January 23, 2046, and arrives at 5.18 km/s [33]. A path that needs no burn exists on every day from late November to early February, but from about the turn of the year it enters at 6.4–6.6 km/s, the top of the landers' envelope.

> Assumption: The committed food flight leaves Earth about December 4–18, 2045, and enters directly, with no braking burn: about 630 t of propellant and 9 tankers, or about 850 t and 11 in the pessimistic case, landing in late September to mid-October 2046. Justification: leaving in the two weeks up to the January 22 optimum and braking by up to 0.8 km/s a few days before arrival would take about 830–850 t and 11 tankers (14 pessimistic) to enter at 6.6 km/s, the top of the envelope; leaving about December 29 to January 12 would need only 8 tankers, but it enters at up to 6.6 km/s and loads the eighth tanker to within about 2 t, the margin that made this chapter size every ship for the worst day of its launch period (this book's calculation on DE441 [32]). A replacement ERV would leave in the same December weeks ([above](#the-earth-return-vehicle)).

A crew ship in this window avoids the burn the same way if it leaves as early. So does a ship in the 2048 window that leaves about late January to early February 2048; near the window's April minimum-energy date it too would arrive at about 5.1 km/s (this book's calculation). When the other chapters date a crew's slip into either window, they name it by its minimum-energy date, January 2046 or April 2048; the crew ship itself would leave some weeks earlier, as above.

## What would change this conclusion

A demonstrated landed mass well below 100 t per ship, or no ship-to-ship transfer before 2033, would each move Wave 1 to 2037 or double the number of landers. Boil-off in the filling orbit costs launches instead. At about 10 t/day, this chapter's upper estimate for a full ship, each ship needs 10–11 tankers and Waves 2 and 3 about 5.6 launches a week; above about 15 t/day even the base case needs more launches for Wave 2 than the pessimistic case's 52, which takes more pads or a wave spread over two windows (this chapter's calculation).

## Autonomy

The autonomy levels are those of [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md), and humans keep launch decisions on Earth. On Mars, EDL has always been autonomous: it lasts minutes, and a signal needs at least 6 minutes for the round trip.

### What A3 must do

In Earth orbit, rendezvous, docking, transfer, and each hold-or-abort decision run automatically under independent safety controllers; campaign scheduling stays with humans. At Mars the ship flies the entry, picks a safe target, and lands on a beacon on its own. On the ground, the site safes each ship, offloads it along preplanned sequences, and recovers from anticipated faults such as a stuck crane. For years before the crew arrives, the Orchestrator keeps the MAV's propellant cold and verified and the ERV healthy on its own, sending reports to Earth.

### What A4 adds

A4 handles what nobody planned for: an improvised offload plan for a lander that touches down tilted, or a rebuilt manifest after a ship is lost at launch, so the loss costs months rather than a wave.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could design pads for the ground it actually finds, build ascent vehicles from local metal, and grow the propellant plant toward the megawatt scale a full refill needs, turning expended landers into returnable ships.

## Risks

- Orbital refueling, launch surge, and ship reuse ([R08](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r08)). No transfer demonstration before 2033 slips Wave 1 to 2037; boil-off above about 15 t/day would push Waves 2 and 3 past the launch rate even the pessimistic case plans for. Launch surge mitigations: more pads, earlier filling if storage works, or one lander moved to the next window.
- The first heavy landing ([R14](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r14)). Without the pathfinder, Wave 1 lands first on unprepared ground. Mitigation: fly the pathfinder, or use the arrival order set out under "The 2033 pathfinder."
- The reference vehicle not ready for Mars ([R16](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r16)). SpaceX's 2026 turn to the Moon [8] leaves the Mars variant unscheduled; landed-tonne budgets let other landers carry the cargo at two to four times the landings.
- The ERV's propellant hold ([R26](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r26)): about 3.2 years, more than five if a window is missed. Mitigation: active cooling sized for Mars orbit (the baseline, about 1 t with a spare cooler), storable return propellants, or the DRA 5.0 transit-vehicle alternative. Counting the alignment burn, the cooling has to last until 0.9–1.8 years before the planned departure; an earlier failure after the crew launches means a missed window and a replacement ERV, the next crew's, already being built to leave in December 2045 for about 11 tankers (13 in the pessimistic case), which needs no new capsule rating. If its own cooling also falls short, about 2–3 missions in 1,000 at the flown rate, the crew stays to about April 2050 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#schedule-logic-what-slips-and-by-how-much)).
- The MAV as a single point of failure ([R28](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r28)); the lifeboat reserve is propellant, not a second vehicle. Mitigation: independent verification of the full load before the crew launches, and NASA's rule that the crew does not land until the MAV is full [23].

Whether Wave 1 flies in 2035 turns on the first two; [the register](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md) scores all five and names what to watch.

## Open questions

- What is the dry mass of a Mars-configured V3 ship, and what landed mass can its EDL system support? A value well below 100 t would force more landers per wave. And how much lighter is an ERV built from one without heat shield, flaps, or landing legs: enough to carry its 9–12 t capsule within 150 t, one tanker fewer for each ERV, or not?
- What boil-off rate does a real Starship see in LEO during a 30-day fill, and on a 6- to 13-month coast? Each additional 5 t/day in LEO adds 1.5 tankers per ship; doubling the coast rate to 0.1% per day adds a tanker in three of the six windows.
- How long will a tanker fleet stand down after a launch failure? In this chapter's campaign model, with pads for about 1.2 launches a day, a week-long stand-down costs under 1% of ships their window and a 16-day one about 7%; with pads for 0.8 a day, about 2–6% and 9–12%. Scrubs alone cost none at either; a tenth tanker per ship, the alternative to flying one on demand after a missed alignment, does not change that.
- Should the 2041 methane option become the baseline, taking methane production off the crew's critical path?
- How closely can the ERV's 2042 capture hit its aim? Two independent calculations and NASA's studies of the same orbit agree on this chapter's 100–170 m/s for lining the orbit up with the ascent from 42°N and the departure, but each degree of error in the approach plane adds 20–50 m/s unless it is corrected in the first days, and a return in the next window after a missed one needs about 210–300 m/s.
- Can the ERV's cryocoolers be qualified to hold its return propellant for more than three years in Mars orbit, and does the crew return in a capsule rated for the 12.1–13.7 km/s Earth entry of the planned dates, or leave Mars about November 16–22 to enter at 11.9–12.1 km/s, still faster than any crew has entered? This book's working assumption sits between them: a capsule rated for about 12.5 km/s on a return from the planned window that reaches Earth about June 23, 2046; tile and seam tests near 3,000 W/cm² decide whether it holds.
- If the ERV fails, does the next crew's ERV, leaving in December 2045 for about 11 tankers, become its replacement, with one in November 2043 only if its parts are built ahead, this book's working assumption, or does a second ERV fly in the November 2043 window on every mission, as a next crew in the 2045/46 window would need anyway ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#crew-and-after-20432048))? How soon after a failure found at capture could a replacement be readied, and what does lining up its orbit for a December 2047 departure cost after only 15 months in orbit?
