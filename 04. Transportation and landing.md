# 4. Transportation and landing

*Last reviewed: September 26, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, November 2035.* The first cargo ship of Wave 1 comes out of the pink sky tail first, three engines lit, a pillar of exhaust scouring the plain. Nobody on Earth watches live; the images arrive minutes after the ship has settled. Nine days later a second ship lands a kilometer to the east, aiming at the beacon the first one set down. It carries the reactor. Within a week two heavy rovers, hitched in tandem, are towing it at walking pace toward the reactor field 2 km away. On Earth, the launch teams are already refurbishing tanker ships for the 2037 window.

[Chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md) picks the ground: flat, rock-poor plain over shallow ice in Arcadia Planitia. Every tonne the colony uses there before its first crew arrives must be launched from Earth, pushed onto a path to Mars, slowed from about 6 km/s at the top of the Martian atmosphere, and set down upright within reach of the tonne before it.

In brief: a Starship-class ship holds two to three times the propellant it needs to deliver 100 t to Mars in any window from 2033 to 2043. Cargo ships are therefore only partly refilled in Earth orbit, with 8 or 9 tanker flights each (9 in 2037, 2039, and 2041), and the landed mass is set by the lander and its entry, descent, and landing (EDL) system. Refueling in orbit, a fast launch cadence, and heavy landings on Mars are all undemonstrated as of September 2026. The crew's way home is a small Mars ascent vehicle (MAV) to Mars orbit, where an Earth return vehicle (ERV) waits.

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) fixes the campaign. An optional pathfinder flies in 2033, and three cargo waves (11 landers, 770 t) land in November 2035, July 2038, and July 2040. In August 2042 an ERV reaches Mars orbit and a support lander brings the surface half of the food reserve and spares; a crew of four departs in November 2043 with a way home. Each ship is planned at ~100 t landed ([the reference vehicle](#the-reference-vehicle-starship-as-of-september-2026) gives the basis), and this chapter derives the tanker flights each ship needs.

## What any cargo lander must do

Any lander qualifies that can do four things.

First, it must reach low Earth orbit (LEO) and leave it with the window's departure energy, which for a heavy lander means refueling in orbit. That energy is C3, the square of the speed left after escaping Earth, in km²/s².

Second, it must survive entry at 5.5–6.6 km/s, slow down in an atmosphere with less than 1% of Earth's surface pressure, and land propulsively. NASA's human-scale lander studies (26–36 t landed) already rule out supersonic parachutes at this mass [18].

Third, it must land within a few hundred meters of a target without digging a crater under itself or blasting rocks into the ships already there.

Fourth, it must get its cargo to the ground: a 15 t reactor in a cargo bay tens of meters up is useless until something lowers it.

NASA's Design Reference Architecture 5.0 (DRA 5.0) sized cargo landers for about 40 t of payload [25], and a 2015 NASA study put the smallest lander able to support human missions at 43 t at Mars entry, sized by the MAV [22]. Landers of that class would need 20–40 landings for [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s 770 t [22], [25]. Because the plan is written in landed tonnes, any mix of landers that delivers the tonnage in the right windows closes the budget.

## The reference vehicle: Starship as of September 2026

Starship is the only vehicle in development sized to land about 100 t of cargo on Mars, and with its dry mass and landing propellant such a ship weighs about 300 t at entry (worked example below). SpaceX's current Starship page gives the V3 ship a propellant capacity of 1,600 t and more than 100 t to orbit in a fully reusable configuration [2]. The page gives no payload to the Martian surface, only several hundred tonnes of cargo carried to Mars after refilling in orbit [2]. This book's ~100 t landed per ship comes from a NASA-led architecture study and earlier SpaceX statements [12]. [Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) adopts it as a planning assumption, and nothing in this chapter derives it.

Waves 2 and 3 plan only 60–70 t of cargo per lander, leaving 30–40 t as margin for lander mass growth, EDL margin in the first heavy landings, and dispersions in the 2037 entry.

Both stages use Raptor 3, a full-flow staged-combustion methane and oxygen engine: 250–280 tf (2.45–2.75 MN) of sea-level thrust and 350 s specific impulse (Isp) by SpaceX's figures [2], [3], and about 380 s for the vacuum variant of Raptor 2 [4]. SpaceX chose methane and oxygen partly because both can be made on Mars [12].

As of September 2026 no ship has reached orbit, and none has been recovered and flown again, although SpaceX has caught and reflown boosters [6]. Starship had flown 13 integrated test flights by July 2026 [28]. V3 debuted on Flight 12 on May 22, 2026; Flights 12 and 13 flew suborbital paths as planned, and both boosters were lost [5], [6], [28]. Flight 14, the first attempt to reach orbit, is set for no earlier than September 28, 2026 [7], [29].

In February 2026 Elon Musk announced that SpaceX's priority is now a city on the Moon, with work toward a Mars city to start in about five to seven years [8], so the Mars version has no funded schedule. The plan still targets 2035. That window pairs a moderate departure energy with the gentlest arrival of the early windows (next section), and a refueled Starship that lands on the Moon is most of what a Mars cargo lander needs.

> Assumption: A Mars-configured V3-class cargo ship has a dry mass of 130 t, with 160 t used as the pessimistic case. SpaceX has not published V3 dry mass; the Mars version needs a larger heat shield, landing legs, and cargo-handling gear.

## Launch windows: only 2037 is hard at both ends

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) lists every window from 2031 to 2048 from NASA's *Interplanetary Mission Design Handbook* [1]. The 2037 and 2039 windows are the most expensive in the plan, with minimum C3 of 14.84 and 12.17 km²/s² against 7.78 in 2033 and 10.19 in 2035 [1]. In 2035 the best path is a fast Type I transfer (less than 180° around the Sun); in 2037 and 2039 it is a slower Type II transfer [1]. Departure declination, in the table below, is the outbound direction's angle north or south of Earth's equator, which sets the lowest orbit inclination for a single departure burn.

A lander must also survive arrival. The handbook gives the Mars arrival hyperbolic excess speed (v∞) for each minimum-energy trajectory [1]. That is the lander's speed relative to Mars before the planet's gravity pulls it in. Combining it in quadrature with Mars's escape speed at the entry interface (about 4.9 km/s at 125 km altitude), as the square root of the sum of their squares, gives the entry speed.

The cheap 2033 window has the fastest arrival of the set. The pathfinder, whose job is to prove EDL, may therefore prefer the Type I alternative, which arrives slower but needs its own orbit plane for its −53° declination, or accept a harder entry than the waves will see. The 2039 arrival is as gentle as 2035's.

> Assumption: Each wave launches within the handbook's minimum-energy launch period, which spans 3–6 weeks around the optimum [1]. Ships in one wave depart on different days inside that period.

## Refueling in Earth orbit

A Starship reaches LEO with little propellant left and must be refilled by tanker Starships, the idea behind NASA's lunar Human Landing System with its tanker, depot, and lander variants [26].

### Ship-to-ship transfer has never flown

The only in-space propellant transfer so far was between two tanks inside one ship, on Flight 3 in March 2024 [10]. NASA's Office of Inspector General (OIG) reported on March 10, 2026, that the large-scale ship-to-ship demonstration, planned for March 2025, had slipped 12 months to March 2026. The report quoted the program's view of cryogenic transfer as "one of the most significant technical challenges facing the provider" [9]. That date has passed without a flight. [Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) assumes the transfer is demonstrated before the 2033 window.

The OIG report describes SpaceX's lunar plan as a depot followed by more than 10 tankers, one about every 6 days, from Starbase in Texas and Kennedy Space Center in Florida [9]; in November 2023 NASA officials put the whole lunar campaign at launches in the "high teens" [11]. Neither is a Mars figure, and no published source gives a Mars-specific count, so this chapter derives its own.

### Each Mars ship is its own depot

Tankers fill each Mars-bound ship directly. A separate depot, as in the lunar plan [9], adds a transfer per ship and helps only if multimonth storage works. Staging from a high elliptical orbit would raise the total tanker count. The plan uses neither, which leaves two questions: the orbit where the ship waits, and how much propellant it loses while waiting.

> Assumption: Filling ships orbit at 28.5–33° inclination and 300–400 km altitude. Justification: tankers from Starbase (about 26°N) and Kennedy (28.5°N) share an orbit only at 28.5° or more. That orbit also keeps about 120 m/s more of Earth's rotation than the space station's 51.6°. A single departure burn needs at least the departure declination, 6–22° for cargo and 31.2° for the crew [1]. Earth's bulge swings the plane about 7° per day, so it lines up with the departure direction only every three to four weeks, and each filling campaign ends on one of those dates.

A fully fueled Starship in LEO with a sunshield absorbs roughly 33 kWt of heat, by one independent estimate [13]; if all of it boils propellant (213 kJ/kg for oxygen, 510 kJ/kg for methane), the ship loses about 10 t per day, about 0.6% of a full load. NASA depot studies with multilayer insulation found boil-off well under 0.05% per day [31], so insulation decides the rate. Removing the heat actively would take about 0.5 MW of electric power on one ship [13], thousands of times the ~100 W any flight cryocooler had reached by 2021 ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)).

> Assumption: A Mars ship being filled loses an average of 5 t/day of propellant over a 30-day filling campaign, 150 t in total, with passive insulation, a sunshield, and subcooled loading (propellant chilled below its boiling point, so it absorbs heat before it starts to boil). No active cooling is assumed. This is the largest uncertainty in the tanker count below.

Boil-off does not stop at departure. After the departure burn a cargo ship carries only its landing propellant, and that has to last a coast of 7 to 13 months.

> Assumption: On the coast to Mars the landing propellant boils off at 0.05% per day, the upper end of the NASA depot figure above [31], in both the base and the pessimistic case. Over the 208–392 days of the 2033–2043 transfers that is 10–18% of the landing propellant, 8–15 t per ship in the base case, so each ship leaves Earth carrying the extra. The ERV's figures below are stated before storage losses; its multiyear hold at Mars needs active cooling or storable propellants in any case ([R26](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r26)).

## The rocket equation: propellant never limits the payload

Propellant needs come from the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, Δv = g₀·Isp·ln(m₀/m₁), applied in two burns. The trans-Mars injection (TMI) burn leaves a 350 km circular orbit, adds 0.1 km/s for finite-burn losses and course corrections, and uses 375 s (a mix of vacuum and sea-level Raptors). The Mars landing burn is 0.9 km/s at 350 s, carried all the way from Earth, with the coast boil-off above. The atmosphere does the rest of the slowing.

```text
TMI delta-v   = sqrt(C3 + 2*mu_E/r) - sqrt(mu_E/r),  r = 6,728 km,  mu_E = 398,600 km^3/s^2
Landed mass   = dry + payload
At Mars entry = landed * exp(900 / (9.80665 * 350))            = landed * 1.300
After TMI     = landed + (at_entry - landed) / (1 - coast loss),   coast loss = 1 - 0.9995^days
At LEO depart = after_TMI * exp((TMI + 100) / (9.80665 * 375))
Propellant    = at_LEO_depart - landed    (must be <= 1,600 t)

Example, 2035, dry 130 t, payload 100 t, 208-day coast:
  TMI = 3.65 km/s  ->  ratio exp(3,750 / 3,677) = 2.77
  at entry = 230 * 1.300 = 299 t;  landing propellant 69 t, so 76.5 t before a 9.9% coast loss
  after TMI = 230 + 76.5 = 306.5 t;  at departure = 306.5 * 2.77 = 849 t
  propellant = 849 - 230 = 619 t
```

The pessimistic case raises the dry mass to 160 t, lowers TMI Isp to 365 s, and raises the landing burn to 1.2 km/s.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2033 | 3.54 | 600 | 802 | 483 / 359 |
| 2035 | 3.65 | 619 | 824 | 465 / 345 |
| 2037 | 3.85 | 689 | 923 | 404 / 291 |

The ship's 1,600 t is 1.7–2.7 times the propellant these cases need. With full tanks, even the pessimistic ship could carry 291–359 t through to landing, nearly three times the ~100 t planning figure. The limit is the lander: its structure, entry mass, and landing burn.

A ship therefore needs only 600–690 t in the base case and 800–920 t in the pessimistic case, and the spare capacity can buy a faster transit (useful for the crew ship) or cover boil-off.

### 8 or 9 tankers per ship

> Assumption: Each tanker delivers 100 t of usable propellant to the Mars ship after its own ascent and transfer losses, matching V3's rated reusable payload to orbit [2]. A 150 t tanker is the optimistic case.

The load to deliver is the propellant needed, including the coast boil-off, plus 5% for residuals and reserve, plus 150 t of boil-off during filling: 780–870 t in the base case.

The expensive windows add a ninth tanker per ship in 2037 and 2039, whose slow Type II coasts also lose the most landing propellant, and the ten-month coast of 2041 needs a ninth as well. 2035's 800 t fills eight tankers exactly, so any shortfall there adds a ninth. None of this changes the payload, and 2037's entry is only 0.4 km/s faster than 2035's. Doubling the coast rate to 0.1% per day would add a tanker in four of the six windows. These counts are below the 11–16 tankers it would take to fill a near-empty 1,600 t tank with 100–150 t per flight.

The annual totals fit existing environmental approvals: 25 Starship launches per year from Starbase [14], 44 from Kennedy's Launch Complex 39A [15], and 76 from Cape Canaveral's Space Launch Complex 37 [16], 145 in all, of which a wave uses at most about a third. But 145 per year averages only 2.8 per week, half the 5–6 per week that Waves 2 and 3 need for eight weeks.

> Assumption: A Starship pad can support one launch every 4–7 days by the mid-2030s, so Waves 2 and 3 need 3–5 active pads across Texas and Florida. A tanker refurbishes and reflies every 14 days, about 4 flights per campaign, so those waves need a fleet of 9–12 tankers. Ship reuse has not been demonstrated as of September 2026 [6]; without it, every tanker flight consumes a new ship and the campaign cost multiplies.

Every Mars-bound ship except the ERV stays there: 14–15 in all (11 cargo landers, the pathfinder, one or two support landers, and the crew ship).

## Entry, descent, and landing

EDL is the least demonstrated part of the plan. Nothing heavier than about 1 t has landed on Mars; the record is Perseverance at 1,025 kg [18]. Heavy landers must use supersonic retropropulsion (SRP): firing engines into the oncoming supersonic flow to slow down and steer. NASA treats SRP as the enabling capability for landing human-scale payloads on Mars [18].

### Supersonic retropropulsion

NASA studied SRP with SpaceX from 2014 to 2017, using Falcon 9 first-stage reentries, which by July 2017 had flown several burns in Mars-relevant conditions [17]. The team concluded that the remaining challenge is flight systems engineering for a specific Mars vehicle, not new technology [17], and NASA Langley continues subscale ground tests and model validation [18]. All of this evidence comes from Earth; Mars's thin carbon dioxide atmosphere, its winds, and the plume's interaction with a large vehicle body are modeled only.

> Assumption: The reference ship enters Mars's atmosphere directly, without aerocapture, then descends belly-first and lands on its sea-level engines, with a total propulsive Δv of about 0.9 km/s. Ballistic capture and skip-entry trajectories are left for later study.

### Landing within 100 m

Perseverance's terrain-relative navigation, which matches descent images to an onboard map, could move its landing point by up to 600 m to avoid hazards, and NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory estimated it raised the chance of a safe landing from about 85% to about 99% [21], a result proven only at the 1 t scale. Colony landers must also land close enough for rovers to tow 15 t loads to the work sites and far enough apart that one plume does not damage another ship or the base. To land inside that band, the plan adds radio beacons on each prepared pad.

> Assumption: 99.7% of landings touch down within 100 m of the pad beacon, using terrain-relative navigation plus the beacons. For a two-dimensional scatter that means about 29 m standard deviation per axis; a 3σ circle of 100 m would hold only 98.9%. The remaining 0.3% per landing leaves about a 3% chance that one of the 11 cargo landings ends outside a prepared site ([R14](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r14)). Pads sit about 1 km apart, the estimated reach of landing ejecta, with berms between them. The landing field lies at least 2 km from the habitat, the reactor field, and the wells, with berms on the line of sight ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md), [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). Chapter 8 prepares each site to this accuracy, so its site size rests on the 100 m figure.

### Why pads come first

NASA's plume-surface interaction research lists the hazards of a landing engine: dust clouds that blind sensors, high-energy ejecta, and craters under the lander. Even NASA's small InSight lander, which touched down in 2018, dug pits on Mars [19]. Models and vacuum-chamber tests to predict these effects are in progress [19], [20]. A Starship-class lander's three sea-level Raptors produce 7–8 MN of thrust, roughly 2,000 times the 3.6 kN of InSight's 12 descent thrusters combined [30]. A NASA-led study of Starship on Mars warns that without landing pads, the exhaust could dig holes under a lander, melt subsurface ice, and tip it over [12].

Prepared pads remove those hazards after the first wave. Wave 1 lands on unprepared ground and carries the pad kit and robots to build the first pads before Wave 2 arrives in July 2038. Chapter 8 covers how pads are built from local regolith.

### The 2033 pathfinder

The optional pathfinder exists to retire the EDL risk before Wave 1 commits three ships and a reactor. One heavy lander in the 2033 window would demonstrate SRP at Mars scale, measure the plume's effect on the actual site, and land survey robots that map the future pad field.

> Assumption: Wave 1 proceeds in 2035 whether or not the pathfinder flies. Ships on direct entry cannot hold in orbit to wait for the first landing. Without the pathfinder, launch spacing puts arrivals days apart: the reactor rides the second ship, critical spares are split across ships, and the first touchdown's data update the aim points of the ships still in flight.

## Surface logistics: the first days after landing

> Assumption: Each cargo lander carries its own offload system (a crane or cargo elevator rated at 20 t) and a ramp or platform for rovers. [Chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md) specifies the rovers and the transport trailer.

The first days after a Wave 1 landing follow a fixed sequence, driven by power and by what can fail first.

1. Safe the vehicle: vent or close off propellant, set the thermal state, and confirm the lander is upright and stable.
2. Establish the communications link through the Mars Relay Network and the lander's own antenna ([chapter 10](10.%20Communications.md)).
3. Deploy the first solar panels and batteries so offload does not drain the lander.
4. Lower the rovers and the trailer, then survey and grade the tow route to the reactor field.
5. Lift the reactor, the most valuable single item, onto its trailer and tow it at least 2 km to the reactor field ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md) describes the delivery).
6. Offload the rest in priority order: water and oxygen equipment ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)), construction robots, and the pad kit.

Empty landers stay useful as tank farms, equipment shelters, and scrap metal ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). Nearly nine years after the first of them lands, the same field receives the crew ship, the first arrival that must also leave.

By design, no Starship is refilled on Mars for the return; a full ~1,600 t refill is an expansion-era project ([chapter 15](15.%20Terraforming%20and%20the%20long%20view.md)).

### The crew vehicle and crew lander

> Assumption: The reference crew vehicle is a Starship-class ship, refilled in LEO, that departs in the November 2043 window, enters directly, and lands on a prepared pad at the colony. After landing it stays as a surface asset (backup habitat, storm shelter) and is not refueled.

No ship lands on the crew pad before the crew does, because every ship stays where it lands. [Chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md) builds the pad new in 2041–2043, sintered out to 50 m with the same recipe as the landing-site cores. By then those cores have taken 11 cargo landings and the support landings, and their plume and ejecta records, with inspection of the finished pad through the February 2043 conjunction and a dust season, are the evidence the crew lands on. The crew ship's unused tank capacity can shorten the trip and cut the crew's cruise dose of about 1.84 mSv/day ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md)).

The fallback is NASA's DRA 5.0 model: a transit habitat waits in Mars orbit while the crew uses a separate lander, merging crew vehicle and ERV [25].

### The Mars ascent vehicle

By one NASA study, the MAV is the most mass-sensitive element in any human Mars architecture: every added kilogram of MAV dry mass adds 5–9 kg to its liftoff mass, which then drives the size of the lander and of the Earth-to-Mars transport system [27].

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s load of 7.0 t of methane and 22.7 t of oxygen comes from the 2015 four-crew design and the NASA plant study sized to fill it [22], [24]. The 2019 update moved the rendezvous to a 5-sol orbit and restated DRA 5.0's rule that the crew is not cleared to land until the MAV's tanks are verified full [23], [25].

> Assumption: The colony's MAV follows the 2015/2019 NASA four-crew design, with the 2015 design's 9.4 t dry mass and 29.7 t propellant load, a landed allocation of 15 t that covers ground-support equipment and growth, and an ascent to the ERV's 1-sol orbit as in the 2015 design. It lands in Wave 3 in July 2040 with empty tanks. It is craned and towed like a reactor to its launch pad in the landing field ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)), and there it is filled from load 1, already waiting in storage ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md) holds the production schedule).

NASA's MAV reference designs (DRA 5.0 and the 2015 and 2019 MAV studies) bring the methane from Earth and make only the oxygen on Mars [22], [23], [25]; a later NASA study sized a plant to make both [24]. This plan makes both. That saves about 7 t of Wave 3 mass and tests the full plant, but it puts methane production on the path to the crew gate, with about two years of margin at the design dry mass.

That margin holds only while the MAV stays near its design mass. By this book's single-stage estimate from the 2015 design's mass ratio (39.1 t at liftoff over 9.4 t dry, about 4.2), each added tonne of dry mass needs about 3.2 t more propellant, roughly 0.75 t of methane and 2.4 t of oxygen, or about 50 more days of plant time per load. If the dry mass grew to 12 t, each load would rise to about 38 t, load 2 would finish in about May 2042 instead of September 2041, and the margin before the August 2043 decision would shrink to about 15 months.

> Assumption: If the methane plant fails, the 2041 support flight can carry one MAV load of methane (7.0 t) from Earth, at the cost of 7 t of that flight's spares, and the oxygen comes from the colony's oxygen line (chapter 6). The option has to be chosen before the October 2041 launch.

### The Earth return vehicle

> Assumption: The ERV is a Starship-class ship with a crew cabin and 20 t of return provisions (150 t total return mass), launched in the October 2041 window, captured into a 1-sol orbit, and holding the propellant for trans-Earth injection.

The table's Δv values assume a Mars departure v∞ of 3.0–3.5 km/s, above the ~2.7 km/s of the planned early November 2045 departure, to cover off-optimal return dates, and a propulsive capture for the 2041 arrival (v∞ 2.49 km/s [1]); the rocket equation then gives ~610 t in LEO and ~70 t held at Mars, before storage losses. Holding 70 t of cryogenic propellant for about 3.2 years needs either active cooling, unproven at this scale, or storable propellants for the return stage.

DRA 5.0 put Earth entry speed on a nominal Mars return at up to 12 km/s, against 11 km/s for a lunar return, and recommended 12 km/s as a limit; earlier studies allowed up to 14 km/s [25]. The ERV brings a capsule rated for that entry or burns propellant to slow down first.

### Return windows and stay length

The plan is a conjunction-class mission, a long stay that waits for the planets to line up again; DRA 5.0 used about 180 days each way and about 500 days on Mars [25].

> Assumption: With arrival on about September 16, 2044, the low-energy Mars departure falls in early November 2045, a surface stay of about 410 days. The return takes 220–235 days, reaching Earth about June 2046, 940–950 days after launch. By this book's trajectory calculation, staying 450 days would raise the departure v∞ from about 2.7 to about 3.5 km/s for a 220-day return. These dates must be refined with ephemeris-grade tools.

Surface consumables are sized for about 1,000 days (chapter 2), more than twice the planned stay; food for the transits travels in the crew ship and the ERV, outside that reserve ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)). A missed return window means waiting about 26 months, a surface stay of about 1,190 days, and the ERV would then hold its propellant for more than five years. The plants keep making oxygen and water, but the food reserve runs out after 1,000 days, in mid-June 2047, about 190 days short.

> Assumption: The January 2046 window (crew rotation and expansion, [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)) lands at least 2 t of shelf-stable food at the colony about December 2046, whether or not the first crew leaves on time; if they do, it starts the next crew's reserve. That covers the 190-day gap with about 80 days of margin, six months before the reserve would run out. Greenhouse output, about a fifth of the calories ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)), adds margin and is not counted.

## What would change this conclusion

A demonstrated landed mass well below 100 t per ship, boil-off above about 15 t/day, or no ship-to-ship transfer before 2033 would each move Wave 1 to 2037 or double the number of landers.

## Autonomy

The autonomy levels are those of [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md), and humans keep launch decisions on Earth. On Mars, EDL has always been autonomous: it lasts minutes, and a signal needs at least 6 minutes for the round trip.

### What A3 must do

In Earth orbit, rendezvous, docking, transfer, and each hold-or-abort decision run automatically under independent safety controllers; campaign scheduling stays with humans. At Mars the ship flies the entry, picks a safe target, and lands on a beacon on its own. On the ground, the site safes each ship, offloads it along preplanned sequences, and recovers from anticipated faults such as a stuck crane. For years before the crew arrives, the Orchestrator keeps the MAV's propellant cold and verified and the ERV healthy on its own, sending reports to Earth.

### What A4 adds

A4 handles what nobody planned for: an improvised offload plan for a lander that touches down tilted, or a rebuilt manifest after a ship is lost at launch, so the loss costs months rather than a wave.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could design pads for the ground it actually finds, build ascent vehicles from local metal, and grow the propellant plant toward the megawatt scale a full refill needs, turning expended landers into returnable ships.

## Risks

- Orbital refueling, launch surge, and ship reuse ([R08](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r08)). No transfer demonstration before 2033, or boil-off above about 15 t/day, slips Wave 1 to 2037. Launch surge mitigations: more pads, earlier filling if storage works, or one lander moved to the next window.
- The first heavy landing ([R14](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r14)). Without the pathfinder, Wave 1 lands first on unprepared ground. Mitigation: fly the pathfinder, or use the arrival order set out under "The 2033 pathfinder."
- The reference vehicle not ready for Mars ([R16](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r16)). SpaceX's 2026 turn to the Moon [8] leaves the Mars variant unscheduled; landed-tonne budgets let other landers carry the cargo at two to four times the landings.
- The ERV's propellant hold ([R26](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r26)): about 3.2 years, more than five if a window is missed. Mitigation: active cooling sized for Mars orbit, storable return propellants, or the DRA 5.0 transit-vehicle alternative.
- The MAV as a single point of failure ([R28](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r28)); the lifeboat reserve is propellant, not a second vehicle. Mitigation: independent verification of the full load before the crew launches, and NASA's rule that the crew does not land until the MAV is full [23].

Whether Wave 1 flies in 2035 turns on the first two; [the register](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md) scores all five and names what to watch.

## Open questions

- What is the dry mass of a Mars-configured V3 ship, and what landed mass can its EDL system support? A value well below 100 t would force more landers per wave.
- What boil-off rate does a real Starship see in LEO during a 30-day fill, and on a 7- to 13-month coast? Each additional 5 t/day in LEO adds 1.5 tankers per ship; doubling the coast rate to 0.1% per day adds a tanker in four of the six windows.
- Should the 2041 methane option become the baseline, taking methane production off the crew's critical path?
- How does the ERV hold its return propellant for more than three years, and how does the crew survive Earth entry at up to 12 km/s?
