# 2. Baseline and budgets

*Last reviewed: September 28, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, 2041.* The Orchestrator keeps one ledger above all others. Every hour it adds what the reactors made, subtracts what the wells, electrolyzers, printers, and heaters took, and checks the balance against a single question: if Earth went silent tomorrow and the sky stayed brown for four months, would everything that matters still be warm, pressurized, and fueled? Since the second reactor finished commissioning in January 2039, the answer has been yes.

This chapter is that ledger, written down before the first lander leaves Earth. It holds the book's launch dates, power levels, tonnages, and physical constants, and the other chapters link here instead of restating them. Every figure must close with the colony running at autonomy level A3, which [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md) defines: a robot fleet that runs the site for at least 120 days without contact and recovers from anticipated faults on its own. The wave success criteria and the crew gate assume A3 and nothing more, and higher levels change only schedules and margins.

In brief: the colony is built in three cargo waves launched in the 2035, 2037, and 2039 windows. By early 2041, once reactor 3 finishes commissioning, it has 200 kWe of firm fission power, the output it can count on with any one reactor down. It fuels a small Mars ascent vehicle (MAV) soon after the vehicle lands in September 2040, banks a second ascent load as a lifeboat reserve by mid-2042, and passes its crew gate in time for a crew departure in November 2043. Two limits stay open. Before reactor 2 is commissioned in January 2039, a planet-wide dust storm that finds no reactor running, in Wave 1's first weeks or after a reactor trip, would leave the critical loads short of power, a risk the plan accepts with mitigations. And every transit option gives the crew more radiation than NASA's 600 mSv career limit, so the first crew flies above it under individual waivers.

## Reference site: Arcadia Planitia at 42°N
<a id="reference-site"></a>

The reference site is in Arcadia Planitia, a smooth northern plain with buried water ice near the surface, and the working point is 42°N, 192°E, at about −3.5 km relative to the Mars datum. Deuteronilus Mensae, known only from orbit, is the backup region. The optional 2033 pathfinder would drill the primary site and leave time to retarget Wave 1; without it, Wave 1 commits to an undrilled site ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md#the-backup-region-and-what-the-pathfinder-buys)). "Within reach" of the wells means the top of the ice lies no more than 5 m down and the ice is at least 10 m thick (chapter 3).

The landing field sits at least 2 km from the habitat, the reactor field, and the wells. Its pads lie about 1 km apart, the estimated reach of landing ejecta on dry ground, and berms block the line of sight between pads and toward the rest of the site; debris from a plume that reaches the ice may carry about 2 km, so the prepared pads must keep it off the ice, and Wave 1's three ships, which land on bare ground, touch down at least 2 km apart (this book's working assumption until the author decides). The field also sits within about 1 km of a crater's boulder field, the source of the pads' rock armor; none lies near the working point, the nearest known being 37 km away, so the survey picks the field's place from HiRISE images ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). The reactor field lies about 1 km from the habitat, beside the well field, so the heat lines stay short ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md), [chapter 5](05.%20Power.md), and [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)).

> Assumption: The site holds at least 3,000 t of mapped, drilled ice within 5 km of the landing field, confirmed before Wave 2 commits.

## Launch windows: the expensive years cost propellant
<a id="launch-opportunities"></a>

A Mars window opens roughly every 26 months, but the calendar does not cooperate with even-numbered years. The minimum-energy departures below come from NASA's *Interplanetary Mission Design Handbook* [2], with three dates corrected. C3, the characteristic energy, is the launch energy per unit mass that the departing stage must supply; for a fully fueled vehicle, higher C3 means less payload. For 2035, 2037, and 2039 the handbook's tables print departures of April 21, June 18, and July 15, but the C3, launch direction, and arrival speed printed beside them, and the handbook's own contour plots, belong to dates 63, 81, and 75 days later, the ones below (this book's check against JPL's DE441 ephemeris [26]).

The 2037 and 2039 windows are the most expensive of the period, with C3 50–90% higher than in 2033. A cargo lander takes on only the propellant its transfer needs, far less than its tanks hold, so the higher energy leaves the landed payload unchanged and adds propellant: up to about 880 t per lander to deliver in orbit, against about 810 t in 2035 and 2043, since 2037's and 2039's slow transfers also lose the most landing propellant to boil-off on the way. At 100 t per tanker every lander from 2035 to 2043 takes nine tanker flights, because each carries propellant for the worst day of its launch period; the expensive windows use up the ninth tanker's slack, and in the pessimistic case 2037 needs twelve ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)). The cheap 2033 window suits a pathfinder if the vehicles are ready in time.

> Assumption: Each wave's landers leave over about four weeks, two on either side of the optimum dates above, and carry propellant for the worst day of that spread (chapter 4). Real launch periods span 3–6 weeks around each optimum [2], which is enough to launch several landers per window from multiple pads.

## Power budget: fission for firm power, solar for the rest
<a id="power-budget"></a>

Power is the colony's master resource: water, oxygen, propellant, heat, construction, and communications all draw from it.

The reactor is a 100 kWe-class closed-Brayton unit of about 15 t, and it is on the critical path: Wave 1 needs a Mars unit qualified by 2034, without waiting for a 100 kWe unit to fly on the Moon. The 15 t figure is the lander capacity NASA's August 2025 directive assumed for a lunar reactor of at least 100 kWe [9], a limit for a lunar unit, not a design mass. Mars adds radiator structures for 0.38 g, hot structures that tolerate CO₂, and more shielding for three units near the habitat (chapter 5). No study has sized a 100 kWe unit for Mars; NASA's Mars studies of the 2000s took their reactor directly from the lunar design and added a heavier on-board shield and a mobility system [25]. This book holds the growth allowance that NASA's mass-growth practice sets for a design this early, 30%, up to about 4.5 t per reactor [24], in each reactor lander's margin, which leaves the Wave 1 reactor lander about 12.5 t. The nearest hardware is NASA's first lunar unit, Lunar Reactor-1 (LR-1), which is to land on the Moon in 2030 [19]. Its draft procurement of August 30, 2026 [20] reportedly asks for at least 20 kWe [15], and 100 kWe-class reactors are a 2030s goal [16]. LR-1 has no hardware contract yet and proves only the design lineage ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)).

> Assumption: A 100 kWe-class reactor of about 15 t is qualified for Mars in time for the June 2035 launch. If it is not, Wave 1 moves to 2037: the five 20 kWe-class units that would replace it weigh up to about 75 t, about 60 t over the allocation and more than Wave 1's margin (chapter 5).

### Power by epoch: no firm power until January 2039
<a id="firm-power"></a>

**Firm power** is what the colony can count on with its largest generator out of service. With one reactor it is zero, with two it is 100 kWe, and with three it is 200 kWe. Reactor 1 delivers first power about April 2036 and its full 100 kWe by about June 2036 (chapter 5). Each later reactor needs several months of commissioning after it lands, so firm power reaches 100 kWe in January 2039, after Wave 2, and 200 kWe in early January 2041. Starting a reactor needs Earth's approval ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md)), so reactor 3, which reaches first power in early November 2040 as the freeze before the December conjunction begins, starts under a standing approval signed before the freeze and raises its power through the conjunction.

> Assumption: Solar fields deliver a clear-sky sol-average of 6.8–8.1% of nameplate (air mass zero) at 42°N and 20–35% of that at τ≈5, so each 400 kW of nameplate gives 27–33 kW (chapter 5, from [chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md)'s insolation table, NASA Glenn's model of Mars sunlight, which puts a τ≈5 storm at the low end of that range, and the sky brightness Curiosity measured near the Sun, which sets the tracking gain).

Construction soaks up whatever headroom the other loads leave: about 70–100 kW in the first epoch whenever the reactor runs. At the 85% availability chapter 8 plans on, that builds Wave 2's landing sites about eight months before it arrives and starts on Wave 3's, with about 5% of the first epoch's spare energy left over once the losses in the line to the landing field are counted (chapter 5 and [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)).

Until reactor 1 reaches power in April 2036, Wave 1 lives on 27–33 kW of solar: keep-alive, communications, and unloading come first, and the oxygen line runs only at low duty for commissioning. After that, in the first epoch the single reactor carries the full load; if it trips in clear weather, the critical 9–14 kW runs on solar and batteries while robots hibernate. From Wave 2's landing in October 2038 until reactor 2 is commissioned, reactor 1 alone also carries Wave 2's critical loads, 23–32 kW with the habitat's heat and pressure hold; a trip in clear weather leaves both solar fields, 55–65 kW, to carry them. Between 2039 and 2040 the full load (up to 168 kW) needs both reactors, but if one trips, the other still covers the critical 23–32 kW and the propellant plant; construction and the oxygen line wait. From early 2041, once reactor 3 is running, even the worst case (194 kW) fits with margin, and the critical subtotal sits far below the 200 kWe firm level.

The keep-alive line assumes [chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)'s tank insulation of 1–2 W/m², which that chapter labels optimistic. At NASA's figure of about 10 W/m² for insulated tanks on the Mars surface [23], storage cooling could reach about 50 kWe. The critical load in 2039–2040 would then be about 70–80 kW, still inside the 100 kWe firm level with one reactor out; with the propellant plant as well, one reactor could not carry it in a storm, so the plant would throttle after a trip, though gate G3's criterion 1, shown in clear weather, would still pass. In the first epoch the same insulation would take 4–23 kWe for Wave 1's oxygen tanks instead of 0.4–4.5, raising the critical load from 9–14 kW to about 13–32 kW. The reactor still carries that, but construction would lose up to about 18 kW, more than the first epoch's ~5% energy margin, and the Wave 3 cores the rovers begin in February 2038 would wait for Wave 2's reactor, finishing about March 2040, still before they are needed. A trip at the top of that range would leave even clear-weather solar about 7 kW short, so tank cooling would be shed, as the survival floor sheds it in a storm, and the stored oxygen would boil off at about 0.25–0.4 t a sol instead of 25–75 kg, product the oxygen line makes again (chapter 5, [R27](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r27); this book's calculation).

> Assumption: Rodwell water wells take their melting heat (~10 kWt per well) from the reactors' waste heat, which a 100 kWe Brayton unit rejects at several hundred kWt. Chapter 5 checks the heat-transport distance and temperature.

### The storm case: a storm with no reactor running
<a id="the-storm-case"></a>

In a 120-day storm at τ≈5, solar output drops to 20–35% of clear-sky levels, a loss of 18–52 kW depending on epoch. Fission is unaffected, apart from dust on its radiators. The colony curtails deferrable loads: construction first, then the oxygen line, then the greenhouse. From January 2039, once reactor 2 has finished commissioning, critical loads never depend on solar power.

Storage cannot stand in for a reactor through a storm. The batteries, about 1 MWh and then 2 MWh, sit at the critical loads' feeders and are sized to carry those loads for at least a sol after a trip or a feeder fault; the rest of their capacity smooths the grid over minutes to hours. One sol of critical load takes about 0.2–0.35 MWh in the first epoch and 0.9–1.2 MWh from 2041, so Wave 3 needs no batteries of its own (chapter 5). This plan rejects storing energy as methane and oxygen: by this book's estimate (chapter 5) it returns only about 16–29% of the input energy, and every tonne burned for power is a tonne the ascent vehicle loses.

Before reactor 2 is commissioned, the colony has one reactor and zero firm power, and a τ≈5 storm that finds no reactor running leaves the critical load short. That can happen in three windows. Wave 1 lands in January 2036 near the end of a dust season, and for its first seven weeks, to about February 24, a storm would come before reactor 1 has started: solar gives 5–11 kW against 7–12 kW of keep-alive and communications. From April 2036, a reactor trip during a storm leaves 5–11 kW against the 9–14 kW critical load, and ~1 MWh of batteries covers the shortfall for about 4.7 sols at worst. From Wave 2's landing in October 2038 until reactor 2 is commissioned in January 2039, a trip in a storm leaves 11–23 kW from both fields against 23–32 kW, and ~2 MWh lasts about 3.8 sols; this window ends about three weeks before the next dust season begins.

The plan accepts all three as one risk, [R27](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r27), with three mitigations (chapter 5). The colony sheds even critical loads to a survival floor of about 3–5 kW, or 5–9 kW once the habitat is up; storm sunlight carries it except in Wave 2's first weeks, before its solar field is deployed, and about 200 kWh of battery is held back for the restart: a closed-Brayton reactor needs outside power to start, about 7–8 kW for up to 10 hours from cold by this book's reading of NASA's start-up studies, and the robots that repair it need most of the rest (chapter 5). Reactor restart is the top priority. Spares for the most likely causes of a trip are on site. A storm before first power leaves no reactor to restart: the reactor work waits for the sky to clear, and first power can slip by up to about four months, to August 2036. All four Wave 2 sites would still be finished about four months before Wave 2 lands, but the first two about two months after G2. The exposure ends when reactor 2 is commissioned in January 2039.

The bank before the habitat lands is about 590 t with the pathfinder and about 530 t without it, because Wave 1's own survey then holds the wells until about July 2036; it is held in bladders beside the well field ([chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md#filling-the-shield)). ## Propellant and consumables: two ascent loads and 1,000 days of stores
<a id="propellant-and-consumables-at-the-crew-gate"></a>

Crew return uses a small MAV that lifts the crew to Mars orbit, where they transfer to an ERV that arrived fueled in 2042. The MAV is sized like NASA's four-crew ascent vehicle from its Evolvable Mars Campaign studies: about 7.0 t of methane and 22.7 t of oxygen per full load, with a dry mass of about 9.4 t in the 2015 design [11], [14]. This plan uses that design and allocates 15 t landed, which covers ground-support equipment and growth. Dry-mass growth costs propellant: by NASA's estimate each added tonne adds 5–9 t to the liftoff mass, about 3.8–6.8 t of it propellant per load, or 70–130 more days of plant time at 85% availability, so a 12 t MAV would leave at most about 3 months before the crew decision, and none at the high end of that range ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)).

| Buffer gas (N₂ and Ar captured from the atmosphere) | ≥2 t | In storage, after the habitat's first fill and its leakage to the decision have been drawn from it: about 3 t captured by August 2043 (chapter 6) |
| Water reserve | ≥100 t | In storage |
| Shelf-stable food (4 crew × 1,000 days × 1.83 kg) | ~7.3 t | Half landed by the 2041 support flight and inspected on Mars before the launch decision, which alone covers about 500 days, more than the 410-day stay; half loaded aboard the crew vehicle and verified before launch; outbound food travels in the crew vehicle and return food in the ERV, outside this reserve (chapter 9) |

Surface stocks are sized for about 1,000 days, more than twice the planned 410-day stay, as margin against a delayed departure; the plants can also make more oxygen and water. A full missed return window stretches the stay to about 1,190 days, and the committed flight in the January 2046 window covers the difference: at least 2 t of food, a pharmacy resupply, suit seals and batteries, and robot spares ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md#return-windows-and-stay-length)). The pharmacy is the exception to surface stocking: it flies with the crew, because 87% of medications flown on the International Space Station have labeled shelf lives under 24 months [21], and each lot is dated through the crew's return to Earth in mid-2046 by its label or by testing its sister lots on Earth ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#medicine-far-from-earth)). The missed window also raises each crew member's dose, to about 1,330–1,360 mSv in chapter 12's baseline case, which the waivers must cover (see the dose section below).

The propellant plant needs 480 days of running per MAV load at 35–52 kW [11], about 565 days at the 85% availability this book plans on (chapter 13). It lands with its cryogenic storage tanks in Wave 2 in October 2038 and, after about six months of commissioning, starts producing in April 2039. Load 1 completes by about late October 2040, a month after the MAV lands in September 2040; load 2, the lifeboat reserve, completes by about May 2042. That leaves about 15 months before the August 2043 launch decision to absorb a slow start, a repair, or a lower rate. The oxygen line makes ~17.5 t of O₂ per Earth year at full rate from about June 2036, once reactor 1 reaches 100 kWe, then drops to a low duty cycle once its tanks are full ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)). The load table still carries its full 25–34 kW. Methane, and the water and power behind it, sets the pace.

A full refill of a V3 ship on Mars, about 1,600 t [8], would be roughly 350 t of methane and 1,250 t of oxygen at a 3.6:1 ratio. By this book's calculation it would need about 1.1–1.6 MW of continuous ISRU power for a full synod. Such a refill is outside this baseline and belongs to the expansion era ([chapter 15](15.%20Terraforming%20and%20the%20long%20view.md)).

## Crew mission and radiation dose: above the career limit in every case
<a id="crew-mission-and-radiation-dose"></a>

The first crew spends about 305 days outbound on the November 2043 Type II transfer, about 410 days on the surface, and 220–235 days returning after a low-energy departure in early November 2045: about 940–950 days in all ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md) and [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md)). This book's working assumption, until the author decides, takes the return within that range that reaches Earth about June 23, 2046, after about 950 days, so that the crew capsule enters at no more than about 12.5 km/s ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md#the-earth-return-vehicle)). The ERV waits in Mars orbit for about 3.2 years. With this chapter's dose constants, the mission gives each crew member an effective dose of about 810–1,260 mSv, depending on transit shielding and on the habitat's water shell (chapter 12 shows the cases). Those cases count cosmic rays at mean rates; allowances for rate uncertainty, a solar particle event in transit, the reactors, and the site's elevation raise the upper bound to about 1,600 mSv. NASA's career limit is 600 mSv [17].

No shielding case in this plan meets that limit. Even with the habitat's water shell and a crew vehicle held to NASA's 1.3 mSv/day design limit, the transits would have to last about 165 days each way, since every day cut from the outbound leg adds a day on the surface. The ships cannot fly legs that short in these windows. A 180-day outbound needs about 660 t of propellant, well inside the tank, but it would enter Mars's atmosphere at about 8.6 km/s, against the 6.6 km/s the landers are built for, and braking to that speed first would take more than the tank holds; a 180-day return would enter Earth's atmosphere at about 14.3 km/s ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#no-case-fits-under-the-limit)). The alternative is an explicit decision to fly above the limit. The National Academies found that a Mars mission is likely to exceed the standard "by up to 150 percent" and that NASA would need waivers for the mission and for each astronaut [22]. The crew gate therefore includes a documented dose projection for each crew member and a waiver each has accepted in writing (chapter 12).

> Assumption: The agency or company flying the crew accepts, under individual waivers signed before launch, a mission dose of up to about 1,600 mSv, which is [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#the-waiver-needs-allowances-above-the-cases)'s baseline case plus its allowances and also covers the top of the range above. The waivers also cover a missed return window, up to about 1,900 mSv. If the flyer does not accept these doses, the first crew waits for faster transits, flown by vehicles that can enter at their arrival speeds, and the uncrewed colony keeps running and growing in the meantime.

## Crew gate: six site conditions before launch
<a id="crew-gate"></a>

The crew departs only when all of the following are true at the launch decision, about three months before the November 2043 window, apart from the launch-day checks below:

1. Firm fission power ≥200 kWe (three reactors, so that 200 kWe survives the loss of any one) has run for ≥12 months.
2. Habitat held at its operating atmosphere (70 kPa, 26.5% O₂) for ≥12 months with leak rate within specification (≤1 kg per sol).
3. MAV fully loaded and the lifeboat reserve in tanks, both verified by independent sensors.
4. ERV in Mars orbit, fueled, and checked out with all its cryocoolers working, carrying an entry capsule qualified for the chosen return's Earth entry speed (gate G4 criterion 9), and the crew pad finished and inspected, its recipe tested by the support lander's September 2042 landing on a site built the same way ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)).
5. Consumables at the levels in the table above.
6. Autonomy proven in three parts, all without Earth intervention: (a) at least 120 consecutive days with no commands from Earth, including a solar conjunction; (b) a storm-mode run of at least 30 days with solar output curtailed to the design storm's output, 5–11 kW per 400 kW field (20–35% of the planned clear-sky output, set in kilowatts because a higher tracking gain than planned would make a percentage too mild), and deferrable loads shed; and (c) operation through any regional or global dust event that does occur.

Three items fly with the crew, so the launch decision reads their loading plan, and a check aboard the crew vehicle before the November 2043 launch confirms them: the crew half of the food (condition 5), and the pharmacy and the suit seals and batteries (chapter 12's criteria 10 and 12).

A natural τ≈5 storm may not come before 2043, so part (b) simulates the design storm; [Appendix A](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md) carries that gap as risk [R39](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r39). [Chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md) turns these criteria into dated milestones, and [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md) adds human criteria 7–15: radiation dose and waivers, medical capability, behavioral health, and planetary protection.

## Seven assumptions the budgets rest on
<a id="key-assumptions"></a>

- A 100 kWe-class fission unit qualified for Mars by 2034, building on the 20 kWe-class LR-1 planned for the Moon in 2030; the reactor is on the critical path ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md), [R16](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r16))
- Ship-to-ship propellant transfer in Earth orbit demonstrated before 2033, in time for the pathfinder's tanker flights, and routine by the Wave 1 gate in March 2035 ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md), [R08](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r08)); as of September 2026 it has not flown
- Heavy-lander EDL demonstrated before gate G1, ideally by the 2033 pathfinder, otherwise by another program's lander (chapter 4, [R14](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r14))
- Ice within reach of Rodwell wells at the chosen site ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md), [R05](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r05), [R36](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r36)), and no external halt of the wells after a life detection or a Special Region ruling ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md), [R40](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r40))
- Autonomy at A3 or better by Wave 1 ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md), [R17](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r17))
- The one open power case, a storm with no reactor running before reactor 2 is commissioned in January 2039 (in Wave 1's first weeks, before reactor 1 starts, or after a reactor trip), accepted with its mitigations ([R27](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r27))
- Crew acceptance of a mission radiation dose above NASA's 600 mSv career limit under individual waivers, unless faster transits become available ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md), [R19](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r19))

Tests, surveys, and reviews can settle the first five before the crew gate. The plan carries the last two as accepted risks.

## What would change this conclusion

A reactor not qualified for Mars by 2034 would move Wave 1 to 2037 and the crew to about April 2048, because the heavier 20 kWe-class fallback does not fit Wave 1's margin of about 17 t per lander ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). A lander that delivers much less than ~100 t would first eat the 30–40 t margins of Waves 2 and 3. And Rodwells that yield far below their modeled 0.38 m³/day would make water the constraint, starting with the ~650 t habitat shield; [chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md) sets out where the ice is and what the colony does if it underdelivers.
