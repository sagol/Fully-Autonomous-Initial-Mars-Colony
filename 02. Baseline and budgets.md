# 2. Baseline and budgets

*Last reviewed: September 26, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, 2041.* The Orchestrator keeps one ledger above all others. Every hour it adds what the reactors made, subtracts what the wells, electrolyzers, printers, and heaters took, and checks the balance against a single question: if Earth went silent tomorrow and the sky stayed brown for four months, would everything that matters still be warm, pressurized, and fueled? Since the second reactor finished commissioning in January 2039, the answer has been yes.

This chapter is that ledger, written down before the first lander leaves Earth. It holds the book's launch dates, power levels, tonnages, and physical constants, and the other chapters link here instead of restating them. Every figure must close with the colony running at autonomy level A3, which [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md) defines: a robot fleet that runs the site for at least 120 days without contact and recovers from anticipated faults on its own. The wave success criteria and the crew gate assume A3 and nothing more, and higher levels change only schedules and margins.

In brief: the colony is built in three cargo waves launched in the 2035, 2037, and 2039 windows. By early 2041, once reactor 3 finishes commissioning, it has 200 kWe of firm fission power, the output it can count on with any one reactor down. It fuels a small Mars ascent vehicle (MAV) soon after the vehicle lands in September 2040, banks a second ascent load as a lifeboat reserve by mid-2042, and passes its crew gate in time for a crew departure in November 2043. Two limits stay open. Before reactor 2 is commissioned in January 2039, a reactor trip during a planet-wide dust storm would leave the critical loads short of power, a risk the plan accepts with mitigations. And every transit option gives the crew more radiation than NASA's 600 mSv career limit, so the first crew flies above it under individual waivers.

## Reference site: Arcadia Planitia at 42°N
<a id="reference-site"></a>

The reference site is in Arcadia Planitia, a smooth northern plain with buried water ice near the surface, and the working point is 42°N, 192°E, at about −3.5 km relative to the Mars datum. Deuteronilus Mensae, known only from orbit, is the backup region. The optional 2033 pathfinder would drill the primary site and leave time to retarget Wave 1; without it, Wave 1 commits to an undrilled site ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md#the-backup-region-and-what-the-pathfinder-buys)). "Within reach" of the wells means the top of the ice lies no more than 5 m down and the ice is at least 10 m thick (chapter 3).

The landing field sits at least 2 km from the habitat, the reactor field, and the wells. Its pads lie about 1 km apart, the estimated reach of landing ejecta, and berms block the line of sight between pads and toward the rest of the site. The reactor field lies about 1 km from the habitat, beside the well field, so the heat lines stay short ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md), [chapter 5](05.%20Power.md), and [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)).

> Assumption: The site holds at least 3,000 t of mapped, drilled ice within 5 km of the landing field, confirmed before Wave 2 commits.

## Launch windows: the expensive years cost tanker flights
<a id="launch-opportunities"></a>

A Mars window opens roughly every 26 months, but the calendar does not cooperate with even-numbered years. The minimum-energy departures below come from NASA's *Interplanetary Mission Design Handbook* [2]. C3, the characteristic energy, is the launch energy per unit mass that the departing stage must supply; for a fully fueled vehicle, higher C3 means less payload.

| 2033 | Apr 28, 2033 | 7.8 | Jan 2034 | Optional pathfinder: one heavy lander tests entry, descent, and landing (EDL) and surveys the site |
| **2035** | **Jun 23, 2035** | **10.2** | **Jan 2036** | **Wave 1: power, water, oxygen, robots** |
| **2037** | **Sep 7, 2037** | **14.8** | **Oct 2038** | **Wave 2: second reactor, habitat, propellant plant** |
| **2039** | **Sep 28, 2039** | **12.2** | **Sep 2040** | **Wave 3: MAV, third reactor, outfitting** |
| 2041 | Oct 19, 2041 | 9.8 | Sep 2042 | Earth return vehicle to Mars orbit; spares |
| **2043** | **Nov 15, 2043** | **9.0** | **Sep 2044** | **First crew** |
| 2045/46 | Jan 22, 2046 | 8.6 | Dec 2046 | Crew rotation and expansion; at least 2 t of food, which covers a missed return window (chapter 4) |
| 2048 | ~Apr 4, 2048 | ~7.9 | ~Jan 2049 | Expansion (date from a two-body calculation, beyond the handbook) |

The 2037 and 2039 windows are the most expensive of the period, with C3 50–90% higher than in 2033. A cargo lander takes on only the propellant its transfer needs, far less than its tanks hold, so the higher energy leaves the landed payload unchanged and adds tanker flights: a ninth per lander in 2037 and 2039, whose slow transfers also lose the most landing propellant to boil-off on the way ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)). The cheap 2033 window suits a pathfinder if the vehicles are ready in time.

> Assumption: Waves launch at the optimum dates above. Real launch periods span 3–6 weeks around each optimum [2], which is enough to launch several landers per window from multiple pads.

SpaceX gives the current (V3) ship a propellant capacity of about 1,600 t but no longer states a payload to the Mars surface [8].

## Power budget: fission for firm power, solar for the rest
<a id="power-budget"></a>

Power is the colony's master resource: water, oxygen, propellant, heat, construction, and communications all draw from it.

The reactor is a 100 kWe-class closed-Brayton unit of about 15 t, and it is on the critical path: Wave 1 needs a Mars unit qualified by 2034, without waiting for a 100 kWe unit to fly on the Moon. The 15 t figure is the lander capacity NASA's August 2025 directive assumed for a lunar reactor of at least 100 kWe [9]. The nearest hardware is NASA's first lunar unit, Lunar Reactor-1 (LR-1), which is to land on the Moon in 2030 [19]. Its draft procurement of August 30, 2026 [20] reportedly asks for at least 20 kWe [15], and 100 kWe-class reactors are a 2030s goal [16]. LR-1 has no hardware contract yet and proves only the design lineage ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)).

> Assumption: A 100 kWe-class reactor of about 15 t is qualified for Mars in time for the June 2035 launch. If it is not, Wave 1 needs several 20 kWe-class units instead, at a higher mass per kilowatt (chapter 5).

### Power by epoch: no firm power until January 2039
<a id="firm-power"></a>

**Firm power** is what the colony can count on with its largest generator out of service. With one reactor it is zero, with two it is 100 kWe, and with three it is 200 kWe. Reactor 1 delivers first power about April 2036 and its full 100 kWe by about June 2036 (chapter 5). Each later reactor needs several months of commissioning after it lands, so firm power reaches 100 kWe in January 2039, after Wave 2, and 200 kWe in early January 2041, after Wave 3 and the December 2040 conjunction, because starting a reactor needs Earth's approval ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md)).

| Epoch | Reactors installed | Installed fission | Firm fission | Solar field (sol-average) | Storage |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Jan 2036–Jan 2039 | 1 | 100 kWe | 0 | 30–40 kW | Batteries ~1 MWh |
| Jan 2039–Jan 2041 | 2 | 200 kWe | 100 kWe | 60–80 kW | Batteries ~2 MWh |
| Jan 2041 onward | 3 | 300 kWe | 200 kWe | 60–80 kW | Batteries ~2 MWh |

> Assumption: Solar fields deliver a clear-sky sol-average of 7–10% of nameplate (air mass zero) and 20–35% of that at τ≈5, so each 30–40 kW needs about 400 kW of nameplate (chapter 5).

Construction soaks up whatever headroom the other loads leave: about 70–100 kW in the first epoch whenever the reactor runs, enough to build Wave 2's landing pads well ahead of its arrival (chapter 5 and [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)).

### Loads by epoch: the budget closes in all but one case
<a id="loads-by-epoch"></a>

Loads are average electric power over a sol, in kW. "Critical" loads must run through a reactor trip and a 120-day storm. "Deferrable" loads stop when power is short.

| Load | Class | 2036–2038 | 2039–2040 | 2041–crew | Basis |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Oxygen from CO₂ (solid oxide electrolysis, 2 kg/h) | Deferrable | 25–34 | 25–34 | 25–34 | Full-scale MOXIE (Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment) successor needs ≥20 kW; 34 kW for 1.97 kg/h [10], [11] |
| Methane and oxygen propellant plant (MAV scale) | Deferrable | 0 | 35–52 | 35–52 | 7.0 t CH₄ + 22.7 t O₂ in 480 days of running [11], about 565 at 85% availability |
| Water wells (pumps; heat from reactor waste heat) | Critical | 2 | 4 | 5 | ~10 kWt heat per well [12]; see Assumption below |
| Construction and robots (sintering, excavation, charging) | Deferrable | 20–30 (70–100 when spare) | 30–40 | 30–40 | Chapter 8 |
| Habitat thermal control and pressure hold | Critical | 0 | 5–10 | 10–15 | ~5 kW thermal control for an Ice Home-class habitat [13]; atmosphere 70 kPa, 26.5% O₂ (chapter 7) |
| Life support systems test and later operation | Critical | 0 | 3 | 10 | Chapter 6 |
| Greenhouse (lighting, climate) | Deferrable | 0 | 5–10 | 15–20 | ~90–115 m² lit from 2040; 20–25% of crew calories (chapter 9) |
| Communications | Critical | 2 | 3 | 3 | Chapter 10 |
| Keep-alive, avionics, cryogenic tank cooling | Critical | 5–10 | 8–12 | 10–15 | Chapters 5 and 6 |
| **Total** | | **54–78** | **118–168** | **143–194** | |
| **Critical subtotal** | | **9–14** | **23–32** | **38–48** | |

Until reactor 1 reaches power in April 2036, Wave 1 lives on 30–40 kW of solar: keep-alive, communications, and unloading come first, and the oxygen line runs only at low duty for commissioning. After that, in the first epoch the single reactor carries the full load; if it trips in clear weather, the critical 9–14 kW runs on solar and batteries while robots hibernate. Between 2039 and 2040 the full load (up to 168 kW) needs both reactors, but if one trips, the other still covers the critical 23–32 kW and the propellant plant; construction and the oxygen line wait. From early 2041, once reactor 3 is running, even the worst case (194 kW) fits with margin, and the critical subtotal sits far below the 200 kWe firm level.

The keep-alive line assumes [chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)'s tank insulation of 1–2 W/m², which that chapter labels optimistic. At NASA's figure of about 10 W/m² for insulated tanks on the Mars surface [23], storage cooling could reach about 50 kWe. The critical load in 2039–2040 would then be about 70–80 kW, still inside the 100 kWe firm level with one reactor out.

> Assumption: Rodwell water wells take their melting heat (~10 kWt per well) from the reactors' waste heat, which a 100 kWe Brayton unit rejects at several hundred kWt. Chapter 5 checks the heat-transport distance and temperature.

### The storm case: one reactor trip the budget cannot cover
<a id="the-storm-case"></a>

In a 120-day storm at τ≈5, solar output drops to 20–35% of clear-sky levels, a loss of 20–60 kW depending on epoch. Fission is unaffected, apart from dust on its radiators. The colony curtails deferrable loads: construction first, then the oxygen line, then the greenhouse. From January 2039, once reactor 2 has finished commissioning, critical loads never depend on solar power.

Storage cannot stand in for a reactor through a storm. The batteries are sized to smooth loads over minutes to hours. This plan rejects storing energy as methane and oxygen: by this book's estimate (chapter 5) it returns only about 16–29% of the input energy, and every tonne burned for power is a tonne the ascent vehicle loses.

Before reactor 2 is commissioned, the colony has one reactor and zero firm power, and Wave 1 has landed in the dust season. If the reactor trips during a τ≈5 storm, solar gives 6–14 kW against a 9–14 kW critical load, and ~1 MWh of batteries covers the shortfall for only days. The plan accepts this as risk [R27](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r27), with three mitigations: critical loads shed to a survival floor, reactor restart as the top priority, and spares on site for the most likely causes of a trip (chapter 5). The exposure ends when reactor 2 is commissioned in January 2039.

## Propellant and consumables: two ascent loads and 1,000 days of stores
<a id="propellant-and-consumables-at-the-crew-gate"></a>

Crew return uses a small MAV that lifts the crew to Mars orbit, where they transfer to an ERV that arrived fueled in 2042. The MAV is sized like NASA's four-crew ascent vehicle from its Evolvable Mars Campaign studies: about 7.0 t of methane and 22.7 t of oxygen per full load, with a dry mass of about 9.4 t in the 2015 design [11], [14]. This plan uses that design and allocates 15 t landed, which covers ground-support equipment and growth. Dry-mass growth costs propellant: each added tonne needs about 3.2 t more per load, or about 50 more days of plant time ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)).

| Stockpile | Quantity | Status required at crew departure (Nov 2043) |
|---|---:|---|
| MAV load 1 (in vehicle) | 7.0 t CH₄ + 22.7 t O₂ | Loaded, verified, held at temperature ≥6 months (in the baseline, about three years) |
| Lifeboat reserve (MAV load 2) | 7.0 t CH₄ + 22.7 t O₂ | In storage tanks |
| Breathing oxygen reserve (4 crew, ~1,000 days: 3.4 t metabolic at 0.84 kg/person/day, plus leakage, airlock losses, and margin) | ~5 t | In storage |
| Buffer gas (N₂ and Ar captured from the atmosphere) | ≥2 t | In storage |
| Water reserve | ≥100 t | In storage |
| Shelf-stable food (4 crew × 1,000 days × 1.83 kg) | ~7.3 t | Half landed by the 2041 support flight and inspected on Mars before the launch decision, which alone covers about 500 days, more than the 410-day stay; half loaded aboard the crew vehicle and verified before launch; transit food travels in the crew vehicle, outside this reserve (chapter 9) |

Surface stocks are sized for about 1,000 days, more than twice the planned 410-day stay, as margin against a delayed departure; the plants can also make more oxygen and water. A full missed return window stretches the stay to about 1,190 days, and a food flight in the January 2046 window covers the difference ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)). The pharmacy is the exception: it flies with the crew, because 87% of medications flown on the International Space Station have labeled shelf lives under 24 months [21].

The propellant plant needs 480 days of running per MAV load at 35–52 kW [11], about 565 days at the 85% availability this book plans on (chapter 13). It lands with its cryogenic storage tanks in Wave 2 in October 2038 and, after about six months of commissioning, starts producing in April 2039. Load 1 completes by about late October 2040, a month after the MAV lands in September 2040; load 2, the lifeboat reserve, completes by about May 2042. That leaves about 15 months before the August 2043 launch decision to absorb a slow start, a repair, or a lower rate. The oxygen line makes ~17.5 t of O₂ per Earth year at full rate from about June 2036, once reactor 1 reaches 100 kWe, then drops to a low duty cycle once its tanks are full ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)). The load table still carries its full 25–34 kW. Methane, and the water and power behind it, sets the pace.

A full refill of a V3 ship on Mars, about 1,600 t [8], would be roughly 350 t of methane and 1,250 t of oxygen at a 3.6:1 ratio. By this book's calculation it would need about 1.1–1.6 MW of continuous ISRU power for a full synod. Such a refill is outside this baseline and belongs to the expansion era ([chapter 15](15.%20Terraforming%20and%20the%20long%20view.md)).

## Crew mission and radiation dose: above the career limit in every case
<a id="crew-mission-and-radiation-dose"></a>

The first crew spends about 305 days outbound on the November 2043 Type II transfer, about 410 days on the surface, and 220–235 days returning after a low-energy departure in early November 2045: about 940–950 days in all ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md) and [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md)). The ERV waits in Mars orbit for about 3.2 years. With this chapter's dose constants, the mission gives each crew member an effective dose of about 810–1,260 mSv, depending on transit shielding and on the habitat's water shell (chapter 12 shows the cases). NASA's career limit is 600 mSv [17].

No shielding case in this plan meets that limit. Even with the habitat's water shell and a crew vehicle held to NASA's 1.3 mSv/day design limit, the transits would have to last about 180 days each way, which chemical propulsion cannot deliver in these windows. The alternative is an explicit decision to fly above the limit. The National Academies found that a Mars mission is likely to exceed the standard "by up to 150 percent" and that NASA would need waivers for the mission and for each astronaut [22]. The crew gate therefore includes a documented dose projection for each crew member and a waiver each has accepted in writing (chapter 12).

> Assumption: The agency or company flying the crew accepts a mission dose of up to about 1,260 mSv, the top of the range above, under individual waivers. If it does not, the first crew waits for faster transit propulsion, and the uncrewed colony keeps running and growing in the meantime.

## Crew gate: six site conditions before launch
<a id="crew-gate"></a>

The crew departs only when all of the following are true at the launch decision, about three months before the November 2043 window:

1. Firm fission power ≥200 kWe (three reactors, so that 200 kWe survives the loss of any one) has run for ≥12 months.
2. Habitat held at its operating atmosphere (70 kPa, 26.5% O₂) for ≥12 months with leak rate within specification (≤1 kg per sol).
3. MAV fully loaded and the lifeboat reserve in tanks, both verified by independent sensors.
4. ERV in Mars orbit, fueled, and checked out, and the crew pad finished and inspected, its recipe tested by the support lander's September 2042 landing on a site built the same way ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)).
5. Consumables at the levels in the table above.
6. Autonomy proven in three parts, all without Earth intervention: (a) at least 120 consecutive days with no commands from Earth, including a solar conjunction; (b) a storm-mode run of at least 30 days with solar output curtailed to design-storm levels (20–35% of clear sky) and deferrable loads shed; and (c) operation through any regional or global dust event that does occur.

A natural τ≈5 storm may not come before 2043, so part (b) simulates the design storm; [Appendix A](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md) carries that gap as risk [R39](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r39). [Chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md) turns these criteria into dated milestones, and [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md) adds human criteria 7–15: radiation dose and waivers, medical capability, behavioral health, and planetary protection.

## Seven assumptions the budgets rest on
<a id="key-assumptions"></a>

- A 100 kWe-class fission unit qualified for Mars by 2034, building on the 20 kWe-class LR-1 planned for the Moon in 2030; the reactor is on the critical path ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md), [R16](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r16))
- Ship-to-ship propellant transfer in Earth orbit demonstrated before 2033 ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md), [R08](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r08)); as of September 2026 it has not flown
- Heavy-lander EDL demonstrated, ideally by the 2033 pathfinder (chapter 4, [R14](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r14))
- Ice within reach of Rodwell wells at the chosen site ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md), [R05](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r05), [R36](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r36)), and no external halt of the wells after a life detection or a Special Region ruling ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md), [R40](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r40))
- Autonomy at A3 or better by Wave 1 ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md), [R17](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r17))
- The one open power case, a reactor trip during a storm before reactor 2 is commissioned in January 2039, accepted with its mitigations ([R27](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r27))
- Crew acceptance of a mission radiation dose above NASA's 600 mSv career limit under individual waivers, unless faster transits become available ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md), [R19](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r19))

Tests, surveys, and reviews can settle the first five before the crew gate. The plan carries the last two as accepted risks.

## What would change this conclusion

A reactor not qualified for Mars by 2034 would force the heavier 20 kWe-class fallback into Wave 1, whose margin is about 17 t per lander. A lander that delivers much less than ~100 t would first eat the 30–40 t margins of Waves 2 and 3. And Rodwells that yield far below their modeled 0.38 m³/day would make water the constraint, starting with the ~650 t habitat shield; [chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md) sets out where the ice is and what the colony does if it underdelivers.
