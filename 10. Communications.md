# 10. Communications

*Last reviewed: September 28, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, February 2043.* The Sun sits almost exactly between Mars and Earth, and for about four weeks no command from Earth can be trusted. The Orchestrator does not wait. It froze new kinds of work a month before the moratorium, closed the uplink queue two weeks ago after Earth's last command load, and has since run the plan Earth gave it plus contingencies of its own. The first ascent vehicle load has sat cold and stable in its tanks for more than two years, and the lifeboat load for nine months. Each sol the colony's relay overhead still accepts bundles for Earth, stamps them, and holds them. When the Sun moves clear, the relay will send the summary first: nothing broke that the colony could not fix.

A colony built before people arrive is run by machines that cannot phone home in time. Light takes 3.0–22.3 minutes to cross between Mars and Earth one way, and once every 26 months or so the Sun blocks usable contact for two to four weeks. Every other chapter of this book assumes robots, reactors, and chemical plants will work through those gaps. This chapter covers how data moves when it can, what happens when it cannot, and who owns each link in the chain from a sensor on the surface to an engineer on Earth.

Earth engineers see only what the colony reports. The crew gate in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) therefore depends on a data path that can carry independent sensor proof that the habitat has held pressure and the ascent vehicle still holds its propellant.

In brief: the colony rides on NASA's Mars Telecommunications Network, expected at Mars around 2030, or about 2032 if it misses its first launch window, plus whatever survives of today's Mars Relay Network. The new network was funded in 2025 and awarded in September 2026, and work on it is suspended under a bid protest. From the 2037 window onward the colony adds its own areostationary relays as gap fillers and keeps an independent direct-to-Earth terminal. Delay- and disruption-tolerant networking (DTN) runs end to end. The colony is built to run for at least 120 days with no commands from Earth while its reports keep flowing home, and its data budget closes on radio hardware of the class already flying at Mars.

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) fixes most of the numbers this chapter uses. A round trip of 6–45 minutes rules out teleoperation (autonomy level A0, as [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md) defines the levels), and each conjunction brings the command moratorium described below. Crew gate criterion 6 asks for 120 consecutive days with no commands from Earth, including a conjunction. Communications is a critical load of 2 kW before October 2038 and 3 kW after, and must run through a reactor trip and the 120-day storm. The link must be proven by each wave's landing, from Wave 1 in January 2036 to the crew in September 2044.

> Assumption: Chapter 2 sets no data volume, so this book assumes the colony needs an Earth-bound data volume, averaged over a synodic cycle, of at least 5 Gb per sol in Wave 1, 50 Gb per sol after Wave 2, 100 Gb per sol after Wave 3, and 200 Gb per sol once the crew is present, with a guaranteed low-rate path (at least 1 kb/s each way) on every sol outside conjunction. In phase F2 the direct terminal alone gives one contact of 6–8 hours a sol, set by its daily track and by Earth's time above the horizon (below), and leaves gaps of 17–18 hours, which a colony built to run 120 days without commands can bear. Relay 1 in F3 cuts the gaps to about 8 hours a day, and from F4 the three paths' 24 daily track-hours, staggered and each relay's placed clear of the up to 1.3 hours a sol when Mars hides Earth from it, keep a path open at all hours. Both need the relays to take the track time the direct terminal cannot use. On about one sol in five it sees Earth for less than 8 hours, down to 6.2, and then relay 1's daily track stretches to about 10 hours in F3, each relay's to about 9 in F4, and in the crew era the 34 m relay track to about 10, beside the 8-hour 70 m-class track (this book's calculation). The basis is construction imagery, inspection video, and daily synchronization of the colony's engineering models with their copies on Earth. For scale, the Mars Relay Network's per-orbiter averages for February–May 2026 sum to about 3.2 Gb per day from all surface users combined [2].

The network grows in seven phases that track the waves: Earth-side preparation (F0), the optional pathfinder (F1), Waves 1, 2, and 3 (F2–F4, the last two adding the colony's relays), the crew era (F5), and expansion after 2046 (F6). The [phase table](#seven-phases-grow-the-network-with-the-waves) near the end of the chapter gives each phase's data target and exit criterion.

## Distance and the Sun set the limits

Light time follows from geometry: 3.0 minutes at the closest approach of about 54.6 million km [1] and 22.3 minutes at about 401 million km, with the Sun between the planets, cycling with the synodic period of 779.9 days. A question sent from Earth near conjunction gets its answer about 45 minutes later.

Data rate falls with the square of distance. A link that closes at a given rate near closest approach delivers about 1/54 of that rate at maximum distance, a loss of 17 dB. The Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO), with a 3 m high-gain antenna and a 100 W X-band amplifier, was designed to send 3–4 Mb/s at close range and at least 500 kb/s at 400 million km to a 34 m Deep Space Network antenna [3]. This chapter uses MRO as the yardstick for every Earth link the colony owns.

When Mars passes behind the Sun, hot ionized gas from the solar corona can corrupt radio signals, so mission teams stop sending commands. Downlink mostly continues, and the spacecraft can still report their health, apart from about two days of total blackout when Mars is directly behind the Sun [4]. The moratorium length depends on the geometry of each event and on the radio band:

The conjunctions that matter to this plan fall on about September 24, 2036, November 1, 2038, December 18, 2040, February 21, 2043, and May 2, 2045. These dates come from a two-body calculation with the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) approximate planetary elements, which reproduces the 2019–2026 conjunctions to within a day or two. In that calculation the Sun-Earth-Mars angle stays below 2° for 10–17 days per event and below 3° for 17–26 days.

> Assumption: The colony sizes each conjunction's command moratorium from its own geometry: the days the Sun-Earth-Mars angle stays below 3°, the span Perseverance's 19 days covered in 2021, plus about two days, and never less than 21 days. The other moratoria on record track the shorter span below about 2°. That gives 21 days in 2036 and 2038, 24 in 2040, and 28 in 2043 and 2045, when the angle stays below 3° for about 26 days (this book's calculation). A 30-day freeze on new activity types precedes each moratorium, so a conjunction removes about seven weeks of Earth-directed change, and about eight in 2043 and 2045; the freeze gives Earth time to review the conjunction plan before uplink stops. Wave 2's ships land from late August to mid-November 2038, depending on launch day ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)): about half of them inside the freeze or the moratorium around the November 2038 conjunction, and the last near the moratorium's end. Earth therefore signs reactor 2's start and the arrival and commissioning sequences of every Wave 2 lander and of relay 1 as a standing approval before the freeze begins in late September 2038 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). Wave 3's last ships can land in mid-November 2040, inside the freeze before the December conjunction, and the same kind of approval covers them; reactor 3, on an early ship, reaches first power in early November 2040 under a standing approval signed before that freeze and raises its power through the conjunction.

## Today's relay network is old and shrinking

As of September 2026, almost everything surface missions send to Earth goes through orbiters: a rover talks to an orbiter overhead in the ultra-high frequency (UHF) band, and the orbiter forwards the data. NASA calls this fleet the Mars Relay Network [2]. It has four orbiters: NASA's Mars Odyssey and MRO, and Mars Express and the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter (TGO) of the European Space Agency (ESA). TGO returns the most, about 2,228 Mb per day in February–May 2026, against about 851 Mb for MRO and 107 Mb for Odyssey [2]. All of them speak the Consultative Committee for Space Data Systems (CCSDS) Proximity-1 protocol at UHF, at coded rates from 1 kb/s to 2,048 kb/s; MRO and TGO carry JPL's Electra radio [8].

The fleet is shrinking. The Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution mission (MAVEN) handled 23% of the relay volume for NASA's surface assets [9] until it went silent on December 6, 2025, and NASA declared the mission ended on June 3, 2026 [10]. Odyssey, launched in 2001, had 3.2 ± 2.0 kg (3σ) of usable propellant in 2025 [9] and is unlikely to be operating in 2035. MRO launched in 2005, and TGO's operation beyond about 2030 depends on future ESA extensions.

Pass geometry caps volume too. MRO passes over a given rover about twice per sol for about 10 minutes, so even at up to 2 Mb/s the two passes carry at most about 2.4 Gb per sol [11], under half of what this plan needs in its first wave.

## The Mars Telecommunications Network

The backbone for the 2035 colony does not yet exist. Today's orbiters relay only as a secondary function, which is why a 2021 JPL study argued for a dedicated relay network [16]. The 2025 reconciliation act, Public Law 119-21, provided $700 million for a high-performance Mars telecommunications orbiter, to be bought under a firm fixed-price contract and delivered by December 31, 2028 [12], [36]. On September 1, 2026, NASA selected Blue Origin to design, build, launch, and operate it under a contract worth up to about $700 million, with the spacecraft operational at Mars by 2030 [13]. The orbiter is a version of Blue Origin's Blue Ring platform [14].

The award is not settled. On September 11, 2026, Rocket Lab, a losing bidder, filed a protest with the Government Accountability Office (GAO). The protest triggers an automatic stay, NASA has suspended work on the contract [37], and a ruling is due by December 20, 2026, 100 days after filing [15]. NASA's release does not publish data rates, bands, or the orbit [13], so this chapter treats its performance as an assumption and leaves it out of the data budget.

> Assumption: The Mars Telecommunications Network is operational at Mars by about 2030 if it launches in the window around December 2028, whose launch period ends about when the orbiter is due for delivery, and by about 2032 if it takes the next window, whose minimum-energy path arrives in January 2032 [39]. It has Ka-band links to Earth, is compatible with Proximity-1 UHF, and supports DTN. Each colony-era wave keeps an independent path (the direct-to-Earth terminal described below) so that no single relay orbiter can end colony operations.

On schedule, the network will have five or six years at Mars before Wave 1 lands, and about four if it misses the 2028 window. Even then it is a single orbiter with an unpublished lifetime, so the colony plans paths of its own.

## The colony's own relays fill the gaps

From the 2037 window the colony launches relay orbiters of its own, so that the base always has a relay in view and does not depend on a single national orbiter.

### Areostationary orbit keeps a relay overhead

The reference orbit is **areostationary**: circular, equatorial, with a period of one sidereal sol, so the satellite hangs over one longitude. Its semimajor axis is 20,428 km, an altitude of about 17,031 km [11]. Seen from the reference site in Arcadia Planitia at about 42°N ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md)), a relay at the colony's longitude stands about 41° above the southern horizon (computed from the orbit geometry), day and night. Mars itself hides Earth from the relay for at most about 80 minutes per sol, and only in some seasons [11]. Autonomous station-keeping with model predictive control, at longitudes where the gravity field is stable, keeps the annual velocity change small [17].

A half-sol Molniya-type orbit at the critical inclination of 63.4°, or a one-sol Tundra-type orbit, would dwell longer over high latitudes. That matters only for sorties to ice fields far north of the base; the base itself is well served from the equator.

### Higher bands and dishes close the longer link

Electra reaches its best case of about 2 Mb/s on high passes, when MRO, in its 255–320 km science orbit [3], is only a few hundred kilometers away. An areostationary relay at the colony's longitude is about 18,000 km from the base, which adds about 35 dB of free-space loss and would cut that rate to roughly 0.5 kb/s with the same omnidirectional antennas (this book's calculation). Higher frequencies and directional surface antennas close that gap. The areostationary white paper's example, a 15 W X-band transmitter with a 30 cm dish pointed at a relay with a 1 m antenna, reaches about 5 Mb/s; the same geometry at Ka-band reaches about 70 Mb/s [11].

> Assumption: Each colony relay is a spacecraft of about 1 t dry with an MRO-class X-band trunk to Earth (3 m dish, 100 W amplifier), with Ka-band on the trunk as an upgrade. It also carries an X/Ka proximity payload with at least 50 Mb/s to the base; a UHF Proximity-1 radio that serves rovers and emergencies and works with every other asset at Mars; and a DTN node with at least 1 TB of storage. Capture into areostationary orbit from [chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)'s arrival speeds takes about 1.7–2.1 km/s through a low periapsis, so with storable bipropellant of about 320 s specific impulse, 42–49% of the relay's mass at Mars is capture propellant, and each relay arrives at about 2 t, or 2.3–2.7 t on monopropellant (this book's calculation). Each launches as its own spacecraft on a launcher of its own, one in the 2037 window and one in 2039, outside the tanker counts and the landed mass in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md). The dry mass is this book's estimate, since no relay has been designed, but it has close precedents. MRO, which carries the same trunk, weighed 1,031 kg dry, 139 kg of it science instruments [40], and its whole telecommunications subsystem, dish and gimbals included, weighs 108 kg [3]. A JPL design for an areostationary relay came to 817 kg dry around a 139 kg telecommunications payload, though it reached orbit by electric propulsion [41]. The Mars Telecommunications Orbiter that NASA was designing in 2004 for a 2009 launch was to weigh about 2 t at launch, "most of which would be propellant," because aerobraking helps little in reaching a high orbit [42]. At about 2 t, a relay weighs about what MRO did at launch (2,180 kg) [40]. MRO and MAVEN (2,454 kg) each flew on an Atlas V 401 [40], [43], so a relay needs a medium-lift launch of that class; the heaviest monopropellant case, about 2.7 t, would outweigh both.

Ka-band or optical crosslinks between relays would let data reach whichever relay has the best view of Earth. No Mars orbiter has flown a crosslink, so this chapter treats them as planned technology for phase F6.

## Earth's antennas are oversubscribed

On Earth, the colony's data lands at NASA's Deep Space Network (DSN). Its complexes at Goldstone in California, near Madrid, and near Canberra are spaced so that one always sees Mars, and each has a 70 m antenna and several 34 m antennas at S-, X-, and Ka-band [18]. Ka-band raises data rates over X-band for the same antenna [19], and arraying 34 m antennas adds aperture [20]. ESA's European Space Tracking (ESTRACK) network, whose 35 m antennas already return TGO's data, could be a second ground segment.

NASA's Office of Inspector General found in 2023 that demand for the DSN at times exceeded supply by up to 40%, and that internal studies project excess demand of about 50% by the 2030s, as crewed lunar missions compete for the same antennas. Upgrades are running late and over budget [21].

> Assumption: The colony contracts 8 hours a day of 34 m antenna time (DSN or ESTRACK) for each Earth path it owns, so 8 hours in phase F2, 16 in F3, and 24 from F4, with one daily 8-hour track moved to a 70 m antenna or an equivalent array of 34 m antennas in the crew era. It books the 34 m hours as a daily total and splits them among its paths, so the relays can take the hours the direct terminal loses while Earth stands low (below). It holds priority rights during the 30 days after each conjunction and during every landing. The 8 hours per path are a planning choice, and whether the networks can sell that much time is open, given the demand above; the data budget below shows what each track returns.

## A direct terminal keeps the colony in contact without relays

The base carries its own direct-to-Earth terminal, so the colony keeps a link to Earth even if every relay orbiter fails. Rovers already have this path at low rates: Curiosity's 28 cm X-band dish, driven by a 15 W amplifier, sends about 350 b/s to a 34 m antenna at maximum distance and about 7 kb/s near closest approach [35].

A fixed base can mount MRO-class hardware. On the surface that hardware faces the same free-space physics (the thin atmosphere barely affects X-band) but sees Earth only while Earth stands high enough above the local horizon. Seen from Mars, Earth's declination swings through about ±26°, so at 42°N Earth stays above 10° of elevation for 6.2–13.8 hours per sol outside conjunction. About a fifth of the time that is less than an 8-hour track, including Wave 1's first weeks and December 2044 to March 2045, while the crew is on the surface. The short spells come when Mars is nearer Earth and the link is fast, never near maximum distance (this book's calculation).

> Assumption: The Wave 1 surface terminal pairs a 3 m X/Ka dish with a 100 W X-band amplifier and tracks Earth above a 10° elevation mask, the minimum elevation MRO's link planning uses [3]. It is powered from the critical bus and counted in the 2 kW communications load.

## The data budget closes on radios of the class flying today

The data budget counts only the Earth paths the colony owns: the direct terminal and each colony relay's trunk, all with MRO-class X-band hardware.

> Assumption: Each of these paths sends 0.5 Mb/s to a 34 m antenna at maximum distance, rising with the inverse square of distance to a ceiling of 4 Mb/s, the range of MRO's design [3]. Averaged over a synodic cycle, that is about 1.8 Mb/s (this book's calculation, with circular orbits). Each path gets its own 8-hour track per day, cut short on the direct terminal when Earth is in view for less, which lowers that path's average by about 3%; from F3 the relays send in those hours instead, at the same rates, which the table below does not count. A 70 m antenna collects about 6 dB more signal than a 34 m antenna, counting only the ratio of their apertures (this book's estimate), enough to raise a path to about 2 Mb/s at maximum distance and about 3.4 Mb/s on average. The budget takes no credit for Ka-band or optical links.

One 8-hour track at 34 m then returns about 14 Gb at maximum distance, about 50 Gb on average, and up to 115 Gb near closest approach.

| F3 Wave 2 | Direct, relay 1 | 16 h at 34 m | ~29 | ~100 | ≥50 |

Every target closes on average, the crew era's with no margin. Near maximum distance, in the months around each conjunction, F3 and F4 fall to 30–45 Gb per sol, and bulk imagery waits in storage until the link improves, as DTN is designed to allow. F2's 5 Gb target holds at any distance on the direct terminal alone. The crew era needs one 70 m-class track a day, the hardest item in the budget to secure given the DSN's congestion, and it goes to a relay trunk, which sees Earth almost all sol; on the direct terminal it would leave F5 about 1 Gb per sol short. Without it, F5 averages about 150 Gb per sol; crew voice, medical, and safety traffic is small and still goes first.

## Optical links come later, as an upgrade

NASA's Deep Space Optical Communications (DSOC) experiment on the Psyche spacecraft sent ultra-high-definition video at 267 Mb/s from about 31 million km in December 2023 and downlinked data from about 494 million km, farther than Mars ever gets from Earth, in December 2024. By the time it ended on September 2, 2025, after 65 passes and 13.6 Tb received, it had shown data rates at least 10 times higher than radio systems of comparable size and power [22], [23]. Receiving took the 5.1 m Hale Telescope at Palomar Observatory, and transmitting a 3 kW laser at JPL's Table Mountain Facility [23]. Clouds stop lasers, and pointing near the Sun is hard, so optical cannot be the only path. In NASA's optical demonstrations, DTN bundles held through cloud outages were delivered complete when the link returned [24].

> Assumption: Optical terminals enter the colony's network as an addition on the second colony relay (phase F4) and become the bulk trunk only in phase F6, when Earth has at least two optical ground stations far enough apart that one is usually clear. Radio remains the path for commands and safety data.

## DTN carries data across delay
<a id="networking-across-delay-dtn"></a>

The internet's protocols assume a live end-to-end path and round trips of a fraction of a second. Neither holds between planets. **Delay- and disruption-tolerant networking** replaces them with store-and-forward: each node keeps a bundle of data in storage until the next hop is available, then sends it on.

> Assumption: Every colony asset, relay, and ground node runs DTN. No Mars asset runs DTN operationally as of September 2026 [25]; running it everywhere is a design choice, and phases F0 and F1 exist to prove it. If the pathfinder does not fly, DTN first runs on Mars over Wave 1's direct link, and phase F2's exit, which gate G2 reads, carries the proof.

DTN does have a flight record in Earth orbit. NASA's Laser Communications Relay Demonstration (LCRD) and the ILLUMA-T terminal on the International Space Station exchanged DTN traffic over an optical relay in 2023–2024, using the older Bundle Protocol version 6 (BPv6), the current version 7, and its security extension, Bundle Protocol Security (BPSec) [24], [33]. In 2024 NASA's PACE Earth-science satellite became the first operational science mission to use DTN [34].

The core standards exist; the space-agency profiles for security and the rules for managing remote nodes are still drafts. In the table, a CCSDS "Blue Book" is a published standard, and Red and Pink Books are drafts under review.

| Standard | What it does | Status (Sep 2026) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Request for Comments (RFC) 9171, Bundle Protocol version 7 (BPv7) | Bundle format and forwarding | Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) Proposed Standard, Jan 2022 | [26] |
| RFC 9172, Bundle Protocol Security (BPSec) | Integrity and confidentiality blocks | IETF Proposed Standard, Jan 2022 | [27] |
| CCSDS 734.3-B-1, Schedule-Aware Bundle Routing (SABR) | Contact graph routing | CCSDS Blue Book, 2019 | [28] |
| CCSDS 734.2-B-1, Bundle Protocol | CCSDS profile of the bundle protocol | Blue Book, Sep 2015, based on the older BPv6; BPv7 revision a draft Pink Book since Apr 2023 | [29], [38] |
| CCSDS 734.5-R-2, BPSec | CCSDS profile of BPSec | Draft Red Book, Sep 2023 | [30] |
| RFC 9675, DTN Management Architecture (DTNMA) | How to manage nodes that cannot be reached in real time | IETF Informational, Nov 2024; management protocol still a draft | [31], [32] |

Priority is local policy. The older Bundle Protocol, RFC 5050, carried bulk, normal, and expedited classes in its header. BPv7 removed those quality-of-service markings (RFC 9171, Appendix A) [26]. The colony therefore marks priority with an extension block, as NASA's Interplanetary Overlay Network (ION) software does, and each node enforces its own queue policy. Safety events and health summaries go first, then command acknowledgments, then engineering data, then imagery and bulk archives.

Routing uses the contact plan, because RFC 9171 does not define routing. A contact plan is a schedule of which nodes can talk to which, when, and at what rate, computed from orbits, antenna schedules, and the Sun. Schedule-Aware Bundle Routing (SABR), the CCSDS standard form of contact graph routing, picks a path through it [28].

Security is end to end. BPSec integrity blocks let the colony reject any command bundle whose origin it cannot verify, and confidentiality blocks protect medical and crew data [27]. The key-management scheme across NASA, ESA, and commercial relays is not yet defined, so the colony carries its own keys, loaded before launch. In normal operations it rotates them over DTN; before a run without uplink it pre-positions enough keys to cover the whole 120 days.

## Every surface node stores and forwards

On the ground, the colony is an industrial campus a few kilometers across ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#reference-site) fixes the layout).

> Assumption: A buried fiber backbone runs in the power line's trenches ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)) between the reactors, the plants, the habitat, the landing field, and the antenna field. It has one route: a second trench would cost about 2,400 t of digging, about 10 MWh, and about three months of four excavators in the first epoch, whose berm schedule has no slack (this book's estimate from chapter 8's figures). A cut therefore falls back to radio, so that no site is isolated: every fixed site carries a point-to-point radio link on a mast above the berms, at a lower rate, and a UHF radio able to reach the colony relays directly, within the communications load. Mobile robots use short-range wireless based on commercial standards, with a mesh fallback.

Safety-critical controllers, including reactor protection, habitat pressure control, and propellant tank venting, run on their own hard-wired loops and do not depend on the backbone or on the Orchestrator, as [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md) requires. The network reports their status; their trip signals travel on the hard-wired loops.

A robot that drives out of range keeps its data until it drives back, because every node on the surface network runs DTN. BPv7 bundles carry a creation time and a lifetime [26]. Expiry is simplest when nodes agree on the time, so the colony keeps every node on one clock, disciplined by the relays and by Earth when Earth is in view. A node that loses sync can still send: it sets the creation time to zero and adds a Bundle Age block [26].

## Operations without uplink

The colony treats each conjunction as a scheduled drill. Before it, Earth uploads the last approved plan and the Orchestrator adds its own contingency branches, which Earth reviews before the freeze. During it, the colony keeps sending: most downlink still works [4], so Earth watches health data without replying. After it, the relays send summaries and anomaly reports first and bulk data later.

Criterion 6's 120-day run is longer, and the colony designs it for the stricter case of no uplink at all, with downlink continuing throughout. Before the run starts, Earth books 120 days of relay passes and antenna tracks against the colony's contact plan, and the colony loads the ephemerides it needs to recompute that plan on site. BPSec keys for the whole run are pre-positioned before it starts, so no key has to cross the link. If a booked track is lost to a DSN conflict, bundles wait in storage for the next one; nothing on Mars depends on the uplink returning.

By the crew launch decision, about three months before the November 2043 window, the colony will have passed four conjunctions (September 2036, November 2038, December 2040, and February 2043). The last of them falls while both ascent vehicle loads sit in cryogenic storage, so the gate tests the colony at full scale.

## What would change this conclusion

A Mars Telecommunications Network that is canceled or fails before 2035 would make the colony's own relays the backbone and move relay 1 to the 2035 window. A demonstrated optical trunk at Mars before 2037 would raise every data target by an order of magnitude.

## Autonomy

### What A3 must do

At A3, site autonomy for known work, the Orchestrator manages the contact plan: it predicts passes, requests relay and antenna time weeks ahead, and replans when a pass is missed. The same system enforces the queue policy, holds bundles through outages of four weeks or more, and delivers them in priority order. When it detects a link fault (a pointing drift, a failed amplifier, a relay in safe mode), it switches paths from a predesigned procedure. Through a conjunction or a 120-day run without uplink it follows the approved plan and only the contingency branches Earth reviewed. DTN nodes already hold and forward bundles without human help, and conjunction plans are standard practice [4], [6].

### What A4 adds

At A4 the colony handles link faults nobody wrote a procedure for, such as a relay that has lost an axis of attitude control, and Earth engineers review its decisions after the fact instead of approving them in advance. The 45-minute round trip then affects only how soon Earth learns what happened.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could design and print replacement antennas, rewrite the routing policy, and argue for a different relay architecture with evidence Earth has not seen. BPSec keys, safety interlocks, and the reactor trip chain stay under independent control at every level.

## Risks

Delay and conjunction are certain and designed for. What remains is a fault during the moratorium that needs expertise the Orchestrator lacks, and criterion 6 tests for it ([R06](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r06)).

The 16–24 daily track-hours the data budget needs must be contracted early, because DSN demand is projected to exceed supply by about 50% in the 2030s [21]. ESTRACK is the second ground segment ([R07](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r07)).

The Mars Telecommunications Network could be late or fail, and the protest has already stopped work, which stays stopped unless NASA overrides the stay [15], [37]. If the orbiter is not at Mars by January 2036, Wave 1 runs on the direct terminal alone until relay 1 arrives, which still meets F2's exit and gate G2 ([R18](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r18)). Until relay 2 follows, losing relay 1 in F3 would leave the direct terminal and the national relay, an exposure F4 exists to close ([R18](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r18)).

A forged command bundle could do more harm than a lost one. BPSec and the independent safety interlocks bound the damage, but no one has yet defined key management across agencies ([R31](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r31)).

The hard-wired safety loops and a UHF radio at every fixed site limit how far a software fault in the network could cascade across the fleet ([R12](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r12)).

## Open questions

- What orbit, links, and lifetime will the Mars Telecommunications Network have, and will it outlast 2045?
- Who holds the keys when NASA, ESA, commercial relays, and a colony operator all carry each other's traffic?
- How much DSN or ESTRACK time, including a daily 70 m-class track in the crew era, can a nonagency colony secure in the 2030s and 2040s?
- Is a conjunction relay worth its cost once the colony has proven it can run 120 days without uplink?
