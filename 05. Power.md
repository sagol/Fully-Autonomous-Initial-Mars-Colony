# 5. Power

*Last reviewed: September 28, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, 2039.* The sky has been the color of weak tea for 41 sols, and the arrays turn toward a sun they can barely see, making a quarter of their spring output. Nine sols ago the second reactor shut itself down over a coolant-loop alarm. The Orchestrator paused the sintering robots within the hour and brought the oxygen stacks down to cold standby by evening; two robots are now at the reactor with a replacement pump. The wells, the habitat test loop, and the propellant plant never noticed. A kilometer north of the habitat shells, the first reactor runs at full power behind its berm, sending electricity down buried cables and heat into the ice wells.

The most valuable single item Wave 1 lands is a reactor ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)), because every other product depends on power. Melting water from buried ice, splitting oxygen from carbon dioxide, and making methane for the ascent vehicle all run on electricity or heat.

Mars makes that power hard to get. Sunlight at Mars is 43% of Earth's at the top of the atmosphere, the sol is 24.66 h long, and a planet-encircling dust storm can dim the surface for months.

In brief: the colony runs on 100 kWe-class fission reactors, one landed in 2036, two by 2038, and three by 2040, so firm power rises from 0 to 100 to 200 kWe, and their waste heat melts the ice in the water wells. Solar arrays carry daytime and deferrable loads, and batteries carry the critical loads for a sol or more after a trip and smooth the grid; no storage system rides through a storm. During a storm the colony sheds deferrable loads in a fixed order. From early 2039, once reactor 2 is commissioned, no critical load depends on sunlight. Before then, a storm that finds no reactor running, in Wave 1's first weeks or after a reactor trip, is the one case the power budget does not close: the colony sheds to a survival floor of a few kilowatts that storm sunlight and batteries can carry, an accepted risk ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#the-storm-case)).

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) sets the power contract for three epochs. Across them, firm fission rises from 0 to 100 to 200 kWe, total loads from 54–78 to 118–168 to 143–194 kW, and critical loads from 9–14 to 23–32 to 38–48 kW. Sol-average solar is 27–33 kW in the first epoch and 55–65 kW later, with batteries of about 1 and then 2 MWh.

**Firm power** is the power the colony can count on with its largest generator out of service. It governs safety; installed power governs production. Chapter 2 gives its timing.

Five further requirements follow from chapter 2. The system must survive the design storm, 120 days at optical depth τ≈5 [10], and supply roughly 10 kWt of heat to each Rodwell water well: 20 kWt in the first epoch, rising to 40–60 kWt by the crew gate. From early 2039 critical loads must survive the loss of any one reactor, and by the crew gate the colony must show 200 kWe of firm fission power sustained for at least 12 months. In the uncrewed single-reactor epoch, from early 2036 to early 2039, the design case is survival of the critical load: 9–14 kW of keep-alive, communications, and well freeze protection, rising to 23–32 kW with the habitat's heat and pressure hold once Wave 2 lands in October 2038.

## Why fission is the backbone

NASA chose fission for human Mars missions in its 2024 architecture review: "NASA has selected nuclear fission power as the primary surface power generation technology for crewed missions to Mars" [2]. Fission works "around the clock, and during global dust storms"; solar power "could be feasible" with dust removal, but "surface dust removal would not mitigate the problem of reduced solar availability due to suspended atmospheric dust during lengthy storms" [2].

An earlier NASA trade study put numbers on the choice: for a crewed campaign at Jezero crater, fission power generation over three expeditions came to about 9.2 t against 17.8 t for solar power with storage [10]. Solar gains toward the equator and loses away from it [10]; the colony's reference site lies near 42°N, where the buried ice is ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md)). For an uncrewed colony the argument is stronger: a solar plant that loses 65–80% of its output in a storm needs an oversized field or deep storage that robots must deploy, clean, and repair.

The main alternative is solar first. Vázquez Pombo's 2021 conceptual design starts with 380 kW of photovoltaics, 125 kW of batteries, and 125 kW of regenerative fuel cells, adding nuclear power only for later crewed stages [21]. This book reverses the order because its production schedule cannot pause for a months-long storm.

## The reactor: 100 kWe, on the critical path

### What NASA has tested and asked for, 2018–2026

The Kilopower Reactor Using Stirling Technology (KRUSTY) test in 2018 ran a 1 kWe-class reactor at about 4 kWt and 800 °C for 28 h and showed stable start-up, steady state, and passive load following [4]. With Brayton conversion now the baseline, that heritage bears on reactor physics, heat pipes, and control. In 2022 NASA's Fission Surface Power (FSP) project asked industry for 40 kWe at 120 Vdc and under 6,000 kg, with no more than 5 rem (50 mSv) per year above background at 1 km. The unit could operate "on the lander, or be transported then operated" [3]. Three teams completed design studies in 2023 [1], and in January 2025 follow-on contracts added power-conversion testbeds [27], [33]. None of this work has yet produced flight hardware.

On August 4, 2025, NASA's acting administrator raised the target to an FSP system with a "minimum 100kWe power output," closed Brayton conversion, an assumed heavy lander with "up to 15 metric tons mass available," and "readiness to launch by the first quarter of FY30" [1]. In January 2026 NASA and the Department of Energy agreed to ready a lunar surface reactor for launch by 2030 [9]. On April 14, 2026, the White House's National Science and Technology Memorandum 3 (NSTM-3) made that lunar reactor mid-power (at least 20 kWe) and scheduled high-power systems (at least 100 kWe) for the 2030s, "optimized for in-space propulsion but can also serve FSP needs" [5], [6], [32].

That first lunar unit is Lunar Reactor-1 (LR-1), due on the Moon in 2030 [31]. Its draft procurement of August 30, 2026, calls for a closed-Brayton reactor of no more than 15 t, fueled with high-assay low-enriched uranium. It must supply at least 20 kWe even after a single failure, for five years without maintenance [8], [30]. As of September 2026 LR-1 has no hardware contract. A sister project, Space Reactor-1 (SR-1) Freedom, a 20 kWe closed-Brayton reactor powering electric thrusters, targets launch in late 2028 and a Mars flyby in 2029 [7].

### What the colony assumes

The colony's reactor is a 100 kWe-class, closed-Brayton unit of about 15 t landed, matching the 2025 directive and NSTM-3's high-power class. Its nearest ancestor is LR-1, with the same conversion cycle, fuel class, and autonomy at one-fifth the power. No 100 kWe unit will fly on the Moon before Wave 1 must commit, so the reactor sits on the plan's critical path: Wave 1 needs a Mars unit qualified by 2034, built on LR-1's design lineage.

> Assumption: A 100 kWe-class closed-Brayton surface reactor, of about 15 t including lander interfaces, is qualified and adapted to Mars in time to launch in June 2035. The 15 t is the directive's lunar lander capacity, not a design mass, so each reactor lander's margin holds a growth allowance of 30%, up to about 4.5 t, which keeps a unit of up to about 19.5 t within the lander's 20 t crane and the trailer's rating ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)). Justification: NSTM-3 schedules ≥100 kWe systems for the 2030s [32], and LR-1 (2030) and SR-1 Freedom (2028) should retire much of the Brayton, fuel, and control risk at 20 kWe [7], [30]. For a design this early, NASA's Compass design team, following the AIAA mass-properties standard, rates a growth allowance and margin of 30% or more as adequate, and its 2022 study of a 40 kWe lunar reactor carried 35% [41]. NASA's Mars studies of the 2000s took their reactor straight from the lunar design with no change to the power system; what grew was a heavier on-board shield and a mobility system [42], [43], which this plan replaces with berms and the lander crane and trailer. This is the plan's most schedule-sensitive power assumption; the fallback and its mass cost are under Risks.

Adapting a lunar design to Mars means accounting for the atmosphere, higher gravity, wind, dust storms, and communication delay [2]. Hot structures designed for lunar vacuum must be checked for compatibility with CO₂, and deployed radiators carry more than twice their lunar load at 0.38 g. The colder Martian sky works the other way: NASA's comparison of lunar and Mars reactor designs found the Moon "a more difficult thermal environment for waste heat radiators" [44].

## Siting: 1 km from the habitat, beside the wells

The 2022 FSP specification allowed a reactor to operate on its lander or to be moved and then operated [3]. This plan moves it. Each reactor lands on a cargo lander in the landing field. The lander's 20 t crane lifts it onto a transport trailer, and heavy rovers in tandem, three on the graded route, tow it across the 2 km or more to the reactor field, where it runs on its own skid ([chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md) specifies the rovers and the trailer). The reactor field lies about 1 km from the habitat and beside the Rodwell field, which keeps the heat lines short. The landing field lies at least 2 km from both ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)), because power systems must also be kept clear of debris thrown by landing vehicles [2]. The reactors sit on ice-poor ground at the edge of the well field, since a pad on the ice sheet would warm the ground beneath it for decades.

NASA's requirements set the 1 km figure. The 2022 FSP specification limited dose to 5 rem (50 mSv) per year above background at 1 km [3], NASA expects LR-1's users to carry its power at least 1 km [30], and NASA's Mars trade study assumed "at least 1 km from continuously occupied crew assets" for the shield mass it chose [10]. That 50 mSv is about a fifth of the ~234 mSv per year that galactic cosmic rays deliver at the surface [29].

> Assumption: Each 100 kWe unit arrives with a shadow shield, a shield on one side of the core that casts a protected zone behind it. Together with a regolith berm that robots build around each reactor ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)), the shields hold the dose from all three reactors at the habitat below 20 mSv per year, the limit NASA's crew health standard sets for nuclear power sources ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md)). Justification: the FSP requirement of 50 mSv per year at 1 km was set for one 40 kWe unit [3]; three units of 2.5 times the power need more shield mass, more distance, or earthwork. NASA's trade study noted that shield mass can be traded against separation distance, at the cost of cable mass and line losses [10]. On Mars, robots can trade landed shield mass for excavated regolith.

The keep-out zone applies to people, and robots may work anywhere on site. Scaling the 2022 specification by inverse square and by 2.5 times the power, ignoring shield geometry, gives a machine 100 m from a reactor at most about 12 Gy of absorbed dose per year and one at 10 m about 1.25 kGy. Electronics are chosen for where each machine works, and the Orchestrator tracks the accumulated dose on each one.

## First power in early 2036

Reactor 1 lands in January 2036 on the second Wave 1 lander and is towed to the reactor field. Before start-up, robots lay and connect the 1 km cable, deploy the radiators, and begin the first berm. Fresh uranium fuel is only weakly radioactive until the reactor first runs, so this work costs the robots little dose. NASA's trade study flagged the time pressure of connecting cables to a remote power farm before the landers' onboard energy runs out [10]; deploying the Wave 1 solar field first removes that pressure in clear weather. Wave 1's first seven weeks, to about February 24, 2036, fall in the dust season, though, and a storm then would leave the field enough only for the survival floor, stopping the reactor work until the sky clears (see the storm table below).

> Assumption: Reactor 1 reaches first power 10–12 weeks after landing, around April 2036, and full 100 kWe by about June 2036 after staged power ascension with checks at intermediate levels. Justification: the tow, cable laying, radiator deployment, and checkout each take weeks; no surface reactor has done this before, so the schedule is this book's estimate. [Chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)'s construction start in April 2036 matches first power, with full-rate work from June.

> Assumption: Reactors 2 and 3 are commissioned about three months after landing, two months faster than reactor 1: reactor 2 in January 2039 and reactor 3 in early January 2041. Each reaches first power about six weeks after landing and then spends about two months raising power; reactor 3's first power, in early November 2040, comes as the freeze before the December conjunction begins, so Earth signs its start ahead of time as a standing approval, as it does for reactor 2 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). Justification: their positions in the reactor field, cable routes, radiator foundations, and berms are prepared before they land, and the robots have done the whole sequence once. Gate G4 depends on it: at reactor 1's pace, reactor 3 would reach full power only in February 2041, and firm power of 200 kWe would have held about five months at G4 instead of six ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)).

## Heat rejection and radiator dust

A Brayton converter turns only part of its reactor heat into electricity. NASA Glenn's design studies note that 100 kWe-class Brayton systems "tend to optimize at efficiencies of about 20 to 25 percent with radiator temperatures in the 400 to 600 K range," and fix the compressor inlet at 400 K [11]. A 100 kWe unit therefore rejects 300–400 kWt, all of it above 400 K (127 °C).

> Assumption: Each unit's radiators are sized for full rejection with no help from the wells or the Martian atmosphere, at roughly 100 m² of two-sided panel per unit. Justification: a gray surface of emissivity 0.85 (it radiates 85% as much as an ideal emitter) at 450 K radiates about 1.9 kW/m² per side to a 200 K sky, so 350 kWt needs about 90 m² of two-sided panel. Convection in the thin CO₂ adds margin, and heat taken by the wells reduces the load further.

Dust should matter less on radiators than on solar cells, but the only flight evidence is lunar: on Firefly's Blue Ghost lander in March 2025, NASA's electrodynamic dust shield (EDS) removed 97% of the dust from a glass surface and 82% from a thermal-radiator surface [16]. That test ran without Mars dust or a CO₂ atmosphere at 600 Pa.

> Assumption: Radiators are mounted vertically, carry embedded EDS electrodes, and are inspected and brushed by robots during scheduled down-power windows. Justification: the lunar demonstration shows the mechanism on a radiator surface [16]; brushing is a mechanical fallback. In the author's judgment, vertical panels shed most settling dust. Mineral dust emits well in the thermal infrared, so a thin film changes emittance little; a thick layer insulates.

## Waste heat melts the wells

A Rodwell melts a cavity in buried ice by circulating hot water through it and pumps the melt to the surface; NASA's model puts the heat at about 10 kWt per well [12], and [chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md) gives the figures and their status. NASA's Rodwell concept assumed a fission system providing "both electrical and thermal power" [12], and Kleinhenz and Paz noted that "waste heat from a fission reactor could be leveraged" for in situ resource utilization (ISRU) heating [13].

Waste heat must be hot enough and close enough. The Brayton cycle rejects heat above 400 K [11], and a Rodwell needs recirculating water warm enough to melt ice. A loop at 80–90 °C (353–363 K) does that easily and still sits about 37–47 K below the Brayton reject floor. A heat exchanger on the Brayton gas cooler warms a pressurized glycol-water loop, and a second heat exchanger at each wellhead passes that heat into the well's own meltwater. The two loops keep the reactor's coolant away from the ice and let the well loop drain safely if flow stops.

The distance is affordable. An insulated pipe run of 300 m out and back, with 5 cm of insulation of conductivity 0.02 W/(m·K) at Mars ambient, loses about 10 kWt; a 1 km run loses about 33 kWt. Moving 60 kWt at a 45 K temperature drop takes about 0.3 kg/s of fluid, a slow flow in a 6 cm pipe with negligible pumping power.

| 2036–2038 | 2 | ~20 kWt | ~10 kWt | none | none | 8–10% |

The water bank adds a temporary load. Its bladders lie beside the well field, and the ~530–590 t they hold when Wave 2 lands may freeze in storage. Melting all of it before it feeds the ~650 t habitat shield takes about 65–72 MWh, or about 5 kWt averaged over the fill ([chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md#filling-the-shield)). The habitat and the greenhouse take their heat from a loop of their own, about 1 km long, to the habitation zone: the habitat draws up to its whole ~5 kW of thermal control from it ([chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md)), the greenhouse's lamps return most of their power to it as heat ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)), and the run itself loses about 33 kWt. The 1 km transfer line that carries the bank's water to the habitat shares that loop's trench and is kept near 5 °C by it, at a cost of about 8 kWt of reactor heat until the shield is full and the line is drained. All together, the colony's heat uses take about a quarter to a third of one reactor's rejected heat, and at most about 36% with six wells, so any one reactor's heat carries them (this book's calculation).

Without waste heat, electric heating would take about 10 kWe per well, 20–60 kWe in total, which fits every epoch's normal budget but comes straight out of construction.

> Assumption: The reactor field is placed within about 300 m of the well field, and waste heat supplies all well melting heat. Justification: the calculation above; insulation conductivity of 0.02 W/(m·K) is typical of aerogel blankets or dry regolith at Martian pressure. The waste-heat assumption in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) therefore holds, at no electrical cost beyond pumps.

Without the pathfinder, reactor 1 is sited before the survey picks the main well field ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md#verifying-the-ice-before-commitment)). A well field up to about 2 km away still runs on waste heat: the line loses up to about 66 kWt, and at 0.3 kg/s the 80–90 °C loop reaches the wells at about 50–85 °C, warm enough to melt ice, though each extra kilometer of line weighs some 7–8 t dry. A field farther away takes reactor 2 beside it (this book's calculation). No requirement for a surface reactor yet asks that it can be moved once it has run, which would remove the question (chapter 3).

## Solar power for daytime and deferrable loads

### Arrays deliver 7–8% of nameplate

Solar nameplate ratings depend on the test condition, which can change the number by a third. This book rates arrays at air mass zero (AM0, 1,361 W/m², the solar constant at Earth), the convention for space arrays. On that basis, [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) assumes an array delivers a sol-average of 6.8–8.1% of its nameplate at the 42°N reference site in clear weather. The chain behind that figure:

| Step | Factor | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Top of atmosphere at Mars, mean (490–720 W/m² over the orbit) [15] | | 586 W/m² |
| Horizontal surface at 42°N, averaged over sol and year (night, low sun; [chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md)'s table) | ~24% | ~138 W/m² |
| Clear-sky atmosphere at τ≈0.5: beam losses, partly returned as scattered light, on a flat panel [37] | ~80% | ~110 W/m² |
| Two-axis tracking at τ≈0.5, under the sky measured near the Sun [37], [45] | +36–39% | 149–152 W/m² |
| Wiring, conversion, temperature, residual soiling | ~80% | 119–122 W/m², 8.8–8.9% of AM0 |
| Seasonal minima, off-nominal tracking, slow dust accumulation | ~78–91% | Planning figure 6.8–8.1% of AM0 |

The tracking gain is the least certain step. Above the atmosphere, a two-axis tracker at 42°N collects about 2.1 times what a flat plate does over a year (this book's calculation from chapter 3's orbit). On the surface, part of the light is scattered by dust, and what pointing gains depends on where in the sky that light comes from. NASA Glenn's model of Mars sunlight treats it as coming evenly from the whole sky. Built on the optical depths the Viking landers measured, it gives a two-axis gain of 19% over a flat panel at Viking Lander 1's 22°N in a clear year at τ = 0.5, and 21% at Viking Lander 2's 48°N through the dusty years Viking saw [38]; rerun at 42°N with chapter 3's orbit, a flat panel keeps about 80% of the sunlight at τ≈0.5, the table's third step, and tracking adds 22–25% (this book's calculation from [37], [38]). Mars dust scatters most of its light forward, though, so the sky is brightest close to the Sun, where a tracker points. Curiosity's navigation cameras measured that brightness from 4° to 29° from the Sun at Gale crater [45]. With the dust at τ = 0.5 and the Sun 45° up, that ring of sky alone sends a Sun-facing panel about 36% as much light as the direct beam (this book's integration of the measured profile). Crediting the tracker with only that measured ring, and treating the rest of the scattered light as even, puts the gain at 42°N at 36–39%, the figure in the table. A radiative-transfer model that matches the measured sky to within about 10% gives 62–70%, and 52–61% at τ≈0.7, where the even-sky model gives 12–14%; the plan does not count that difference (this book's calculation from [37], [45]). No tracking array has yet measured the gain on Mars.

Two more routes land between the table's figure and that model. The dust that fits the measured ring also scatters light inside 4° of the Sun, where the camera did not look, and adding it raises the bound to 42–46%. An Earth analog gives about the same. Perez and colleagues fitted a model of how skylight is spread across the sky to measurements at nine American and European sites, Phoenix and Albuquerque among them, and its term for light from near the Sun peaks in skies about as clear as Mars's at τ≈0.5, which they traced to forward scattering by haze or thin cloud [46]. Given the Mars split between direct and scattered light, it gives 43–45% (this book's calculation from [37], [46]). The analog carries the shape of a hazy sky, not Mars dust itself: on Earth, coarse desert grains send more light close to the Sun than fine haze does [47], and the model fitted to Mars dust points higher still. So does a second Mars model: the scheme that the Mars Planetary Climate Model of France's Laboratoire de Météorologie Dynamique uses for sunlight on slopes, which accounts for scattering by the dust [49], gives 57–60% for a panel that faces the Sun, and 56% with the panel's tilt held to 60° (this book's calculation from [37], [48]). Both Mars models spread the dust's forward scattering over the whole sky; the 42–46% routes treat the sky beyond 29° as even or borrow the shape of Earth's hazier skies.

> Hypothesis: Under Mars's clear-season dust at τ≈0.5, two-axis tracking at 42°N gains about 40–60% over a flat panel, more than the 36–39% the plan counts and less than the fitted model's 62–70%. Basis: the sky Curiosity measured 4–29° from the Sun [45], with the light inside 4° added from the dust that fits it (42–46%), an Earth sky model fitted at nine sites, which gives 43–45% for the same split of direct and scattered light [46], and a Mars climate model's scheme for sunlight on slopes, which gives 57–60% [48], [49] (this book's calculation); the lower figures treat the sky beyond 29° as even, and the Earth model carries only the shape of a forward-scattering sky, since on Earth haze and thin cloud make that shape and on Mars dust alone does. Test: a reanalysis of Curiosity's and Perseverance's sky images out to 90° from the Sun and at low Sun, a desk study the mission program can ask of the rover teams before G1 in March 2035; then Wave 1's field, logged against a flat reference panel and the measured optical depth from January 2036 and read at G2 in June 2037. A gain below 36% would cost each field up to about 3 kW, which the first epoch's headroom absorbs.

> Assumption: The Wave 1 solar field is about 400 kW (AM0 nameplate), roughly 1,000 m² of 30%-efficient cells on two-axis trackers, giving 27–33 kW sol-average in clear weather. Wave 2 doubles the field to about 800 kW, giving 55–65 kW. Justification: chapter 2 values; the derivation above. At 3–4 kg/m² including gimbals and support posts, NASA's estimate for 12 m UltraFlex wings with 30% growth [10], each field is 3–4 t, within chapter 2's ~10 t solar allocation.

### A τ≈5 storm leaves 20–35% of clear-sky output

During a storm the direct beam fades but scattered light remains: at the worst of a storm, NASA's Mars power study notes, it still reached 30–40% of clear-day direct light, and its solar sizing assumed incident energy falling to 35% of the baseline at τ = 5 [10]. Chapter 2 plans on 20–35% of clear-sky output at τ≈5, so the solar field drops to 5–11 kW in the first epoch and 11–23 kW later. NASA Glenn's model puts the design storm at the low end of that range: at τ≈5 a flat panel keeps about 20% of what a tracker collects in clear weather under the measured sky, and a tracker that keeps pointing only 17–18%, because in a storm almost no light comes from near the Sun and a tilted panel sees less of the bright sky than a flat one (this book's calculation from [37], [38], [45]). That low end, about 5 kW per field, is a flat panel's light alone, so it holds whatever the clear-sky tracking gain turns out to be. In a storm the trackers therefore lie flat. At the τ above 10 that Opportunity experienced in June 2018 [14], output falls to a few percent. Opportunity's last contact was on June 10, 2018 [28].

### Dust soiling and cleaning

Settled dust does slower damage: InSight, dimmed by four years of it, ended its mission in December 2022 [25]. Of the cleaning options, EDS is the most mature. Its fine electrodes sit under a transparent film, and a high-voltage wave lifts and moves the dust. Besides the 2025 lunar result [16], in the laboratory with Mars simulant it removed 85% with single-phase and over 90% with three-phase electrodes, much of it at Earth pressure, with development aimed at 5 mbar (500 Pa) of CO₂ [17]. It has never flown on Mars. Other often-cited methods do not transfer: a reported 95% removal applies to spacesuit fabric [18], charge induction needs relative humidity of about 30% [19], and Chladni-pattern vibration was tested only on sand at Earth ambient [20].

> Assumption: Arrays combine EDS electrodes, two-axis gimbals that periodically tilt steeply to shed dust, and robotic brushing. Justification: gimbal articulation was NASA's dust-removal assumption, with a permanent dust loss factor of 0.95 [10]; EDS has lunar flight evidence [16]; brushing is simple and robots are on site. None of these restores output during a storm, because the loss is in the sky.

## Storage: batteries only

### Batteries for a trip, not a storm

Batteries carry the critical load through the night after a first-epoch trip, absorb start-up surges, and steady the grid while a reactor changes power. In the first epoch, 1 MWh carries the 9–14 kW critical load for 70–110 h with no generation at all, about 3–4.5 sols. A 120-day storm with a 52 kW deficit, the most solar output a storm takes from both fields, would need about 150 MWh, so batteries play no part in storm survival.

> Assumption: Battery storage is lithium-based at about 165 Wh/kg at the pack level, so about 6 t per MWh, the figure NASA's Mars power study used [10]. [Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) carries about 6 t of batteries (~1 MWh) in Wave 1 and 6 t more in Wave 2.

### Why not regenerative fuel cells or power-to-methane

Regenerative fuel cells store energy as hydrogen and oxygen. NASA Glenn sized them for Mars only to bridge a single night of 12.3–13.5 h at 10 kW; their mass advantage appears for lunar nights of 73–356 h [22]. NASA rated them at technology readiness level (TRL) 3 in 2019 [23], and its architecture memo says fuel cells "do not trade well" for Mars because making the reactants in situ takes "more energy . . . than the fuel cells could provide" [2]. The colony carries none.

Storing energy as the propellant plant's own methane and oxygen, made in good times and burned during a storm, fails on efficiency. Power-to-gas plants convert electricity to methane at 46.7–48.5% efficiency in the best terrestrial studies [24], before Martian CO₂ capture and cryogenic liquefaction. Natural gas engines return 30 to over 46% of the fuel's energy as electricity [35] and stationary fuel cells 34–47% today, with higher targets [36]; taking a generous 35–60%, the round trip is 16–29% by this book's estimate. To cover that 150 MWh storm deficit, the colony would need 520–920 MWh of input electricity. The fuel would be 18–31 t of methane and 72–123 t of oxygen, 2.6 to 4.4 times the methane in one ascent vehicle load. Every tonne burned for power is a tonne not available for crew return.

## The 120-day storm and the shedding order

The 2018 storm shows the scale of the design case: it reached Opportunity by June 2, encircled the planet by June 20, and decayed with a 44-day e-folding time (dust opacity fell by a factor of about 2.7 every 44 days) until conditions returned to normal in mid-September [14].

The colony rides out the storm on firm fission and a fixed shedding order. Critical loads (keep-alive, communications, well freeze protection, habitat thermal and pressure control, life support, cryogenic tank cooling) are shed only down to a survival floor, and only in the one case below that the budget does not close. Deferrable loads go in this order:

1. Construction, sintering, and excavation, keeping only robot charging for inspection and repair
2. The solid oxide electrolysis oxygen line, brought to cold standby
3. Greenhouse lighting, reduced to a plant-survival photoperiod
4. The methane and oxygen propellant plant, throttled last because it has the longest production chain, although [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) leaves it about 15 months of schedule margin

The oxygen line goes cold rather than hot because MOXIE (Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment), its flight ancestor, proved "surprisingly robust against thermal cycling" [26], while holding the stacks near 800 °C for months would waste power. The Orchestrator keeps them warm only for curtailments expected to last a few sols.

In normal operation the first epoch has energy to spare. The reactor and solar field supply 127–133 kW against a 54–78 kW load, 49–79 kW of headroom on average, and the Orchestrator gives it to the builders: sintering and excavation draw 70–100 kW whenever the reactor runs. From June 2036 to October 2038, about 20,500 h, 70 kW would come to about 1,440 MWh if the reactor never stopped; at the 85% availability [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md) plans on, it is about 1,220 MWh, construction's own allocation included. That covers the ~680 MWh of the four Wave 2 landing sites and the rest of the first epoch's work, about 1,165 MWh in all once the Wave 3 cores the rovers begin in February 2038 and the losses in the line that carries the power to the landing field are counted, with about 5% to spare, which chapter 8 holds as a reserve. That reserve and the Wave 3 core work begun early, about 305 MWh together, also cover propellant tanks that leak five to ten times the heat [chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md) plans, about 70–160 MWh over the epoch ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#loads-by-epoch)). Construction stops if the reactor trips; otherwise the number of robots and their charging capacity set its pace.

A first-epoch trip also stops the heat to the wells. The Rodwell model holds the pool close to freezing [12]. By this book's estimate, a pool losing 9–10 kWt starts to form ice within hours: a 5 m³ pool would freeze solid in about 2 days, and the 10–25 m³ pools the model builds up over months in 4–10 days. The wells therefore enter a safe state: the surface lines drain, the pumps stop, and the cavity is remelted after restart, at the cost of lost production time. From 2039, heat taps on at least two reactors mean one trip does not freeze the wells.

The three rows that do not close are one case: a τ≈5 storm with no reactor running, before reactor 2 is commissioned. It can come in Wave 1's first weeks, before reactor 1 has started (only the seven weeks to about February 24, 2036 fall in the dust season); after a first-epoch trip; or after a trip between Wave 2's landing and reactor 2's commissioning, when the habitat's heat and pressure hold raise the critical load to 23–32 kW. That last window ends about three weeks before the next dust season begins in late January 2039, or about six weeks inside it if reactor 2 takes as long as reactor 1 did. Chapter 2's [storm case](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#the-storm-case) treats the case as one accepted risk. The colony then sheds even the critical load to a survival floor.

> Assumption: The survival floor is about 3–5 kW before Wave 2 lands: the Orchestrator's core computers and the independent protection systems (0.5–1 kW), heaters that keep electronics, batteries, and reactor instruments above their survival temperatures (1.5–2.5 kW), a reduced link to Earth (0.5–1 kW), and keep-warm heat for the four robots held back to restart the reactor (0.4–0.6 kW). The well pumps stop and the wells drain; tank cooling stops, and the stored oxygen boils off at about 25–75 kg a sol, product the oxygen line can make again. From Wave 2's landing to reactor 2's commissioning the floor rises to about 5–9 kW: the habitat is held just warm enough to protect its membranes and seals (2–3 kW, about half its ~5 kW thermal control), and Wave 2's landers and plants are kept warm (0.5–1 kW). About 200 kWh of battery is held back for the restart: about 60 kWh for the robots' drive to the reactor and the repair, and up to about 80 kWh for the reactor's own start-up. Justification: this book's estimate, built up from the loads that must keep running; the restart team's heat and charge are counted here because the construction allocation that normally pays for them is the first load shed.

A closed-Brayton restart does draw outside power. The alternator runs as a motor to spin the turbine and circulate the gas until the reactor's heat makes the cycle self-sustaining, and "a power source (batteries, solar arrays, etc.) is required to start the system before the system can become power-producing" [40]. For a 100 kWe system of two 50 kWe Brayton units, NASA Glenn's analysis puts the motoring power at about 4 kW for one unit started from 400 K and about 6 kW from 200 K, and at 10–16 kW for both at once, so the units start one at a time [40]. NASA's 40 kWe surface reactor concept drew up to almost 5 kW during its start for pumps, drives, radiator deployment, and converter motoring, reached break-even at about 8 hours, and needed no outside power after 10, from a 30 kWh battery and a 5 kW array [39]. A restart days after a trip, with the loop near Mars's 200 K, then takes about 7–8 kW for up to 10 hours, about 80 kWh; one soon after a trip, while the loop is still warm, takes about 10–25 kWh, close to the ~18 kWh that NASA's lunar start-up profile draws (this book's calculation from [39], [40]). The ~200 kWh reserve covers one cold restart and the robots' work with about 60 kWh to spare, enough for a second attempt only from a warm start.

Against 5–11 kW of storm solar, the first-epoch floor holds, with almost nothing to spare at the dark end of the range, so the ~800 kWh of battery left after the restart reserve only carries the nights; with no sun at all it would hold the floor for 6–11 sols. Against the full 9–14 kW critical load, the whole 1 MWh would last about 4.7 sols at worst. In the Wave 2 window the floor holds on the two fields' 11–23 kW; in the first weeks after landing, before the Wave 2 field is up, the Wave 1 field leaves a deficit of up to 4 kW, and ~800 kWh covers it for about 9 sols. Restarting the reactor, the main mitigation, is an A3 duty below.

Once reactor 2 is commissioned in early 2039, the storm case is mild: two reactors alone exceed the whole colony's load. The transition after Wave 3's landing closes as well: until reactor 3 is commissioned in January 2041, a trip in a storm leaves 111–123 kW against Wave 3's critical load of 38–48 kW plus the propellant plant, 73–100 kW, though the greenhouse, shed before the plant, can lose up to half its 15–20 kW at the top of that range ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)).

## The microgrid: 120 Vdc on two independent buses

Each reactor steps its output up for a buried cable of about 1 km [30]. NASA's Mars trade study assumed 1,000 Vdc transmission stepped down to 120 Vdc at the point of use [10], and the 2022 FSP specification set 120 Vdc at the user interface [3]. The colony uses 120 Vdc as its load bus. Another buried line at 1,000 Vdc, about 2.5 km long with feeds on to each landing site, carries construction power, and from late 2040 the MAV pad's loads, to the landing field and loses about 5–7% of what it carries, perhaps a third less in the cold ground, which the line's own meters will show ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). The 2025 directive's "Standardized Modular Interface" [1] has no published voltage yet, so that choice may change.

The atmosphere limits voltage on Mars. At about 600 Pa of CO₂, gas between exposed conductors breaks down at far lower voltages than at sea level, near the minimum of the Paschen curve. NASA's 1997 Mars reference mission sized its transmission cables at 500 V for this reason, noting that exposed conductors above 500 V "could experience large power drains due to atmospheric discharges" [34].

> Assumption: Exposed conductors, array strings, and open terminals stay at or below about 400 V; transmission runs above 500 V only in fully insulated, jacketed cables buried or covered by regolith, with potted or gas-filled connectors. Justification: the 500 V guideline [34]; NASA's 1,000 Vdc transmission assumption [10]; burial also protects cables from landing ejecta and ultraviolet light.

The grid is a ring with two independent buses, so the loss of one cable, converter, or bus segment leaves every critical load fed from the other side. Solid-state breakers isolate faults in milliseconds. Critical loads have dedicated feeders and their own local batteries sized for at least one sol. Those local batteries are the colony's ~1 and ~2 MWh, not an addition to them: one sol of critical load is about 0.2–0.35 MWh in the first epoch, 0.6–0.8 MWh in 2039–2040, and 0.9–1.2 MWh from 2041, which leaves about 0.8–1.1 MWh for smoothing even then, so Wave 3 lands no batteries (this book's calculation). Two hard-wired systems act independently of the Orchestrator, as [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md) requires for all safety-critical controllers: the protection system that trips breakers, and the reactor protection system that shuts a reactor down in an emergency (a scram). The Orchestrator issues set points and shedding commands; it cannot override a protection trip.

The rule covers the MAV pad too, about 2.5 km from the reactor field. The construction line feeds it through its switching station, and a lighter feeder on its own route from the other side of the ring stands ready to take over ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). A fault on either path, or in the station, leaves the MAV's cryocoolers and its own keep-alive fed from the other, while the pad's battery bridges the switch. A second MAV pad, where one is built, gets both feeds, each extended from its own path so that they do not meet at the first pad.

## What would change this conclusion

A reactor that misses the 2035 window would overturn this chapter's plan: the LR-1-class fallback under Risks does not fit Wave 1, so a late reactor moves Wave 1 a window and the crew to about April 2048. So would a Rodwell that cannot use low-grade heat, which would take 20–60 kWe of electric heating out of construction.

## Autonomy

Under the levels of [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md), the power system's first autonomous duty is restarting a tripped reactor without Earth.

### What A3 must do

At A3, site autonomy for known work, the power system recovers from every anticipated fault and meets crew gate criterion 6 ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)). That means autonomous reactor start-up, power changes, and restart after a trip, all within an envelope set on Earth and enforced by the independent reactor protection system. The Orchestrator estimates optical depth from the solar field's output, the sky cameras, and orbital weather reports, and runs the shedding order from it. It schedules dust cleaning against measured soiling, drains and remelts the wells around a heat outage, and lays, mates, and inspects cables. LR-1 itself is specified for autonomous operation [8], and the FSP specification required load following from 0 to 100% at the user interface [3].

### What A4 adds

An A4 system diagnoses faults nobody anticipated, such as a Brayton alternator that begins to vibrate in a new way or a radiator loop with a slow leak, and tests a workaround on a spare or a simulation before touching the live system. The architecture stays the same; A4 buys fewer spares and less lost production.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could make radiator panels, cable, and array structures from local materials and plan the megawatt-class expansion of [chapter 15](15.%20Terraforming%20and%20the%20long%20view.md). It would also need a rule for who approves a reactor change it designs.

## Risks

- Reactor schedule ([R16](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r16)). The 100 kWe-class unit exists only as a directive and a policy class, and the first lunar reactor is a 20 kWe design [30]. If no Mars unit is qualified by 2034, Wave 1 needs five LR-1-class units for the power of one: up to 75 t at 15 t each [30], about 60 t over the allocation and more than Wave 1's ~50 t of margin across three landers ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)). Making room by leaving robots behind does not work either, because Wave 2's four landing sites need all of Wave 1's rover time: with one of the four rock-armor teams gone, the armor falls short before Wave 2 lands ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). A reactor that is not qualified by 2034 therefore moves Wave 1 to 2037 and the crew to about April 2048 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)).
- A storm with no reactor running before reactor 2 is commissioned, in Wave 1's first weeks or after a trip ([R27](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r27)), the accepted case above; see also the storm itself ([R02](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r02)) and its simulated test ([R39](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r39)).
- Fission plant anomaly and common-mode failure ([R10](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r10)). Three reactors of one design can share one latent defect; staggered arrival (2036, 2038, 2040) gives two years of operating data before each additional unit is committed, and [chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md) holds the spares.
- Dust ([R11](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r11)). Mitigation on Mars is unproven at scale; solar losses cost only production from early 2039, but radiator fouling cuts the reactors' heat rejection.
- Heat loops ([R32](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r32)). The waste-heat loops add a leak or frozen-line failure mode, contained by two loops, drainable wellhead exchangers, and heat taps on at least two reactors.
- High-voltage transmission at Martian pressure has no operating history, so connectors need early testing in a Mars chamber ([R22](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r22)).

Of the risks [Appendix A](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md) scores here, the reactor schedule alone decides whether Wave 1 carries a reactor in 2035.

## Open questions

- Will a ≥100 kWe surface reactor be qualified for Mars by 2034, and will it be a surface-optimized design or, as NSTM-3 suggests, a propulsion reactor adapted to the surface [32]?
- How much shielding, distance, and berming do three 100 kWe units need to stay below 20 mSv per year at the habitat, and what dose do robots accept near the reactor field?
- Are the reactor's hot structures, radiators, and dust shields compatible with years of CO₂, dust, and 600 Pa?
- Does the Rodwell heat balance hold at the chosen site? Waste heat still covers wells that need twice the modeled heat.
- Should the Mars reactor be required to move once after it has run, so that reactor 1 can follow the well field if the survey places it more than about 2 km away?
- If the chosen design carries a liquid-metal loop, what keeps it from freezing through an outage of several sols, and does that heat fit inside the survival floor, which now holds only instrument heaters?
- How much does two-axis tracking gain at 42°N? The sky measured near the Sun supports at least 36–39%, the light inside 4° and an Earth sky model point to about 42–46%, a Mars climate model's slope scheme gives 57–60%, and a model fitted to the measured sky gives 62–70%. A reanalysis of the rovers' sky images before G1 can narrow it; Wave 1's field, logged against a flat reference panel and the measured optical depth from January 2036, will settle it, and the upper figure would add about 5–7 kW per field.
