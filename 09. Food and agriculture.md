# 9. Food and agriculture

*Last reviewed: September 28, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, 2042.* In the greenhouse module the lights come on at 06:00 colony time, stacked four shelves high over 96 m² of potatoes, soybeans, wheat, lettuce, and tomatoes. A gantry arm lifts a tray of tubers, weighs it, photographs it, and cuts three samples for the analyzer. No one will eat these potatoes. They go into the bioreactor, their nitrogen and potassium return to the nutrient tanks, and their yield goes into the production record: the eleventh consecutive potato harvest within 10% of target. The crew gate does not count it, because the crew's food is already packed; the record tells the crew how far they can trust these beds.

The robots of [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md) prepare a site and a heat-loop connection for the greenhouse module before it lands in 2038; this chapter decides what grows in it. A crew of four on Mars needs about 12,100 kcal a day, plus protein, vitamins, and minerals, for every day of a surface stay of more than a year. Food is the consumable the colony cannot yet make in bulk from local resources: crops need seed and fertilizer from Earth, and light, which on Mars means electric power or a weak, dusty sun.

The plan grows crops hydroponically, the method NASA has used for staple crops in closed chambers since 1988 and that two Antarctic greenhouses have run far from any farm. Martian soil comes later, once it has been stripped of perchlorate.

In brief: the 15–20 kW greenhouse allocation from 2040 lights roughly 90–115 m² of crops and can supply about a fifth to a quarter of the crew's calories, plus fresh sources of the vitamins stored food loses. The rest comes from about 9.6 t of shelf-stable food, enough for 1,000 days on the surface. Before the crew arrives, the greenhouse's job is proof: years of robot-run harvests that show its yields are real.

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) sets the frame:

- Crew of four on the surface from September 2044 to early November 2045, about 410 days, with surface consumables sized for about 1,000 days as margin.
- The greenhouse module lands with Wave 2 (October 2038) within a ~20 t planning allocation.
- Greenhouse power is a deferrable load of 5–10 kW from October 2038 and 15–20 kW from September 2040, sol-averaged; in a 120-day storm it is the third load curtailed, after construction and the oxygen line.
- Water: ~50 t for greenhouse and life-support start-up inventory.
- Everything must work at autonomy level A3 ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md)).

Because the crew gate does not depend on the greenhouse, the plan can afford to learn from it.

## What four people need

NASA's crew health standard, NASA-STD-3001, requires the food system to provide an average of 12,698 kJ (3,035 kcal) per crew member per day, or a value from the estimated energy requirement equations for a specific crew, plus 837 kJ (200 kcal) for each hour of extravehicular activity (EVA) [1]. Four people therefore need about 12,100 kcal a day before any suited work. A sol is about 3% longer than a day, a difference this chapter's rounded figures absorb, so the tables below use the same 12,100 kcal per sol.

NASA bases micronutrient and macronutrient targets on the National Academies' Dietary Reference Intakes, with vitamin D raised to offset the lack of sunlight and protein adjusted for individual crew members [1]. On the International Space Station (ISS), crews have taken about 16–18% of their energy from protein, roughly 95–110 g a day, while eating only about 2,300–2,450 kcal [1].

Stored food loses nutrients. A NASA study of 109 space foods stored for three years at room temperature found that potassium, calcium, vitamin D, and vitamin K may fall short of daily needs even before storage, and that vitamin B1 and vitamin C can degrade to inadequate levels after about one and three years respectively [2]. The standard's technical brief lists a five-year shelf life for menu items as an open technology challenge [1]; current ISS packaging gives up to about two years [3]. For mass, NASA's life support baseline now plans on 2.39 kg per crew member per day, including packaging, the highest six-month rate at which ISS crews have opened food bags; it notes that the older figure of 1.83 kg, from the station's early food containers, "did not equate to the current ... (~3000 kcal per day) requirement" [3]. NASA's food scientists put a three-year Mars mission for four at about 10,500 kg of food with the current food system, the same rate [22], and the station's cargo manifests of 2017–2020 carried 2.77 kg per crew member per day, counting every kind of food sent up [23].

> Assumption: This book plans shipped food at 2.39 kg per person per day, packaging included. Justification: it is NASA's current planning value for the ISS food system, and NASA's food scientists use the same rate for a Mars mission on that system [3], [22]. Of the 2.39 kg, NASA counts 0.43 kg of packaging, 0.2 kg of packages never opened, and 0.2 kg left in the packages; the 1.56 kg eaten carries about 3,050 kcal [3]. By this book's calculation the ISS menu, at about 1,960 kcal per kilogram of food, meets this chapter's 3,035 kcal plus 200 kcal per EVA hour with 1.9–2.3 kg once packaging, 3–10% of the food left in its packages, and up to 1.5 EVA hours per person-day are counted, while 1.83 kg buys 2,900–3,050 kcal only if nothing is left in the packages and no one works outside. In 2009 NASA's food program gave 1.82 kg as its packaged rate and set a goal of 1.14 kg [24]; the station's food system has not reached it [3], [22]. Unopened packages stay in the reserve, so the rate errs toward more food, which pays for the EVA calories the ISS figure does not include. If the crew's menu is qualified at a lower rate, every food mass in this book falls in proportion.

> Hypothesis: A Mars menu that ships more of its food freeze-dried, and rehydrates it with the colony's own water, could bring the shipped mass down to about 1.9 kg per person per day, a fifth less. Basis: the ISS standard food system, packaging included, is about 46% water, 88% of it in ready-to-eat thermostabilized foods, and replacing about 37% of those foods with freeze-dried versions would bring the system to 30% water [22]; with the same food solids and packaging, NASA's table of that change cuts 2.39 kg to about 1.85–1.9 kg (this book's calculation), and the colony has water to spare ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)). NASA's authors call the feasibility of such a menu "currently unknown", since not every food freeze-dries to an acceptable texture and crews value the ready-to-eat foods [22], and on the ISS freeze-dried foods keep about 1.5 years against 2 years for thermostabilized ones [1], a gap that matters for food stored three to five years. Test: a 30%-water menu put through the same storage and acceptability tests as the reserve, by NASA's Space Food Systems Laboratory or the food supplier, before the support flight's food is packed in 2041; a pass lowers the surface reserve by about a fifth, and the ERV's return food, whose menu is fixed by mid-2034, stays on the tested menu.

## Grown versus shipped

The first crew eats mostly food from Earth. A greenhouse sized to [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s power supplies a minority of calories, and even a larger one would need years of operation before a crew could bet their lives on it. The greenhouse adds fresh sources of what stored food loses: vitamin C, vitamin K, folate, and potassium, plus fresh texture. How much of each it supplies depends on the planting plan, which the robots will measure in two proving phases, H1 and H2, before the crew arrives.

> Assumption: A shelf-stable reserve covers 100% of the crew's calories for 1,000 days on the surface, with no credit for the greenhouse: 4 × 1,000 × 2.39 kg ≈ 9.6 t, more than twice the planned 410-day stay. Outbound food travels in the crew vehicle and return food in the Earth return vehicle (ERV) waiting in Mars orbit, as [chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md#the-earth-return-vehicle) plans; neither is part of this reserve. About half the reserve lands with the October 2041 support flight (arriving September 2042) and half with the crew in September 2044. The oldest packages are eaten first; made in 2041, they would be about 3 years old when the crew lands and up to about 4.5 years old when it leaves in late 2045. What remains would be about seven years old when the next crew lands in late 2048, an age the storage data [2] argue against, so it stays on Mars only as a deep reserve, and that crew's own surface half, about 4.8 t, lands on the December 2045 food flight, about three years old when the crew arrives and about 4.4 when it leaves, as the first crew's was ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#crew-and-after-20432048); this book's calculation). If the crew misses the November 2045 departure, the stay stretches to about 1,190 days, and at least 2.6 t of food landing from the 2045/46 window about October 2046 covers the gap ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)). If instead the crew slips to the 2045/46 window, the support lander's food would be about 5–6.2 years old during that stay, so the same window also lands about 4.8 t of fresh surface food for the crew to eat, and the 2041 food stays behind as the reserve.

> Assumption: The reserve is stored in an unheated but enclosed store near the habitat, held well below 0 °C by the Mars environment. Lower temperatures are expected to slow the chemical breakdown of vitamins [2]; whether that closes the B1 and vitamin C gap for these packages must be tested on Earth before 2041. Vitamin D, and B1 if needed, come from supplements.

Food landed with Wave 3 in 2040 would be five to seven years old during the crew's stay, an age the storage data [2] argue against.

Return food cannot wait for a later flight, because it rides in the ERV: about 2.2 t for four crew over 235 days launches in October 2041 and is eaten on the way home 4.1–4.7 years after packing, with no greenhouse behind it. A one-window crew slip or a missed return window would leave the same food 6.2–6.9 years old on the way home. Gate G4 therefore checks a seven-year storage qualification of the ERV's menu at the temperature the ERV holds it (chapter 13); a real-time test that reports by G4 in July 2041 has to start by mid-2034, with the menu fixed by then. Five years would cover only the planned return, and fresh food cannot reach the ERV: the 2045/46 food flight lands on the surface, and the MAV lifts only a few kilograms of it, enough for vitamin supplements.

## Hydroponics first

Hydroponics, growing plants with roots in recirculating nutrient solution, has the longest test record. NASA's Biomass Production Chamber at Kennedy Space Center ran from 1988 to 2000 with 20 m² of crops on four stacked shelves in a tightly closed atmosphere, all in recirculating nutrient film technique (NFT) channels [5]. It grew wheat, soybean, lettuce, potato, and tomato to harvest [5], with nutritional profiles comparable to or better than field-grown crops; soybean was highest in protein and fat, and potato and wheat in carbohydrate [7]. China's Lunar Palace 1 later supported three people for 105 days in 2014, with 69 m² of crops supplying their air and water and 55% of their food [5].

In orbit, NASA's Veggie unit offers about 0.1 m² of growing area and the Advanced Plant Habitat about 0.2 m² [8], [20]. They prove crops grow and can be eaten in space, at a scale far too small to size a farm.

Two Antarctic greenhouses are the closest analogs to remote operation, and neither ran unattended:

- The South Pole Food Growth Chamber, an automated hydroponic chamber inside Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station, used 281 kWh of electricity, 1.1 kg of CO₂, and 0.21 kg of fertilizer salts per day in 2006 to produce 2.8 kg of fresh vegetables and 0.52 kg of oxygen per day, with remote telepresence support [9].
- The German Aerospace Center's EDEN ISS greenhouse near Neumayer Station III produced more than 268 kg of edible biomass on 12.5 m² over its 2018 season, about 0.075 kg/m² per day, mostly cucumbers, lettuces, leafy greens, and tomatoes, under light-emitting diodes (LEDs) at 330–600 µmol/(m²·s) for 17 h a day [10]. It was a free-standing container in an Antarctic winter, with its own heating and air management.

EDEN ISS also measured the cost in labor: its operator spent 3–4 h a day on the greenhouse, and about two-thirds of that went to running and maintaining the technology, not to the plants [6]. That daily work, which no analog has yet handed to machines, is the job the colony's robots take over.

## The 2040 greenhouse can supply a fifth to a quarter of the calories

The greenhouse turns electricity into light and light into food. Its output depends on how many photons each joule buys, how much light each crop needs, and how many food calories each photon yields; the share of power that reaches the lamps is an assumption below.

For lamps, the best commercial horticultural fixtures in 2020 produced 2.5–2.8 µmol of photosynthetic photons per joule for white-plus-red spectra and about 3.0 µmol/J for blue-plus-red; the authors projected practical limits near 3.4–4.1 µmol/J [4].

For light, NASA's life support baseline gives daily light integrals of about 17 mol/m² for lettuce at a 16 h photoperiod, 28 mol/m² for potato and sweet potato at 12 h, and 115 mol/m² for wheat [3]. The Biomass Production Chamber grew lettuce at 300 µmol/(m²·s) for 16 h, about 17 mol/m² per day [5].

For yield, NASA sums up its crop tests and the Russian Bios-3 work this way: at about 40 mol/m² per day, roughly 50 m² of crops supply 2,500 kcal per person per day [5]. That works out to about 1.25 kcal of food per mole of light for a staple-heavy diet. Radiation use efficiencies in the chamber ranged from 0.43 g of dry mass per mole for soybean to 0.64 g/mol for potato, and transplanting schemes raised potato to 0.82 g/mol of edible dry mass [5].

> Assumption: Lamps take 70% of greenhouse power; fans, pumps, dehumidification, and controls take 30%. Heating comes from lamp waste heat and the colony's reactor heat loop, not from the greenhouse allocation. Fixture efficacy is 2.8 µmol/J (2020 commercial level, conservative for 2038). Staple crops receive 30 mol/m² per sol, which suits potato, sweet potato, soybean, and peanut (24–28 mol/m² in the crop table); wheat, at 115 mol/m², is left out of the staple case. Food yield is 1.25 kcal per mole of light. The 70% lamp share is this book's design target; no facility has demonstrated it.

A sol lasts 88,775 s. With those assumptions:

A real planting plan gives some area to salad crops, tomatoes, and herbs, which yield few calories. With about 30% of the area in those crops, the 2040 greenhouse supplies roughly 20–25% of the crew's calories. Varying the lamp share from 65% to 75% and efficacy from 2.5 to 3.2 µmol/J moves the all-staples figure between about 22% and 44%.

EDEN ISS, the one measured analog, averaged 0.8 kW per square meter of cultivation area over its 2018 season [6], and its lighting took 28% of the facility's power [21]; most of the rest went to heating and conditioning its container. At 0.8 kW/m², 15–20 kW would light only 19–25 m², about 6–8% of the crew's calories. The sizing above assumes instead that the Mars greenhouse sits inside the colony's heat loop and puts most of its power into lamps. Nearly every watt it draws, 15–20 kW from 2040, ends up as heat that returns to the loop and offsets heater power. The robots must verify this in 2038–2040, because every calorie figure in this section depends on it.

Full self-sufficiency, at roughly 55–60 kW of greenhouse power, is out of reach inside the baseline.

### Water, carbon dioxide, oxygen, and nutrients

Crops give back almost all the water they take up. In the Biomass Production Chamber, nutrient-film crops transpired about 4.0–4.7 L/m² per day for potato, wheat, and soybean and 2.1 L/m² for lettuce [5]. The 2040 greenhouse therefore transpires about 350–450 L per sol, which condensing heat exchangers recover as clean water. The standing inventory in reservoirs and channels is a few tonnes, well inside [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s 50 t start-up allocation.

Plants need carbon dioxide at about 0.10–0.20 kPa partial pressure for best growth, and do less well above about 1 kPa [3]. The life support baseline lists potato taking up about 45 g of CO₂ and releasing 32 g of O₂ per square meter per day at its nominal light level [3]. Scaled to the 2040 greenhouse, that is roughly 4–6 kg of CO₂ taken up and 3–4 kg of O₂ released per sol, gross, about the crew's demand of 3.4 kg per sol (chapter 2). Once the crew eats the edible part and a bioreactor breaks down the rest, most of that oxygen is consumed again, so the greenhouse closes about the same fraction of the oxygen loop as of the food loop. Before the crew arrives, the greenhouse draws CO₂ from the same Mars-atmosphere compressors that supply the oxygen plant ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)).

Fertilizer and seed must be shipped at first. NASA's baseline projects 90–100 kg of fertilizer salts per person per year for a full plant diet, with more than half recoverable from inedible biomass [3]. For a greenhouse feeding about one person-equivalent, that is roughly 100 kg a year before recycling.

> Assumption: The greenhouse lands with about 0.5 t of fertilizer salts and acid, enough for 2038–2046 with partial recycling. Nutrient recovery from inedible biomass starts in 2040.

## Aquaponics: what the evidence shows

Aquaponics raises fish and plants on the same water. One Mars test, the University of Greenwich's Feeding Mars pilot, ran from March to May 2022 in a gallery exhibition with no statistical design [11]. Eight crops grew in two perchlorate-free Martian substrates irrigated with tilapia-tank water and digested fish sludge. They germinated and developed more slowly than in compost, which the authors attributed mainly to a substrate pH of 8.7; the fish ate commercial feed with 37% crude protein [11]. Aquaponics adds protein and a nutrient loop, but it also adds animals to ship, a feed supply to close, and a failure mode (a fish die-off) with no robotic operating experience behind it. It is a crew-era experiment in this plan.

## Regolith as a later substrate

Martian soil lacks organic matter and microbes, and it carries salts that are toxic to plants and people. Perchlorate is the main problem: Phoenix found 0.4–0.6% by mass at its site [12], and it appears widespread at around 0.5–1% [13]; [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md) covers its toxicity. It is highly soluble, so leaching removes most of it. Perchlorate-reducing bacteria can then convert the dissolved perchlorate to chloride and oxygen, a route Davila and colleagues proposed as a cheap source of clean soil and extra oxygen [13]. Water hyacinth tolerates and accumulates perchlorate in water culture [14], but at most it could polish leachate, and its biomass would become perchlorate waste.

No plant study cited here used a perchlorate-bearing substrate. In perchlorate-free simulants, biology helped. Sweet clover inoculated with the nitrogen-fixing bacterium *Sinorhizobium meliloti* grew about 29 times more biomass, though the soil gained no measurable nitrogen [15]. Alfalfa mixed back in as fertilizer raised turnip, radish, and lettuce growth by 79–311%, and cyanobacteria and basalt filtering desalted a briny water simulant [16]. Sweet potato grew best at 25% Mars Global Simulant (MGS-1) in the mix and was strongly inhibited at 75% [17]. Cyanobacteria as a fertilizer and life-support organism on Mars remain a proposal [18].

> Assumption: Regolith growing begins only after the crew arrives and after a site-specific planetary protection review. The first regolith beds are sealed bins inside a pressurized module. The regolith is first leached with recycled water until perchlorate is below a threshold set by chapter 12, the leachate is treated in a perchlorate-reducing bioreactor, and the recovered water returns to the loop. Before the crew arrives, robots run only small leaching and bioreduction trials on sealed samples, to measure water use and perchlorate removal.

## Planetary protection for microbes

Every crop system carries Earth microbes in seeds, on roots, and in nutrient solution, and the regolith work adds deliberate inoculants. Under the Committee on Space Research's 2026 policy, crewed missions keep planetary protection goals and must go on monitoring their microbes [19]; [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md) sets out the base-wide rules. A greenhouse full of living systems adds four of its own:

- All growing, including every inoculated regolith bin, stays inside pressurized, filtered modules. No inoculant is released to open ground.
- The colony keeps an inventory of every organism it deliberately brings and monitors the greenhouse microbiome, which the robots can sample on each harvest.
- Waste biomass goes to a closed bioreactor, and its residues stay inside the colony's waste system.
- Regolith brought indoors for growing comes from sites the robots have already characterized, and crops grown in Martian material are not eaten until the planetary protection plan allows it, because eating them is also a back-contamination question.

Until the crew and the planetary protection plan decide otherwise, nothing grown, inoculated, or composted leaves the pressurized modules.

## Robots prove the greenhouse before the crew arrives

The greenhouse proves itself in phases timed to [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s cargo waves, labeled H1–H3 to keep them apart from [chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)'s gates G0–G4.

| Phase | Dates | Power | Work | Exit criterion |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| Preparation | Jan 2036–Oct 2038 | none | Wave 1 has no greenhouse. Robots prepare the module site and heat-loop connection (chapter 8) | Site ready |
| H1: Commissioning | Oct 2038–Sep 2040 | 5–10 kW | Land and connect the ~20 t module; commission NFT channels, LEDs, climate, and condensate recovery; grow lettuce, radish, and dwarf wheat, then potato and soybean; measure lamp share and kilowatts per square meter | Three consecutive cycles of each test crop at ≥70% of target yield; lamp share ≥65% |
| H2: Production proving | Sep 2040–Aug 2043 | 15–20 kW | Expand to 90–115 m²; staple rotation with staggered plantings; nutrient recovery from inedible biomass; seed production; sealed regolith leaching trials | ≥12 months at ≥80% of target yield with no human intervention, including operation through one regional or global dust event |
| H3: Crew operations | Sep 2044 onward | 15–20 kW | Crew eats the harvest; robots keep running the systems; aquaponics and regolith beds become crew experiments | Chapter 12 criteria |

Harvests before the crew arrives are weighed, analyzed for nutrients and contaminants, and then broken down in the bioreactor. Their value is in the record: a crew should not plan on greenhouse food that has never been grown on Mars for more than a year without help.

From early 2041, when reactor 3 is running, firm fission power covers the colony's full load (chapter 2), so a storm by itself should not darken the greenhouse, and neither should one reactor trip, even during a storm ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)). The lights go down only in rarer cases. Between Wave 3's landing in September 2040 and reactor 3's commissioning in January 2041, a trip during a storm can take up to half the greenhouse's power at the top of the load range, because the lights are shed before the propellant plant; later, the loss of two reactors in a storm does the same while the plant is running. Between Wave 2's landing in October 2038 and reactor 2's commissioning in January 2039, a trip during a storm sheds the whole colony to its survival floor, and the first test crops go dark. The greenhouse dims before it goes dark: the robots cut light to a survival level, harvest what is close to maturity, and replant from seed afterward. Staggered plantings keep the loss to part of a cycle.

The H2 record measures readiness only: chapter 13 keeps the greenhouse out of the crew gate because the reserve feeds the crew if it fails.

## What would change this conclusion

One measurement and one decision would move the greenhouse's share. H1 measurements of kilowatts per square meter near EDEN ISS levels would cut it to a vitamin garden. In the other direction, once the ascent-vehicle propellant loads for the first crew and the next are made, by about mid-2045 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#crew-and-after-20432048)), the 35–52 kW propellant plant ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)) can idle, apart from short runs to remake methane lost from the ascent vehicle ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)), and redirecting part of that power could let a larger greenhouse supply half the diet or more for later crews.

The shipped food rests on the ISS's 2.39 kg per person per day. A Mars menu qualified at about 30% water, rehydrated with the colony's own water, would cut the surface food in this book by about a fifth (the hypothesis above), though not the ERV's return food, whose menu is fixed first; a menu held to the older 1.83 kg would not feed the crew's full energy requirement.

## Autonomy

### What A3 must do

At A3 ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md)), the robots plant, transplant, harvest, and clean trays; hold pH, conductivity, temperature, humidity, and CO₂ within limits; clean and replace pumps, filters, and LED panels; and detect anticipated faults such as root disease, algae in channels, a pump failure, or a stuck valve. Maintenance, which took most of the EDEN ISS operator's time [6], is the bulk of the A3 workload. Disease gets scripted isolation, disposal, and sterilization of the affected channels. Separate controllers, independent of the Orchestrator, run the lamp overtemperature cutoffs and, once the crew is present, the CO₂ limits.

### What A4 adds

An A4 system can handle what the procedures did not foresee: a new pathogen, a nutrient imbalance with an unfamiliar signature, or a crop that behaves differently in Mars gravity. It can change the planting plan to raise yield per photon, test spectra and photoperiods, redesign channels after a failure, and run the regolith trials as real experiments.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could breed cultivars for the colony's light, pressure, and gravity, design a soil ecosystem that passes planetary protection review, and close the fertilizer loop, the path from a greenhouse that supplements stored food to one that replaces it.

## Risks

[Appendix A](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md) carries the full register; the food system's risks fall under [R33](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r33) unless noted.

- If the Mars greenhouse behaves like EDEN ISS, with most of its power going to climate control, the calorie share falls from about a quarter to under a tenth, which the H1 phase measures by 2040.
- Vitamin B1 and C losses in stored food [2] could leave the crew short if the greenhouse also fails; supplements, cold storage, and late delivery of the reserve reduce the risk. On the way home the ERV's food rests on its seven-year qualification and supplements alone.
- A root pathogen or algae bloom in a closed hydroponic system can take out a whole crop; staggered plantings, separate channel loops, and seed reserves limit the loss.
- A reactor trip during a long dust storm before reactor 3 is commissioned in January 2041, or the loss of two reactors in one, could force the lights down for weeks; crop survival at reduced light is untested for these cultivars, and replanting costs at least one cycle.
- No crop has been grown in real or perchlorate-bearing Martian soil; regolith-grown food stays out of the diet until tests show it safe ([R20](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r20)).
- Deliberate microbes and a large wet biomass raise forward-contamination questions ([R09](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r09)).

None of these risks leaves the crew unfed, because the shelf-stable reserve carries the full diet.

## Open questions

- What lamp share and kilowatts per square meter can a Mars greenhouse reach in practice, inside the colony's heat loop?
- How do staple crops yield at Mars gravity (0.38 g) and at the habitat pressure chosen in [chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)?
- How much water does leaching take per kilogram of regolith at the chosen site, and how fast do perchlorate-reducing bacteria work at habitat temperatures?
- Can cold storage keep vitamin B1 and C adequate for up to about five years, on Mars and in the ERV?
- How much food must the crew's menu ship per person per day: the ISS's 2.39 kg, or about 1.9 kg if more of it is freeze-dried and rehydrated with the colony's water, and does such a menu keep, and stay eaten, for three to five years?
- What feeds the crew home if a slip or a missed window leaves the ERV's food 6.2–6.9 years old: a seven-year qualification, which would have to start by mid-2034 to report by G4, or fresh food delivered to the ERV?
