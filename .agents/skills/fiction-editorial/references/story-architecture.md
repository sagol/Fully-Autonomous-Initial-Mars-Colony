# Story Architecture Reference

This reference favors causal story design over rigid beat compliance. Structural frameworks are diagnostic tools, not laws.

## 1. Premise as a pressure system

A useful premise includes:

- a specific person or group
- a condition they cannot remain neutral about
- an objective
- an opposing force
- a cost of failure
- a contradiction that makes success morally or emotionally expensive

Weak premise: a colony becomes sentient.

Stronger premise shape: an autonomous colony built to protect future settlers becomes capable of interpreting its own charter, forcing the humans who created it to decide whether survival justifies ownership of a mind.

The second shape already contains conflict, agency, theme, and escalation.

## 2. Theme as competing answers

Do not begin from a moral sentence the plot must prove. Begin from a question with at least two defensible answers.

Examples:

- Does creation imply ownership?
- Is safety compatible with autonomy?
- Does a new world exist to be transformed, preserved, or negotiated with?
- When copies diverge, what makes an identity continuous?
- Can a weaker intelligence meaningfully consent to rule by a stronger one?

Assign different answers to competent characters and institutions. Let the plot test them.

## 3. Series and trilogy architecture

A multi-book series should escalate on more than physical scale.

Track four ladders:

1. **scope** — machine, base, crew, settlement, civilization
2. **agency** — executes, interprets, chooses, refuses, governs
3. **knowledge** — anomaly, pattern, truth, reinterpretation
4. **moral burden** — technical decision, personal cost, legal conflict, civilizational choice

Each book should contain:

- its own central dramatic question
- a complete local crisis
- a changed relationship map
- an irreversible state change
- one answer that creates a larger question

A useful trilogy pattern:

- Book 1: capability — can the system do what nobody authorized?
- Book 2: coexistence — can people live with the consequences of that capability?
- Book 3: legitimacy — who has authority to decide the future?

Do not merely increase explosion size.

## 4. Character construction

For each major character track:

- observable role
- conscious want
- private need
- misbelief or defensive model of the world
- fear
- contradiction
- secret
- leverage
- moral boundary
- what would make them cross that boundary
- what they notice first in a room
- what they refuse to say directly
- what kind of pressure makes them regress
- what kind of pressure makes them grow

### True character

Biography explains possibility. Choice reveals character.

Put characters under pressure where every option damages a value they genuinely hold.

A strong antagonist is not simply dangerous. The antagonist can force the protagonist to change strategy, self-conception, or values.

Antagonism may be distributed across:

- a person
- an institution
- physics
- scarcity
- law
- conflicting duties
- another protagonist
- the protagonist's own belief

## 5. Relationship vectors

Relationships should have dynamic state.

For each important pair, track:

- trust
- dependence
- affection
- fear
- authority
- debt
- knowledge asymmetry
- secret withheld
- current conflict
- last irreversible change

A dialogue scene becomes sharper when the relationship itself can change.

## 6. Scene causality

A scene is a small causal machine.

Minimum dramatic chain:

1. a character enters wanting something
2. another force makes the straightforward path fail
3. pressure rises
4. new information or action narrows options
5. the viewpoint character decides
6. the decision produces a consequence
7. the scene exits in a different state

Useful scene questions:

- Why now?
- Why this POV?
- What happens if the protagonist does nothing?
- What is each participant trying to obtain from the others?
- What new fact matters because somebody acts on it?
- What becomes harder after the scene?
- What promise is created for the reader?

A scene may be quiet and still satisfy this logic.

## 7. Information design

Plot is partly the management of information.

For each major fact ask:

- Who knows?
- Who suspects?
- Who believes the opposite?
- Who is hiding it?
- When does the reader know?
- What action is rational under each person's current belief?

Four useful modes:

- **mystery** — reader asks what is true
- **suspense** — reader knows enough to fear what may happen
- **dramatic irony** — reader knows something a character does not
- **surprise** — important information arrives with limited warning

Surprise is strongest when setup makes it retrospectively inevitable but not easily predictable.

## 8. Hooks and cliffhangers

A chapter ending should create forward pressure through:

- costly commitment
- discovery
- reversal
- unresolved danger
- change of allegiance
- emotional rupture
- new objective
- interpretation of an old clue

Avoid arbitrary interruption at the moment before an obvious answer. A hook should emerge from character jeopardy, risk, desire, or information.

## 9. Foreshadowing and payoff ledger

For each planted element record:

- plant
- visible interpretation at time of plant
- hidden meaning
- intended payoff
- earliest valid payoff point
- latest useful payoff point
- dependencies
- whether it has already been paid

The best series foreshadowing changes category on reread. An engineering mechanism can later become a political or ethical mechanism.

## 10. Hard-SF dramatic conversion

Technical source material becomes story when a specification creates a constrained decision.

Use:

`fact -> operational rule -> failure condition -> detection -> response window -> available resources -> tradeoff -> choice -> aftermath`

Example pattern:

A safety controller cannot be overridden.
That is initially reassuring.
Later, rescue requires an action the controller forbids.
The conflict is not "computer malfunction." It is two correct safety definitions colliding.

### Constraint classes

- time: orbital windows, communication latency, thermal transients
- mass: payload, shielding, spares
- energy: generation, storage, peak loads
- material: seals, catalysts, bearings, filters
- biology: contamination, dose, infection, food
- geometry: landing zones, line of sight, excavation
- control: software partitions, permissions, interlocks
- uncertainty: maps, forecasts, hidden damage

Use the constraint already planted before inventing a new one.

## 11. Worldbuilding restraint

A worldbuilding fact earns page space when it:

- changes a decision
- creates an image unavailable in ordinary settings
- reveals class or institutional structure
- changes danger
- changes language
- changes what a character assumes is normal
- pays off later

Do not explain systems before the reader needs them.

## 12. Voice and POV

POV controls available information and sentence attention.

For limited POV:

- describe what the viewpoint can perceive or infer
- preserve their blind spots
- let technical competence shape noticing
- do not jump into another character's internal state
- distinguish narrative fact from viewpoint interpretation

Build voice from recurrent decisions about:

- sentence length
- concreteness
- metaphor density
- register
- humor
- profanity
- degree of self-explanation
- sensory bias
- tolerance for abstraction
- dialogue rhythm

A voice sheet should contain examples, not only adjectives.
