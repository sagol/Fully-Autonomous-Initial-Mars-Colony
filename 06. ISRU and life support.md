# 6. ISRU and life support

*Last reviewed: September 28, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, October 2040.* The last few kilograms of methane trickle into the storage tank at 112 K, and two independent level sensors agree: 7.0 t of methane and 22.7 t of oxygen, a full ascent load. Making it took 565 days, 480 of them running: water drawn from buried ice, carbon dioxide frozen out of the air, and hydrogen split from the water and bound back to carbon. In the next tank row sit 5 t of breathing oxygen and about 1.4 t of nitrogen and argon, half of it left over when the plant froze out its carbon dioxide and half from the small capture unit on the oxygen line's intake. The ascent vehicle that will carry this load landed last month and waits at its pad, and the plant is already starting the lifeboat load. The crew who will ride it are still in training.

[Chapter 5](05.%20Power.md) gives the colony up to 300 kWe of electricity and several hundred kilowatts of reactor waste heat. In situ resource utilization (ISRU) turns that energy and local material into supplies. On Mars the raw materials are buried ice and an atmosphere of about 95% carbon dioxide. From them the colony makes water, breathing oxygen, rocket propellant, and the inert gas a crew needs to dilute its oxygen. Life support closes the loop: it recycles water and air once people arrive, and draws on ISRU stockpiles for the rest.

In brief: Rodwell wells melt buried ice with reactor waste heat, and solid oxide electrolysis makes oxygen from 2036. A methane and oxygen plant at Mars ascent vehicle (MAV) scale, producing from April 2039, fills one ascent load by October 2040 and a lifeboat load by May 2042. Its carbon dioxide capture step also yields nitrogen and argon for the habitat, and a small capture unit makes the rest of the buffer gas the crew gate needs. It then runs on to make the next crew's two loads by about mid-2045, and after that restarts only to replace methane that cooling outages vent from the MAV. The habitat runs at 70 kPa with 26.5% oxygen, and its life support runs for at least 12 months with simulated crew loads before anyone leaves Earth.

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) sets the production targets and the stockpiles the colony must hold, each verified by independent sensors, at the crew launch decision in August 2043; [chapter 5](05.%20Power.md) supplies the power and heat. The water need is about 830 t, most of it for the ~650 t habitat shield. An oxygen line makes 2 kg/h from June 2036, and a propellant plant makes 7.0 t of methane (CH₄) and 22.7 t of oxygen (O₂) per MAV load in 480 days, two loads in all. The habitat must have held its operating atmosphere (70 kPa, 26.5% O₂) for at least 12 months, leaking no more than 1 kg per sol.

## Two raw materials: buried ice and CO₂ air

### Buried ice

NASA's Subsurface Water Ice Mapping (SWIM) project combines orbital neutron, thermal, radar, and imaging data into maps of how consistent each area is with ice in the top meter or so, across the midlatitudes [1]. The maps "can hint at buried ice but can't outright confirm its presence or quantity" [1]. Exposed scarps elsewhere in the midlatitudes show ice sheets more than 100 m thick, starting as little as 1–2 m below the surface [30]. Arcadia Planitia, the reference site, ranks well on the SWIM maps. Ground truth comes only from drilling, and [chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md) makes confirmed ice at well depth a condition of the site choice.

### Air that is 95% carbon dioxide

The Sample Analysis at Mars mass spectrometer on Curiosity measured the composition over several Mars years: carbon dioxide about 95%, nitrogen 2.59%, argon 1.94%, oxygen 0.16%, and carbon monoxide 0.06%, with seasonal swings as CO₂ freezes onto and sublimates from the poles [2]. The colony's sensors track local composition and pressure, which varies with season and elevation; at the reference site it is about 840–870 Pa ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)).

## Water from Rodwells

### How a Rodwell works

A Rodriguez well, or Rodwell, is a hole drilled into ice in which recirculating hot water melts a growing cavity and a pump lifts the melt to the surface. The U.S. Army Cold Regions Research and Engineering Laboratory (CRREL) developed Rodwells for Greenland camps in the early 1960s, and the South Pole Station now uses its third [3]. With more heat than withdrawal, the cavity grows; with less, the well "collapses" and stops producing liquid water [3].

On Mars a Rodwell needs a drill to cut through the overburden and set a casing, a drill or melt probe into the ice, and a heat source [3]. The casing lets the cavity be sealed above the triple-point pressure of water, so the pool stays liquid [3]. NASA's reference concept, on a pressurized-rover chassis, came to about 4.8 t including water filtration and ultraviolet sterilization [3].

### Model figures and their status

NASA and CRREL simulations found that about 10 kWt of heat sustains a withdrawal of 379 kg (100 U.S. gallons) per day from ice at −80 °C, and that with Martian heat-transfer terms the balance point falls to about 9 kWt [3]. These are the figures behind [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s estimate of about 0.38 m³/day and 10 kWt per well. They are model results, which the authors call "provisional" pending experiments on the Martian parameters [3].

Those experiments followed. In the Mars Rodwell Experiment at Johnson Space Center, water pools exposed to CO₂ at 8–1,000 mbar (0.8–100 kPa) and −20 to −40 °C showed that pools "can be formed under ambient surface conditions on Mars" [4]; that work fed the model but did not build a Rodwell [4], [5]. The closest hardware is Honeybee Robotics' RedWater, which pairs coiled-tubing drilling with a Rodwell and reached technology readiness level (TRL) 5 in a Mars-simulating chamber [6]. Later end-to-end runs in Honeybee's thermal-vacuum chamber, with ice near −60 °C at 5–7.5 torr (670–1,000 Pa), delivered liquid water outside the chamber [31], and its developers now rate the system TRL 6 [32]. Nothing has flown.

> Assumption: Each well produces about 0.38 m³ of water per day (about 0.39 m³ per sol) on about 10 kWt of heat. Justification: NASA/CRREL modeling [3], [5]; one reactor alone rejects 300–400 kWt ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)), 4 to 13 times what the wells need including line losses, depending on the epoch, so a well that needs twice the modeled heat still runs.

### Once the wells work, water is not the constraint

Chapter 2's well plan, at the 85% availability this book plans on, banks about 530–590 t of water by October 2038, before the habitat lands, and four wells then keep the ~650 t shield filling. By September 2040 the colony has made about 1,370 t, all that the shield and the bladders can hold; the wells throttle whenever storage is full, and Wave 3 adds bladders for more ([chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md#filling-the-shield)). The capacity of 4–6 wells to 2043 is about 2,900–3,500 t, three to four times the ~830 t need; the surplus is margin against failed wells, and real extraction is bounded by the ice mapped.

Midlatitude ice may carry dust and dissolved salts, including perchlorates. Water from the wells therefore passes through sediment filters, deionization, and ultraviolet sterilization, as in NASA's concept [3], and electrolysis feed gets a further electrodeionization polish, as in the Kleinhenz and Paz plant model [7]. Shield water needs little treatment and drinking water the most; each stream is held until its sensors agree.

The heat comes from the reactors through a glycol-water loop; chapter 5 checks its temperature, its losses, and a reactor trip.

## Fallback water from hydrated minerals

If the wells fail, the colony can bake water out of hydrated minerals. Kleinhenz and Paz modeled this route for a MAV-scale plant. Typical regolith, as at Gale crater, releases about 1.3% water by mass at 300 °C; a sulfate-rich deposit about 8% at 150 °C [7]. The baseline case needed about 17 kW of heat to extract the 15.7 t of water for one MAV load in 480 days [7], about 12 kWh of heat per kilogram of water.

The fallback covers the propellant, reserve, and start-up water, about 180 t in total. At 1.3% that means excavating and heating about 14,000 t of regolith; at 8%, about 2,300 t. Brayton waste heat above 400 K reaches the 150 °C (423 K) needed for sulfates only at the hottest end of the cycle, and the 300 °C needed for typical regolith would take electric or direct reactor heat. The fallback cannot supply the ~650 t habitat shield. A site without workable ice means a different habitat, shielded by regolith, as [chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md) discusses.

The hardware has not advanced since NASA's 2018 status report, which had breadboard extractors in fabrication and test [8]. That Mars work stopped when NASA turned its ISRU effort toward the Moon in 2017; the auger dryer breadboard was reused for a lunar ice extractor, and no Mars subsystem test has been reported since [33].

## Oxygen from carbon dioxide

### What MOXIE proved

The Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment (MOXIE) on the Perseverance rover made oxygen on Mars by solid oxide electrolysis (SOXE): hot ceramic cells at about 800 °C split CO₂ into oxygen and carbon monoxide. Over 16 runs from 2021 to August 2023 it produced 122 g of oxygen, up to 12 g/h, twice its requirement, at 98% purity or better [9]. It suffered no failures on Mars [10].

Two of the team's lessons shape a full-scale plant [10]. Compressor power "currently dominates the MOXIE power budget," so a production plant should run at the lowest possible cathode pressure. MOXIE compressed Mars air from 6.3–7.7 mbar (630–770 Pa) to 0.5–1 bar (50–100 kPa); the present stack limits inlet pressure to about 100 mbar (10 kPa), and a redesign could lower that by another order of magnitude [10], [11]. And "with careful operation, the technology is surprisingly robust against thermal cycling, dust, changes in atmospheric density and temperature" [10]; a simple baffle removed nearly all dust from the flow. The team tracked stack health with a figure of merit called intrinsic area-specific resistance (iASR), derived from current-voltage sweeps.

### The full-scale plant

A production plant needs 2–3 kg/h, "several hundred times" MOXIE's rate [11]. The MOXIE team expects its next step to be "a full-scale system, powered with at least 20 kW," filling a tank with "up to 30 tons of oxygen" [10]. NASA's plant model put an oxygen-only SOXE system for 22.7 t in 480 days, 1.97 kg/h, at 0.9 t of hardware and 34 kW [7]. Thermodynamics sets the floor. Splitting CO₂ takes about 4.9 kWh of heat per kilogram of oxygen at the thermoneutral point (where the cell neither heats nor cools itself) and about 3.3 kWh of electricity at the minimum. For 2 kg/h that is 6.6–9.8 kW in the stack alone, before compression, heating, and liquefaction. [Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s 25–34 kW sits between the MOXIE team's lower bound and NASA's model.

> Assumption: The oxygen line is one 2 kg/h plant of about 1 t on 25–34 kW, running at low cathode pressure with a redesigned stack, recuperative heat exchange between exhaust and feed, and an insulated, actively cooled compressor inlet. Justification: MOXIE lessons learned [10]; NASA's 34 kW model [7]. At 2 kg/h it makes about 17.5 t per Earth year.

The cathode exhaust is carbon monoxide in unconverted CO₂, nitrogen, and argon. The CO could be made into methane only after separation from the CO₂ [12], so the baseline vents it; CO is already a trace gas in the Martian air [2].

### What the oxygen line is for

The oxygen line lands with Wave 1 in early 2036 and is commissioned at low duty on solar power; it runs at full rate from about June 2036, once reactor 1 reaches 100 kWe, almost three years before the propellant plant. Its first product is the 5 t breathing reserve. Its second is a full MAV load of oxygen, 22.7 t, which takes about 1.3 Earth years at full rate. Together these fill the oxygen tanks by early 2038.

With that oxygen made, failed wells or a slow methane plant would leave only the methane to import, on the 2041 support flight ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md) gives the option). NASA's architecture baselined the same split, oxygen-only ISRU with methane brought from Earth [7]. Once its tanks are full, the line runs at low duty to keep the stacks proven and to top up losses. In a power shortage it is the second load shed ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)).

Filling those tanks by early 2038 means Wave 1 must carry its own oxygen liquefier and storage, since the propellant plant's tanks arrive only in October 2038. The ~28 t of liquid oxygen needs about 25 m³ of tankage. By this book's estimate, liquefying 2 kg/h (cooling from ambient and condensing at 90 K) takes about 0.2 kW of cooling, or 1.4–5 kWe of cryocooler input at the efficiencies given below.

> Assumption: The Wave 1 oxygen liquefier and about 25 m³ of oxygen tankage, in four vacuum-jacketed tanks with the tank farm's vacuum pump (below), are allocated from Wave 1's ~134 t of unitemized mass (chapter 2); their mass is not yet designed, and the jackets alone add about 0.4–0.6 t if built for Mars's pressure and evacuated in cruise, or about 1.3–1.9 t if built for Earth's air (this book's estimate from NASA's figures [36]). Liquefaction power is counted inside the oxygen line's 25–34 kW, and holding power inside chapter 2's keep-alive and cryogenic cooling allocation.

### Plasma splitting: a research line

Cold plasmas can split CO₂ at low temperature and pressure [24]. Laboratory runs made about 47 g/h of oxygen at 0.021 kWh/g [25] and about 76 g/h at 0.013 kWh/g at 25 mbar (2.5 kPa) and 1 kW [26], against about 0.017 kWh/g for NASA's SOXE model [7]. But the plasma oxygen is still mixed with CO and CO₂, and separating it costs more energy and has not been shown at scale [24], [26]. The same plasmas fix nitrogen into nitrogen oxides, 1.25 g/h at 0.79 kWh/g [25], a possible fertilizer feedstock for [chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md). At TRL 2–4, plasma ISRU stays out of the baseline.

## Methane and oxygen for the ascent vehicle

### The chemistry: methane sets the rate

The propellant plant follows NASA's reference design by Kleinhenz and Paz [7]. It freezes CO₂ out of the air, splits water into hydrogen and oxygen by electrolysis, and combines hydrogen with CO₂ in a Sabatier reactor:

CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O

The product water goes back to the electrolyzer, where 4H₂O → 4H₂ + 2O₂. The net reaction is

CO₂ + 2H₂O → CH₄ + 2O₂

so the plant makes oxygen and methane in a 4:1 mass ratio (64:16) and consumes 2.25 kg of water per kilogram of methane [7]. Recycling the Sabatier water halves the demand on the wells; electrolysis still handles 4.5 kg of water per kilogram of methane. An engine burns less oxygen than the plant makes. NASA's ascent vehicle runs at 3:1–3.5:1, and SpaceX's Raptor at about 3.6:1, so the plant always overproduces oxygen and "methane production is the driving requirement" [7].

### One load every 480 days on 35–52 kW

For one load in 480 days of continuous operation, the plant must make 0.61 kg/h of methane from 1.36 kg/h of water and 1.67 kg/h of CO₂, and it produces 2.43 kg/h of oxygen [7]. Per load, the balance is:

| Stream | Per MAV load | Notes |
|---|---:|---|
| Water in (from wells) | 15.7 t | After recycling Sabatier water [7] |
| CO₂ in (frozen from the air) | 19.2 t | [7] |
| Methane out | 7.0 t | One MAV load [7] |
| Oxygen out | 27.9 t | 22.7 t to the MAV, 5.2 t surplus, enough for the breathing reserve [7] |
| Nitrogen and argon out | ~0.7 t | By-product of CO₂ capture (see below) |
| Power and time | 35–52 kW, 480 days | Chapter 2 allocation |

NASA's model sized the whole plant, including three parallel modules and liquefaction, at 1.7 t and 52 kW when water comes from heated regolith; 17 kW of that is regolith heating, and with heat recovered from a reactor the draw falls to 35 kW [7]. With water from Rodwells, the colony's plant sits near the lower end of [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s 35–52 kW allocation, plus a few kilowatts for water cleanup.

The Sabatier reactor is the best-understood step; NASA's design study sized one unit at about 0.34 kg/h of methane [13], so a plant needs two to three. Catalyst durability is less well known. Short challenge tests at NASA exposed nickel and ruthenium catalysts to vibration, liquid water, Mars dust simulant, and chemical contaminants for about an hour each and found "minimal change" [14]. Nobody has yet measured how these catalysts age over years of continuous operation.

> Assumption: This book's working assumption is that the plant carries two spare Sabatier catalyst beds and one spare electrolysis stack for each module, with a guard bed ahead of each reactor, as the station's Sabatier team recommends [53]. The Orchestrator trends each bed's temperature profile, conversion, and pressure drop, and each stack's cell voltages, to decide when to swap them. Justification: long-life data are lacking [14]; on the station's record both parts wear out rather than fail at random (below), so the count depends on how many lives fit into the roughly 23,000 hours each module runs for the first crew's two loads, and the 46,000 of all four the plan makes ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#crew-and-after-20432048)). The stack's life is set by its own membranes, which the station has measured; the bed's is set by the feed, which nobody has measured on Mars. Spare beds are light, and without them a worn catalyst could cost the crew date. The alternatives stay open: one of each, which covers the first two loads on a feed no harsher than the station's, or two of each. Switch rule: the Earth endurance run showing a bed life above about 23,000 hours drops the second bed before the plant flies; a life under about 11,500 hours in that run or in the plant's first months, or stack voltages heading for the station's failure signature within one load, adds a bed or a stack per module to Wave 3's manifest before it closes, so that one stays in hand. For the next crew's two loads, the plant's own bed profiles and stack voltages, read at G4 in July 2041 after about 17,000 hours of running and again at the crew gate, set how many beds and stacks the support lander and the crew ship bring.

Read as constant failure rates, the station's record looks thin for one spare of each. NASA's reliability review puts the station electrolyzer's stack, on its flight record, at about 35,000 hours between failures [52], and the station's Sabatier reactor failed once, after about 20,000 hours [53]; over 23,000 hours a module would then need a second stack about one time in seven and a second catalyst bed about one time in three (this book's calculation). But neither part failed at random. The first station stack failed early from a design flaw that a design change cured [52]; its successors ran 5.3 and about 4.5 years of electrolysis before the same cell failed, traced in the first to thinning membranes, and the next showed the same signs after about 2.5 years, at a higher production rate [54], [55]. The Sabatier catalyst died from the front of the bed backward as sulfur and silicon compounds reached it, with fluorine and chlorine at its rear [53]. Parts that wear out give warning, and all three modules wear together, so what matters is how many lives fit into 23,000 hours: one spare covers a life above about 11,500 hours, two a life above about 7,700. Industry points the same way, for the mechanism more than the hours: commercial PEM stacks run about 40,000 hours to a 10% voltage loss [56], and ammonia plants' nickel methanation catalysts, which only remove traces of carbon oxides from hydrogen, a far milder duty than the plant's, commonly last more than ten years, with poisoning "the only significant form of de-activation" [57].

This book's simulation of the two loads, with the spares shared among the modules and the Wave 3 spare modules, finishes load 2 by August 2042 in about 96 runs in 100 with one spare of each at the constant rates, and in 99.5 with a second bed; without the Wave 3 modules the figures are about 70 and 93. With parts that wear out at the station's lives, one of each is enough. A feed that poisons the beds twice as fast as the station's cabin air changes that: one bed per module then finishes on time in about 96 runs in 100 with the Wave 3 modules and 16 without, and two beds in 100 and 96. These wear-out cases spread each part's life about its mean as a Weibull distribution of shape 3, an assumption; with a spread about twice as wide, two beds with the Wave 3 modules still finish in 99 runs in 100 or more, but without them one of each at the station's lives finishes in 84–89 and two beds at the twice-as-poisonous feed in 74 (this book's calculation). A module waiting for its part leaves the other two at 80% of the rate, enough to finish both loads before the decision ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#schedule-logic-what-slips-and-by-how-much)). The spare process modules that chapter 13's working assumption puts on Wave 3 add a stack and a bed for each module from about March 2041. The support lander, whose manifest G4 fixes in July 2041, lands only after load 2 is due, so it restocks a slow load 2 and the methane the plant remakes later. The next crew's two loads double each module's running time to about 46,000 hours. On the same spares with the Wave 3 modules, the fourth load is finished by the next crew's decision in October 2047 in about 99 runs in 100 at the station's lives, and by September 2045, in time even for a next crew in the 2045/46 window, in 96 or more; at the constant rates, or with a spread about twice as wide, those figures fall to about 90–93 and 80–85, and three more stacks and three more beds on the crew ship raise them to about 99 and 92–93. A feed twice as poisonous as the station's leaves the fourth load unfinished at the October 2047 decision in about 9 runs in 10, unless the support lander brings about six more beds and three stacks, which lift it to about 94 or more (this book's calculation). Both station failures came from causes a fresh part would meet again, a design flaw in the electrolyzer and poisons in the Sabatier reactor's feed [52], [53], so a spare buys time only until the cause is found and fixed.

> Hypothesis: The plant's catalyst beds outlast the station's 20,000 hours, because the Mars feed lacks the station's worst poisons. Basis: the station's bed was killed mainly by sulfur and silicon, from a compound in the crew's urine carried over in the electrolyzer's hydrogen and from siloxanes in the cabin air, with fluorine from an overheated seal [53]. The plant's water comes from ice through deionization, and its CO₂ from the Mars air, whose hydrogen chloride, 1–4 parts per billion in the dusty season [58], would bring at most about a tenth of a gram of chlorine to each module's beds over all four loads even if all of it froze out with the CO₂, against 0.2–0.65 g in each poisoned section of the station's bed (this book's calculation). No sulfur gas has been detected in that air, but the upper limits set from orbit, 20 parts per billion for SO₂ and 15 for H₂S [59], would allow up to about 0.7 g of sulfur per module over the four loads on the same assumption, about as much poison as one to three sections of the station's bed held (this book's calculation). The plant has poisons of its own: its PEM stacks shed fluoride, which the station traps in an ion-exchange bed [55], and dust must be kept out by filters, as the station's 2-micron CO₂ filter kept out its zeolite dust [53]. Test: the Earth endurance run of a flight-like module through one load's 480 days, reported by G2 in June 2037 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)), on Mars-simulant CO₂ with its trace gases, sulfur gases at those limits among them, and dust, and on well-grade water, logging the bed's temperature profile; then the plant's own profiles from April 2039, read before Wave 3's manifest closes. A bed life under the station's 20,000 hours refutes it.

### 15 months of schedule margin

The plant lands with Wave 2 in October 2038, together with its cryogenic storage tanks, about 20 t in all. After about six months of commissioning it starts producing in April 2039, running on the second reactor's power. At the 85% availability this book plans on, catalyst and stack swaps and reactor trips included, each load takes about 565 days: load 1 completes by about late October 2040, a month after the MAV lands with Wave 3 in September 2040, and load 2, the lifeboat reserve, by about May 2042 (chapter 2). That leaves roughly 15 months before the crew launch decision in August 2043 to absorb a slow start, a repair, or a lower rate. The water for both loads, about 32 t, is three weeks of output from four wells.

## Cryogenic storage and zero boil-off

Liquid oxygen boils at 90 K and methane at 112 K. By the crew gate the colony holds two MAV loads, one inside the MAV from late 2040, where it stays about five years, to the ascent in November 2045, plus 5 t of breathing oxygen. At liquid densities of about 1,140 and 420 kg/m³, that is roughly 20 m³ of oxygen and 17 m³ of methane per load, about 78 m³ in all. The plant's storage tanks therefore need to hold only one load, about 37 m³, because load 1 moves into the MAV as load 2 begins; the next crew's two loads go into two more tank sets ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#crew-and-after-20432048)).

NASA's plant model deliberately left out storage tanks and "storage maintenance systems (eg. zero boil-off systems)," because it liquefied propellant straight into the vehicle's tanks [7]. A NASA Glenn concept does that: cold gas is liquefied inside the ascent vehicle's own tank by a cryocooler-chilled network of tubes on the tank wall, which then holds the tank at zero boil-off [15].

The gap is cryocooler capacity. As of 2021, flight cryocoolers at 90 K did not exceed 20 W of cooling, and no flight unit at any temperature had exceeded 100 W [16]. Since then Creare has built a flight-like 90 K turbo-Brayton cooler that lifts over 120 W and has passed vibration testing; it is ground hardware at about TRL 5, and none has flown [34], [35].

Life is the other gap. Small coolers of the flexure-bearing kind have shown it: NASA's 2020 survey of long-life space cryocoolers counts nine that had run near or past 20 years without stopping, and of its 63 units only one or two ever failed to give their instrument the cooling it needed [39]. The two pulse tubes on NASA's AIRS instrument have run since 2002 with their drive level up only 2–3% [40]. The turbo-Brayton type that propellant tanks need has flown twice. Hubble's NICMOS cooler, built by Creare like the 90 K unit, ran 57,000 hours, about 6.5 years, until its coupling to the instrument failed [37], [39]: after a planned shutdown in 2008 it would not restart, most likely because water ice had gathered in the pump of the loop that carried its cold to the instrument [50]. Three units cooling a freezer on the space station to 190 K had run 82,000–120,000 hours each without degradation [39]. One failure in about 350,000 unit-hours is about 2.5% a year per unit; with a single failure on record, the true rate could be as much as four times higher (this book's analysis of the survey [39]). Tanks held cold by cryocoolers have a far shorter record: 156 hours of zero boil-off for an 18 m³ hydrogen tank at NASA Marshall [41], 19 days without venting for a nitrogen tank standing in for oxygen at NASA Glenn [42], and four months in orbit for 19 kg of methane on the space station, until the cooler's electronics failed [34]. The 90 K unit has run 31 days at NASA Glenn [43], and NASA lists a multiyear life test, and redundant coolers shown to take over from one another, as gaps to close before a crewed vehicle relies on them [44].

The nearest large record on the ground points the same way. The eight helium plants that cool CERN's Large Hadron Collider run expansion turbines on gas bearings, and in about 2.4 million hours of running eight turbines had to be replaced, most for damaged bearings, where impurities from liquefied gases to dust were found; breakdowns came more often around restarts [51]. That is about 3% a year per machine, close to the flown turbo-Brayton rate. A turbo-Brayton cooler has two high-speed machines, a compressor and a turbine [45], plus its drive electronics, so a cooler whose parts failed like CERN's would fail about 6–7% a year (this book's calculation).

> Hypothesis: A 100 W-class turbo-Brayton cryocooler that runs without stopping fails at about 2.5–7% a year per unit, and more of its failures come from contamination and restarts than from wear. Basis: the two flown turbo-Brayton designs, one failure in about 350,000 unit-hours, and that one after a shutdown [39], [50]; CERN's gas-bearing turbines at about 3% a year per machine, mostly bearing damage with impurities found [51] (this book's calculation). The ground analog is large plant with filters, utilities, and repair crews; a third of its turbine failures came from degraded filters that a sealed cooler lacks, but NICMOS shows that a sealed loop can gather ice too, so how far the analog carries is unknown. Test: a ground life test of several flight-build units at the ERV's and the MAV's duty, with planned stops, restarts, and power losses, run by NASA and the supplier. It must start by about 2038 to report three years at G4 in July 2041, and an earlier start shows more: four units from 2032 would give G4 about 38 unit-years, and no failure in them would hold the rate below about 5% a year at 90% confidence, counting the flight record (this book's calculation). A failure mode that every unit shares would show there first.

> Assumption: Every storage tank, and each of the MAV's own tanks, holds its heat leak to 1–2 W/m² inside a welded metal vacuum jacket over multilayer insulation, as industrial liquid-oxygen tanks do and as NASA's 2015 design already puts on the ascent vehicle's tanks, inside its dry mass [65], giving 0.2–0.4 kW of cooling at 90–112 K across all tanks, which takes 1.5–10 kWe of cryocooler input; Wave 3's two further tank sets for the next crew's loads ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#crew-and-after-20432048)) raise both to about twice that by 2045 (this book's calculation). A jacket built for Earth's air is evacuated before launch; one built only for Mars's pressure, the lighter kind, is purged with nitrogen for launch, opened to space in cruise, and sealed before landing, as in NASA's lighter design [36]. Either kind keeps its vacuum with a getter, and small vacuum pumps at the tank farm and in the MAV's pad kit restore a jacket that leaks. Justification: multilayer insulation needs a hard vacuum and works poorly in 600 Pa of CO₂, where gas conduction dominates [36]; in NASA's analysis of tanks on the Mars surface, only insulation sealed from the air reached 0.3–2.7 W/m², against 8–10 W/m² for aerogel or foam open to it [36]. Industrial vacuum-jacketed oxygen tanks of 6–35 m³ boil off 0.1–0.35% of their contents a day [60], about 1.5–3.2 W/m² at Earth's temperatures and about 0.3–1.9 W/m² against Mars ground at 200–214 K (this book's calculation), so the planning value sits toward the conservative end of that evidence, but not for the smallest tanks: those of Wave 1's size, about 6 m³ each, scale to 0.6–1.9 W/m² (this book's calculation). Cryocoolers need 7–25 W of input per watt of cooling at these temperatures, the low end for high-capacity turbo-Brayton units now in ground test [37], [38], and the 90 K unit drew 15 W per watt on the ground and is projected at about 7.4 in flight [45]. Liquefaction during production is already inside the plant power.

That target holds only while the jackets hold their vacuum. NASA's analysis of 50 mm of aerogel and multilayer insulation open to the Mars air gives about 10 W/m², and 81 mm of foam under multilayer insulation about the same [36]. NASA's later tests found that aerogel soaks up CO₂, which raises its conductivity [36], [61], and NASA's 2025 concept for moving propellant on Mars insulates its tanks with foam for that reason, inside the landers' insulated nose shrouds, where the two 150 W coolers running on each tank cap its heat leak at about 3–4 W/m² [48] (this book's calculation). Tanks with foam or aerogel open to the air would need five to ten times the cooling of jacketed ones. [Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) carries the planned load inside its keep-alive and cryogenic cooling allocation (8–12 kW in 2038–2040) and runs the open-insulation case as a sensitivity: with coolers of the size that load needs, about 15–30 kWe of storage cooling would fall outside that allocation, and the critical load in 2039–2040, about 36–52 kW, would stay inside the 100 kWe firm level with one reactor out. For Wave 1's oxygen tanks in the first epoch, the same case takes about 5–14 kWe instead of 0.4–4.5; it costs construction about 70–160 MWh, which the first epoch's reserve and the Wave 3 core work begun early cover ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)), and, after a reactor trip in a storm, oxygen that boils off while tank cooling is shed, which the oxygen line makes again (chapter 2). A jacket that loses its vacuum and cannot be pumped back down is worse than foam: multilayer insulation in gas at the site's pressure conducts about 150 times as much heat as in vacuum [62], some 30–60 W/m², more than the coolers can remove; Wave 1's store in a single tank would boil off at about 0.7–2.2 t a day (this book's calculation). This book therefore splits Wave 1's store into four tanks, so that a failed jacket costs at most one tank's oxygen, about 7 t, which the oxygen line makes again in about 4.7 months at full rate. If the Earth test below reads well above 2 W/m² before Wave 1's manifest freezes, about 2.5 t more of solar array on Wave 1, some 120–140 MWh over the first epoch, would pay for most or all of the extra cooling instead of the Wave 3 work (this book's calculation).

> Hypothesis: A welded metal jacket keeps its vacuum longer on Mars than on Earth, and a small pump can restore one that leaks, so the tanks stay within 1–2 W/m² for the years the colony stores propellant. Basis: at the site's 840–870 Pa a leak lets in at least 100 times less gas than at Earth's pressure, and about 95% of that gas is CO₂, which freezes on an oxygen tank's 90–95 K wall with a vapor pressure far below the 10⁻³ torr (0.13 Pa) above which multilayer insulation degrades quickly [62]; there only the nitrogen, argon, oxygen, and carbon monoxide, about 5% of Mars air [2], spoil the vacuum, so the same leak spoils the jacket some 3,000 times more slowly than on Earth, while the gas given off inside the jacket is the same as on Earth (this book's calculation), where makers warrant the vacuum of industrial tanks for five years [60]. A methane tank gains less: CO₂ frost on its 112 K wall holds about 0.6 Pa, some five times that threshold, so CO₂ that leaks into its jacket degrades the insulation until the pump or a getter that takes up CO₂ removes it (this book's interpolation between measurements at 85–92 K [66] and 154–196 K [67]). NASA's analysis finds that a jacket sealed by a valve leaking 10⁻⁶ standard cm³ of helium a second would reach only about 3 × 10⁻⁴ torr in 500 sols, where multilayer insulation begins to degrade but stays far from soft vacuum [36]. In 2021 NASA was developing light jackets for Mars tanks with two companies; the one tested on a 400-liter tank did better than required, though it leaked at its welds and ran with a vacuum pump attached, and NASA names welds and seams failing under day-night cycling among the risks [61]; the maker reports 0.19 W/m² between 105 and 210 K in hard vacuum and in Mars conditions [64]. NASA's programs for light jackets since the 1970s all failed, mainly by leaking, when they strayed from solid metal vessels [63]. Test: an Earth test of flight-like jacketed tanks of a few cubic meters, one at oxygen and one at methane temperature, in CO₂ at the site's pressure, their walls cycled through Mars's 150–225 K day [61], for six months or more with a deliberate leak and a pump-down, run by NASA's cryogenic laboratories and the tanks' maker and reported by about 2032, before the Wave 1 manifest freezes; then Wave 1's own tanks, whose cooler power and jacket pressure the Orchestrator logs from mid-2036, read at G2 in June 2037, and the plant's methane tanks from April 2039. A heat leak above about 2 W/m² with the vacuum intact, or a jacket that leaks faster than the pump restores it, would refute it.

> Assumption: The MAV's tanks are cooled from its pad, through tubing on their walls, by two 90 K turbo-Brayton coolers per tank, either of which holds its tank alone at 1–2 W/m²: about 45–90 W for the oxygen and 40–80 W for the methane, within NASA's own worst case of just under 100 W per MAV tank [47]. All four run at part load, together about 3–4 kWe inside [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s keep-alive and cryogenic cooling line, and three more wait on the shelf; the robots swap a failed unit in days while its partner carries the tank. At about 0.1 t each with their electronics [45], [46], the seven come to about 0.7 t from Wave 3's unitemized mass. Justification: in the seven-year case, with load 1 aboard from November 2040 to about December 2047, each running cooler runs about 62,000 hours. At the flown turbo-Brayton rate of about 2.5% a year, the four running units and their landing expect about 0.8 failures in that time, and three spares cover the case 99 times in 100 (this book's calculation); NASA's concept for moving propellant on Mars likewise gives its tanks two running coolers and a spare [48].

That count rests on a thin record, but the shelf does not have to last seven years alone. The support lander in September 2042 and the 2045/46 food flight both carry spares, which can include coolers, and G4 fixes the support lander's manifest in July 2041, after the pad's first eight months ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). If each landing restores the shelf to three, it stays stocked through December 2047 about 998 times in 1,000 at the flown rate, and about 997 times through an ascent in April 2050, if an ERV cooler failure holds the crew two windows and load 1 waits about 9.4 years ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#schedule-logic-what-slips-and-by-how-much); this book's calculation). At the upper rate the record allows, about 10% a year, a support lander that restores it to five keeps it stocked 98 times in 100, where a single delivery would need seven spares for 99 in 100. Spares do not cover a fault shared by every unit of the design, because the units on the shelf share it too. Counted at the one-in-ten share of failures that NASA's risk-assessment guide gives such common causes when a design has no record of its own [49], that fault lowers these figures to about 98 and 92 in 100, and more spares would barely raise them (this book's calculation), so finding it before the coolers fly is the life test's main job. An empty shelf after ordinary failures leaves one tank on a single cooler until the next landing, not a warm tank. At NASA's 10 W/m² the tanks need nine running coolers drawing about 10–13 kWe; counting only failures that strike one unit at a time, with the shelf restored at each landing, four spares cover the case 99 times in 100 at the flown rate and nine at the upper rate, against five and thirteen from a single delivery (this book's calculation). The colony's own coolers will add to the record: by the crew gate in August 2043 the pad's four will have logged about eleven unit-years.

A loss of cooling unfolds over days as the tanks warm, and the Orchestrator can vent boil-off to hold pressure, trading a small loss of propellant for time to repair. By this book's estimate, a two-week outage vents about 0.1–0.2 t of the MAV's 7.0 t of methane at the assumed 1–2 W/m², and about 0.9 t at NASA's 10 W/m². Lost oxygen is easy to replace, because the oxygen line runs on and each plant load leaves 5.2 t of surplus. Methane has no surplus, since the plant makes only the loads the plan needs: the first crew's two, and then the next crew's two, in two more tank sets from Wave 3, by about mid-2045 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#crew-and-after-20432048)). Whenever the MAV has lost methane, it is topped up from the storage tanks by one trip of the mobile tank that carried its load to the pad ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md#the-mars-ascent-vehicle)), and the plant, running or restarted, remakes what was lost, 0.1–0.9 t in about one to ten weeks at 35–52 kW, with 2.25 kg of water per kilogram, so load 2 stays a full load. The power is there: the plant keeps its line in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s load table, and construction and a larger greenhouse borrow its 35–52 kW only while it idles ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md), [chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)). If the plant has failed and a load of Earth methane has replaced it ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#schedule-logic-what-slips-and-by-how-much)), nothing on Mars can remake methane, and the MAV's coolers must hold the whole load.

## Habitat atmosphere: 70 kPa, chosen for fire safety

NASA's Exploration Atmospheres Working Group (EAWG) weighed fire, decompression sickness, and hypoxia across exploration vehicles [20]. For long-duration surface habitats NASA settled on 8.2 psia (56.5 kPa) at 34% oxygen, an atmosphere designed for frequent extravehicular activity (EVA) with little oxygen prebreathe [20], [21]. The alternative in NASA's experience base is the Space Shuttle's staged atmosphere of 10.2 psia (70.3 kPa) at 26.5% oxygen [20]. The two are physiologically close. Subtracting water vapor at body temperature (6.3 kPa), the inspired oxygen partial pressure is 17.0 kPa at 70.3 kPa and 26.5%, and 17.1 kPa at 56.5 kPa and 34%.

> Assumption: The habitat's reference atmosphere is 70 kPa total (the Shuttle's 70.3 kPa) with 26.5% oxygen, about 18.6 kPa of oxygen and 51.4 kPa of nitrogen-argon buffer. The structure is proof-tested to 101 kPa, and 56.5 kPa at 34% oxygen remains an option. Justification: flammability, the colony's EVA pattern, and buffer gas, as follows; [chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md) adopts the same reference.

Flammability decides it. The EAWG noted that 30% oxygen is "the maximum flammability test level for ISS/Space Shuttle Programs" [20]. At 26.5% the habitat, home to a crew for well over a year, can use materials already qualified against that database. Above 30%, the working group listed polycarbonate windows that "may be lost," radiation-shielding polymers that must be wrapped in nonflammable material, higher costs from restricting commercial equipment, and fire suppression for which there was "no good story" [20].

The EVA argument for 56.5 kPa is weaker here, because robots do most outside work. From 70.3 kPa, the Shuttle's staged protocol put astronauts into a 29.6 kPa suit after at least 12 hours at the lower pressure and 40–75 minutes of oxygen prebreathe in the suit [20], and a small EVA-staging module can hold that pressure without changing the whole habitat. Those EVAs were weightless. For walking EVAs from a lunar habitat at the same 70.3 kPa and 26.5%, the working group estimated that the final in-suit prebreathe may need to grow to about 2.5 hours, and longer with a suit below 29.6 kPa, and it expects walking on Mars to raise decompression risk in the same way [20]. Its model put the risk of decompression sickness on a four-hour walking EVA from this atmosphere into the 29.6 kPa suit, after a 60-minute prebreathe, at about 18%. Its design points that pair a 41.4 kPa (6.0 psia) suit with cabins of 80–91 kPa came to 4–9%, and it recommended that surface suits offer that higher pressure "to enable rapid EVA egress" with the least risk [20]. [Chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#dust-perchlorate-suits-and-suitports) gives the prebreathe for each suit pressure from this cabin, about three to four hours at 29.6 kPa and none to half an hour at 41 kPa, with NASA's 2024 chamber tests of a similar nitrogen cabin, or about five hours and one hour if argon weighs 1.5 times as much as nitrogen, as its hypothesis has it. Chapter 12 takes a suit that walks at about 41 kPa as its working assumption.

The cost in buffer gas is small. The habitat holds about 0.5 t of gas, 0.4 t of it nitrogen and argon. It is first pressurized with filtered Martian gas for its structural pressure hold. By mid-April 2041 it switches to the crew atmosphere, so 0.4 t of buffer gas and about 0.13 t of oxygen must be banked by then. The switch takes the Martian gas's CO₂ out with the habitat's own CO₂ removal in about 80 days, about five months at the unit's design rate, instead of flushing it with crew gas, which would take about 2.6 t ([chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md#pressurized-in-2038-filled-by-2040-proven-by-2042)), so it costs no more buffer gas than the fill itself. After that, the leak-rate specification of 1 kg per sol means up to about 360 kg of makeup gas per year, about three-quarters of it buffer gas.

## Buffer gas as a by-product of CO₂ capture

On Mars the buffer gas is whatever remains after CO₂ is removed from the air: about 57% nitrogen and 43% argon by volume, since the two are 2.59% and 1.94% of the atmosphere [2].

Sridhar, Finn, and Kliss pointed out that a propellant plant does not make buffer gas as a product, and that the need on "each human Mars mission will probably be in the order of metric tons, primarily due to losses during airlock activity" [17]. [Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) sets at least 2 t in storage at the crew launch decision. The habitat draws on the stock before then: about 0.37 t for its first fill in April 2041, and up to about 0.6 t of leakage at the specification limit over the 2.3 years to August 2043. Holding 2 t at the decision therefore means capturing about 3 t by then. The 2 t then covers the crew's stay with room to spare: leakage at the limit is about 0.3 t over the planned 410 days and about 0.9 t over a missed window's 1,190 days, and the rest covers airlock losses, which robotic EVA, small airlocks, and gas recovery pumps keep down.

The propellant plant freezes CO₂ out of the air; what does not freeze is nitrogen and argon. By mass, the inert gases are about 3.5% of the Martian atmosphere, so every 19.2 t of CO₂ captured for one MAV load [7] passes about 0.7 t of nitrogen and argon. Finn and Sridhar described a temperature-swing adsorption process that holds back CO₂ and passes the nitrogen-argon mixture for collection [18], and NASA's later review agreed that nitrogen and argon "should be captured as well" [19]. Load 1 alone yields about 0.7 t by October 2040, ahead of the habitat's switch to crew atmosphere, and the two loads together about 1.4 t by May 2042. That leaves about 1.6 t for a small dedicated capture unit on the oxygen line's intake. It freezes CO₂ out of the intake air as the plant does, keeps the nitrogen and argon, and passes the CO₂ on to the oxygen stack or back to the air; banking 1 t of buffer gas takes about 29 t of processed air.

> Assumption: The capture unit lands with Wave 2 and runs from April 2039, alongside the propellant plant, at about 0.06 kg/h of nitrogen and argon, freezing out about 1.7 kg/h of CO₂, the plant's own intake rate. At 85% availability it captures about 0.45 t a year, so the plant and the unit together have captured about 2.2 t by ladder rung 3 in October 2041 and about 3.3 t by August 2043, which leaves about 2.3 t in storage at the decision; the stock first reaches 2 t in about March 2042. By this book's estimate the unit draws 1–3 kWe, mostly cryocooler power to remove about 0.3 kW of heat near 140 K, and weighs about 0.3–0.5 t, a fraction of the 1.7 t plant that captures CO₂ at the same rate [7]. Justification: the plant's by-product covers only about 1.4 t of the ~3 t needed, and the unit's power fits inside the oxygen line's allocation in chapter 2, since that line runs at low duty once its tanks are full.

> Assumption: Nitrogen and argon are collected together, compressed, and stored as high-pressure gas, about 9 m³ of tankage at 20 MPa, which holds about 2.4 t at room temperature. Justification: the Mars capture literature collects the inert mixture without separating it [18], [19]; storing gas avoids a second cryogenic system. If physiology rules argon out, the stored mixture is separated instead (below).

People have lived in argon-rich air, 30–45% argon for 10–60 days in Russian fire-safety studies, but none has then decompressed to suit pressure ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#life-support-ready-for-people)). The EAWG listed decompression sickness risk "given that a tri-gas breathing mixture including argon is present" (a mix of oxygen, nitrogen, and argon) as an open research question [20], and its workshop called argon "an undesirable pre-breathe gas" [20]. Other NASA, U.S. Navy, and U.S. Air Force work points the same way: argon is about twice as soluble as nitrogen in fatty tissue, in Navy rat dives it was up to 55% more potent per unit pressure, and in a U.S. Air Force chamber study it raised decompression sickness ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#suitports-keep-suits-and-dust-outside)). An argon-rich habitat atmosphere needs physiological testing on Earth. [Chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#life-support-ready-for-people) makes a chamber study with people part of crew gate criterion 9, and its result is due at gate G3 in June 2039, while Wave 3's manifest can still change. Rejecting argon costs more than a separator: nitrogen is only about 48% of the captured mixture by mass, so without more capture the stock at the decision would fall to about 0.7 t of nitrogen.

> Assumption: If the chamber study rules argon out at G3, Wave 3 carries a second capture unit with about 2.3 times the first one's rate and a small cryogenic column at its cold end that separates the argon near 85 K and vents it; the column also works through the mixture already in storage. The set weighs about 1–1.5 t and draws about 3–8 kWe, from Wave 3's margin and the third reactor's spare power, and it yields the ~0.3 t of nitrogen the habitat's first fill needs in April 2041 and 2 t in storage at the decision (this book's estimate). Justification: the column's cooling load is only about 50 W, and the stored mixture covers the first fill once separated. A failure found after G3 cannot be answered this way: the next delivery, the support lander of September 2042, arrives after criterion 2's pressure hold must start in the crew's gas, so the crew moves one window.

## Crew life support leans on ISRU

The International Space Station (ISS) is the only long-duration closed life support system people have. Its U.S. segment recovers water from humidity condensate and urine. In 2023 NASA reported that the Brine Processor Assembly, installed in 2021, recovers water from the urine processor's leftover brine and raises total recovery to about 98% [22]. Oxygen comes from water electrolysis, and regenerable sorbents remove CO₂ from cabin air. The station also shows the limits: failure-rate estimates for its life support units remain uncertain after more than a decade, and preflight estimates were off by factors of several in both directions [23].

On Mars the colony can make more water and oxygen. High closure still lowers the load on the plants and the spares, but the crew's survival does not depend on reaching 98%. For four crew over about 1,000 days at 0.84 kg per person per day, metabolic oxygen is about 3.4 t; the rest of the ~5 t reserve covers leakage, airlock losses, and margin.

> Assumption: The crew habitat carries an ISS-derived water recovery system with brine processing, an oxygen generator fed by electrolysis, and regenerable CO₂ removal, backed by ISRU water and oxygen. Justification: ISS heritage [22]; the ISRU reserves above make an open-loop fallback possible for months at a time.

Before any crew launches, robots run the habitat's life support for at least 12 months with metabolic simulators that inject CO₂, water vapor, heat, and trace contaminants on a four-person schedule and draw oxygen at 0.84 kg per person per day. The simulators also feed the water recovery system urine and wash-water simulants at four people's rate, so the urine and brine processors, which give the station its 98% recovery, run under load. The trial runs from 2042, after the equipment lands with Wave 3, on the [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) life support allocation of about 10 kW. It yields what the station never had before its crews arrived: a year of failure data from the actual hardware in the actual place. [Chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md) turns those data into spares.

## Planetary protection: every well is a Special Region
<a id="planetary-protection"></a>

A Rodwell is a spacecraft-induced Special Region by design: it holds a pool of liquid water near 0 °C inside the ice for years. The Committee on Space Research (COSPAR) policy, 2026 version, requires such regions "to be evaluated . . . on a case-by-case basis" and any uncharacterized Martian site to "be evaluated by robotic precursors prior to crew access" [27]. That version is looser than the July 2024 text [28], but it keeps the controls for Special Regions. [Chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md) gives the Special Region limits and [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md) the crewed-mission rules. A life detection, or a ruling against drawing water from induced Special Regions, could halt the wells ([R40](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r40)).

> Assumption: Drill strings, casings, and melt probes are sterilized before insertion; the well loop is closed and runs hot enough to pasteurize; nothing is returned to the cavity except its own recirculated water. Justification: COSPAR's case-by-case requirement [27]; the recirculating water is already heated for melting, so pasteurizing it costs little.

The chemical plants vent only gases already in the Martian air; brine, filter residues, and spent catalyst are stored, and biological processes stay inside sealed volumes with filtered exhausts. The robotic phase also lets the colony establish its own microbial baseline before any crew arrives, as COSPAR's crewed-mission guidelines ask [27].

## What would change this conclusion

The conclusion rests on workable ice at the site. Without it, the colony could still fuel its ascent vehicle with Martian oxygen and imported methane, but the habitat design and the water budget in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) would change.

## Autonomy

Under the levels of [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md), the plants are judged by one product: an inventory Earth can audit.

### What A3 must do

At A3 the plants run as one system for weeks without contact. The Orchestrator holds each Rodwell's heat and withdrawal in balance, seals and drains wells around a heat outage, and trends the oxygen stack's iASR and cathode pressure against the limit beyond which the cells start depositing carbon. It throttles the Sabatier reactors and electrolyzers as power changes, swaps catalyst beds and stacks from spares, and keeps zero boil-off cooling alive. The mass balance must be auditable: at least two independent sensors count every kilogram of water, oxygen, methane, and buffer gas that enters the crew gate's inventory. From their first year the plants must meet crew gate criterion 6 ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)), including a conjunction's two-week command gap [29].

### What A4 adds

A4 diagnoses what the procedures did not foresee. A well cavity may grow sideways into a dust lens, a catalyst may age in an unexplained way, or a new trace contaminant may appear in the meltwater; A4 replans the well field or adjusts the chemistry and tests each repair on a spare module first. It can also shorten the year-long life support trial by recognizing when the failure data have converged.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could redesign the oxygen stack for low cathode pressure on site or build a plasma line from laboratory results. The buffer gas and the habitat atmosphere serve only the crew; the mind itself runs on power.

## Risks

- The ice itself ([R05](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r05), [R36](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r36)). If the wells fail, hydrated minerals can still supply the propellant, reserve, and start-up water, but not the ~650 t habitat shield. Cavity pressure, dust, and salts could change the heat balance, a schedule risk while waste heat far exceeds the need.
- Oxygen scale-up and plant life ([R04](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r04), [R13](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r13)). Catalyst and stack life over years of continuous operation are unknown [10], [14]; on the station both wore out, the stack by membrane thinning [54] and the catalyst from poisons in its feed [53], and a Mars feed that poisoned the beds about three times as fast as the station's would use up even two spare beds per module before the Wave 3 modules arrive, and the next crew's two loads double each module's running hours, so the support lander and the crew ship bring more beds and stacks if the plant's record calls for them; spares, including the spare process modules on Wave 3 that chapter 13 assumes, and three years of oxygen-line operation before the methane plant let the Orchestrator learn how the hardware ages, and the 2041 methane option covers a plant that fails.
- Cryogenic hold ([R26](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r26)). Load 1 sits in the MAV for about five years, to the ascent, and about seven if the crew slips a window or misses its return window, on cryocoolers larger than any flown [16], [34] and on vacuum jackets that must hold for years, without which the insulation would leak five to ten times the heat assumed or more [36], [62]. The pad's four coolers and three spares cover the seven years at the flown turbo-Brayton failure rate; at the upper rate the record allows, the shelf would need seven, or five if the support lander restores it. A fault shared by every unit of the design, which no spare covers, stays at about 2 in 100 at the flown rate (this book's calculation). The plant restarts to replace vented methane, except in the cases where it has failed.
- Heat to the wells ([R32](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r32)); see [chapter 5](05.%20Power.md).
- The argon-rich buffer atmosphere ([R03](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r03)) needs physiological validation on Earth, reported by G3 in June 2039 so that Wave 3 can carry the argon separation set if it fails (criterion 9).
- Planetary protection ([R09](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r09), [R40](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r40)). Spacecraft-induced Special Regions are evaluated case by case [27], so the treatment of a decade of Rodwell operations is uncertain.

No spare or fallback fully covers the ice; [Appendix A](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md) scores all six risks.

## Open questions

- Will drilling at the reference site confirm ice at Rodwell depth, and how salty is it?
- Does the Rodwell heat balance hold on Mars, including cavity pressure control?
- Can a SOXE stack run at 10 mbar (1 kPa) cathode pressure, as MOXIE's team recommends exploring?
- How long do Sabatier catalysts and electrolysis stacks last in continuous operation, and does the Mars feed poison the beds more slowly than the station's cabin air, as this chapter's hypothesis expects, or fast enough to need a third bed per module?
- Does a vacuum-jacketed tank hold 1–2 W/m² through years of Mars nights, dust, and thermal cycling, as an Earth test of a flight-like tank in CO₂ at the site's pressure must show by about 2032, and how often must its jacket be pumped?
- How long does a 100 W-class 90 K cryocooler run before it fails? One unit has run 31 days. The flown turbo-Brayton record and ground turbomachinery point to about 2.5–7% a year per unit; a multiyear life test of the flight design, begun early in the 2030s with four or more units, would narrow that before G4 and set the MAV's spares and the ERV's redundancy.
- People have lived up to 60 days in argon-rich air; after months in a nitrogen-argon buffer, is decompression to suit pressure as safe as from nitrogen alone, and how fast does argon leave the body?
- How will planetary protection authorities treat an induced Special Region maintained for a decade?
