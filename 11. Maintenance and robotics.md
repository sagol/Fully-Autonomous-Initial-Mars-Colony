# 11. Maintenance and robotics

*Last reviewed: September 28, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, January 2037.* Hauler 5 rolls into the garage with 8,900 km on its left front drive module, 1,100 km short of its rated life. The Orchestrator has pulled it early because the motor current on that wheel has crept up for three weeks. A manipulator undoes four bolts and one connector, fits a module from the shelf, and sends the hauler back to the rock-armor run within the hour. In the clean cell a second manipulator opens the old module and photographs the seal: fine red grit has worked past the outer lip and into the bearing. This is the third drive module with the same wear since Wave 1 landed. The Orchestrator raises the seal's failure rate in its ledger and moves seals and bearings up the parts list it will send to Earth for the Wave 2 manifest.

Every machine on Mars will break. This chapter asks whether a colony with no people can keep enough machines working, for nearly nine years and through four solar conjunctions, to build and hold everything that chapters 5–9 describe. Earth offers no repair visits, a supply ship at most once every 26 months, and no advice for about two weeks at each conjunction.

Mars rovers have outlived their design lives many times over, but they drive a few kilometers a year; construction robots will drive that far in a day. Spares alone do not solve the problem, because some units on the International Space Station (ISS) failed 7.5–22 times more often than predicted. So the colony relies on a fleet designed for repair: swappable modules, a workshop where robots fix robots, spares sized from measured failure rates, and a site intelligence that schedules maintenance alongside power and construction.

In brief: Wave 1 lands about 47 robots with a combined mass of ~36 t inside [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s ~65 t construction kit, together with ~8 t of robot spares, a ~6 t workshop, and a ~2 t trailer that carries the reactors. Waves 2 and 3 add ~20 t of robots and ~19 t of robot spares. At autonomy level A3 the fleet repairs known faults by module swap; novel faults are contained until A4 judgment or Earth can design a fix, and every class keeps a machine ready while another is in the shop.

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) sets this chapter's limits: ~65 t of robots and construction kit in Wave 1, and 20–30 kW for construction and robot charging until October 2038 (70–100 kW when spare power exists) and 30–40 kW after. Every wave and gate criterion must be met at A3 ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md)), and crew gate criterion 6 asks for 120 days with no commands from Earth, including a conjunction. Those limits become five requirements:

1. Keep enough machines working to meet the construction schedule in [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md).
2. Keep the reactors and chapter 2's critical loads serviceable at all times, through a conjunction and a 120-day dust storm. The critical loads are well pumps, habitat thermal control and pressure hold, life support, communications, and keep-alive and cryogenic tank cooling; the oxygen line is deferrable and waits its turn.
3. Repair any anticipated fault on site, without Earth, using stocked spares and predesigned procedures.
4. Contain any unanticipated fault so it cannot spread, and keep the rest of the fleet working until a fix is found.
5. Stay inside the mass and power budgets.

## What wears out on Mars

### Dust sets the service life

Apollo's lunar dust is the best-documented case of abrasion on another world. NASA's review of the Apollo missions found that "the severity of the dust problems were consistently underestimated by ground tests." Pete Conrad's Apollo 12 suit showed more wear after 8 hours on the Moon than training suits after 100 hours, and its leak rate rose after each moonwalk, so that "one or two additional EVAs [moonwalks] could have resulted in a pressure failure." On Apollo 12 all of the environmental sample and gas sample seals failed because of dust [14].

Mars rovers show what dust and terrain do even at low duty. Opportunity was designed for 90 sols and worked for more than 14 years, covering 45 km [15], but its arm shoulder motor began stalling in 2005 [17]. Spirit's right front wheel stopped turning in 2006 [16], and Curiosity's wheels were punctured and torn within about a year and a half of landing [19]; its drill feed stopped extending in December 2016 [18]. Dust on InSight's solar panels ended its mission in December 2022 [20].

A hauler that makes 5–10 round trips a sol between the landing field and the working area 2 km away drives 20–40 km a sol, close to Opportunity's lifetime distance. A single construction robot will drive farther in its first year than all Mars rovers combined, and every bearing, seal, and joint on it will wear in proportion. No test has run a machine that long in Martian dust.

The design answers follow from Apollo: sealed and labyrinth-protected bearings, bellows over sliding parts, dust-shedding surfaces, brushless motors with no exposed commutation, and seal replacement on a schedule, before failure. Drive modules are rated for a service life in kilometers and swapped at that mileage whether or not they have failed.

> Assumption: Each drive module has a planned service life of 10,000 km before overhaul in the workshop, where bearings and seals are replaced from stocked parts. At a fleet-average 10–20 km per sol, that is one overhaul every 1.5–3 years. Justification: it is a design target to be proven in an Earth dust-chamber life test before Wave 1 flies; no Mars data exist at this duty cycle.

### Cold is mostly an energy cost

At the Viking 1 landing site the air swung between −89 °C and −31 °C each sol [21]. A machine that stands outside at a midlatitude site sees about 670 such cycles per Mars year, or roughly 3,000 between 2036 and the crew's arrival. Solder joints, connectors, and bonded parts fatigue under that cycling. Lubricants stiffen: Curiosity's 31 actuators use a wet lubricant and need warm-up heaters to bring them above −55 °C before use in winter, when the air falls to −113 °C [22]. Batteries lose capacity in the cold and age if charged cold.

The colony's machines park in the garage or under covers when idle, keep their batteries and electronics warm, and preheat joints on reactor power before work. The energy for that heating is part of the robot charging load in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md), except for the four robots held back to restart a tripped reactor before January 2039: their heat is part of the survival floor, and their charge comes from a battery reserve held for the restart ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)).

### Radiation upsets chips more than it ages them

Radiation at the surface is a much smaller problem for silicon than for people. Curiosity measured an absorbed dose of 0.21 ± 0.04 mGy per day at Gale crater [23]. Over nine years that sums to under 1 Gy (0.1 krad), and the cruise adds about 0.1 Gy more. NASA's electronics radiation guidance notes commercial device failures "as low as 1 krad(Si)" [24]. Total ionizing dose therefore does not limit ordinary commercial parts on the surface, as long as their tolerance has been tested.

Single-event effects matter more. A single heavy cosmic-ray ion can flip a memory bit, crash a processor, or, in susceptible power devices, cause a latch-up that destroys the part; NASA describes commercial devices as "highly susceptible to radiation" in this sense [25]. Mars has already shown how to live with it: the Ingenuity helicopter, planned for up to 5 flights, made 72 over about three years on a commercial Qualcomm Snapdragon 801 processor [26], [27], [28].

The colony uses two tiers of electronics. Safety-critical controllers, such as those for the reactor, pressure hold, and motor power stages, use radiation-hardened parts and stay independent of the Orchestrator, as [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md) requires. Planning, vision, and learning run on commercial processors with error-correcting memory, watchdogs, redundant units that vote, and fast reboot. NASA's High Performance Spaceflight Computing (HPSC) processor, built by Microchip, produced its first chips in late 2025 and began testing at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in February 2026. NASA's design goal is up to 100 times the computing capacity of current spaceflight computers; in the early tests, JPL reported performance about 500 times that of the radiation-hardened chips now in use [29], [38]. By 2035 it or its successors should give the colony radiation-tolerant computing between the two tiers.

### One trailer moves the reactors and the MAV

[Chapter 5](05.%20Power.md) describes how each ~15 t reactor gets from its lander to the reactor field; this chapter supplies the trailer and the traction. One trailer serves all three reactors and, in late September 2040, the empty MAV on its way to the launch pad (chapter 8); at ~15 t landed, the MAV is no heavier than a reactor. From late October 2040 it also carries the MAV's propellant from the storage tanks to the pad, three trips a load in a ~2 t set of mobile tanks that brings the towed mass to about 14 t ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md#the-mars-ascent-vehicle)). It also carries the loads too heavy for a hauler's 1.5 t: Wave 1's water and oxygen plants, about 20 t in several units, in early 2036, and in late 2038 Wave 2's ~20 t greenhouse module, its habitat package of up to 18 t, and the propellant plant and its tanks, all of which cross the same 2 km or more from the landing field. With craning and hitching each tow takes about a sol (this book's estimate), so these few loads fit beside reactor 2's tow in the weeks after Wave 2 lands, while three heavy rovers are free of armor work.

> Assumption: The trailer is a passive wheeled frame of ~2 t, its own line in Wave 1's kit, rated for about 20 t of cargo. Justification: at 0.38 g, 20 t of cargo and the trailer weigh about 82 kN, what 8.3 t weighs on Earth, so the frame is sized like an Earth trailer for a light load. Heavy rovers tow in tandem. Each 3 t rover weighs about 11 kN on Mars and, with traction of about half its weight, pulls about 5.6 kN before its own rolling resistance, so about 4.5 kN of net pull on a graded road (rolling resistance 0.1 of weight) and 3.3 kN on soft ground (0.2). A reactor on the trailer, about 17 t, or up to about 21.5 t with [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s 30% growth allowance (a 19.5 t reactor, just inside the trailer's rating), needs 6–16 kN, and the greenhouse module 8–16 kN. Three rovers pull any of these loads on a graded road with grades up to about 2.5–4°; two manage a 15 t reactor or the MAV only on grades under about 1.7°; on soft ground a load needs four or five (this book's estimate; no Mars test exists). The fifth heavy rover grades the tow routes before the loads move.

### Can the fleet keep the schedule?

Yes for digging, hauling, and sintering, by this book's estimates, if each berm is dug beside where it stands from the loose soil above the ice table, the landing field sits within about 1 km of a rock field for the armor, a crater's boulder field (the smooth landing plains hold far too little stone, and the nearest known field lies 37 km from the working point, chapter 8) with enough loose stone to pick, because the fleet carries no crusher or rock breaker, and the sintering heads run at 25 kW; heavy-rover time is tight. Chapter 8's berms before Wave 2 need roughly 22,000 t of regolith, about 90 MWh at about 4 kWh per tonne. Eight excavators at three times RASSOR 2.0's 2.7 t per day [1] move about 65 t per day, about 55 t at chapter 8's 85% availability, so the berms take about 400 days of the 28 months available, and the first stretch of the trench for chapter 8's power line to the landing field, the trench for the water-bank bladders, and the garage cover, all dug first, about eight weeks more. Those rates hold in loose soil only: in NASA's Break the Ice finals, robots of 250–770 kg dug a 1.5–2 MPa stand-in for lunar icy regolith at 12–312 kg an hour, the drum robot at 170 kg [41], about 40% of an excavator's loose-soil pace once scaled to its 0.2 t, and ground with ice-filled pores may be ten or more times stronger, so every ditch and trench stops at the ice (chapter 8). Seven haulers, each carrying about its own mass on 5–10 trips a sol over the 2 km road, move about 50–105 t per sol. Power sets the sintering pace: at 25 kW, chapter 8 gives about 8.5 months of running per landing-site core, or 10 months at 85% availability, so the two sintering rovers finish the four Wave 2 cores in about 20 months, around February 2038, if they start when reactor 1 reaches full power in June 2036.

Chapter 8's rock armor needs about 76 team-months before Wave 2, each team a heavy rover and a hauler. Three teams working from March 2036 to August 2038 would supply 87 calendar team-months, but only about 74 at chapter 8's 85% availability, and about 38.5 by G2, just enough for the 38 that two finished sites need. So Wave 1 carries a fifth heavy rover and a seventh hauler, about 4.5 t from its unitemized mass ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)), and four teams place armor from March 2036. At 85% they deliver about 99 team-months, a margin of about 30%, have two sites done before G2, and finish around January 2038. They tie up four of the five heavy rovers and four of the seven haulers. The armor haulers have the fleet's heaviest duty: about 61,000 t of rock for Wave 2's sites (chapter 8), some 18 loads a sol each, which from rock 1 km away is up to about 36 km a sol, the top of the range above and two to three times the fleet average behind the drive-module life, so they wear out drive modules that much faster. From the nearest crater that might hold rock, 9 km from the working point, it would be about 330 km a sol, which no hauler in the fleet can drive, so the landing field goes beside the rock instead (chapter 8). The fifth heavy rover grades, compacts, and clears the ground ahead of the teams and prepares the cores. The lunar pad study that chapter 8 scales from grades 0.1 m² a second and compacts 0.05 m² a second [40]; applied unchanged to this heavier rover, a 100 m site takes about 11 sols of running (this book's estimate). The fifth rover's jobs before Wave 2 lands (the eight Wave 2 and Wave 3 sites, the MAV pad, about 6 km of roads and tracks, the yards, the habitat and greenhouse sites, trench backfill, crane lifts for the Wave 1 installation, its share of the reactor tow, and the garage and its covers) come to about 6.5 rover-months against about 28 available at 85%, and to about half of them at a third of the study's rates. It grades the first two sites in about four weeks in February 2036, before the armor starts, and then stays far ahead of teams that cover only about 180 m² a day, so its shop visits cost the armor nothing. That leaves three haulers for the berms. On the 2 km run they would move only 22–45 t per sol, and at 85% availability the berms would stretch to 19–39 months, yet none can wait for Wave 2: the line-of-sight berms protect hardware already on the ground (the Wave 1 landers, reactor, wells, and habitat site), and the berms between sites protect each Wave 2 ship from its neighbors' landings. So each berm is dug from a borrow ditch beside it (chapter 8), and the three haulers shuttle a few hundred meters, 20 or more trips a sol each and about 90 t a sol together, faster than the excavators dig. Digging sets the pace, the berms are finished in early July 2037, and gate G2 checks that they will be done before Wave 2 departs ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). Towing does not compete with the armor work: the first reactor tow and Wave 1's plants move before it starts, while all five heavy rovers are free, and in late 2038 the fifth rover and Wave 2's two extra heavy rovers make the three-rover team for reactor 2 and Wave 2's heavy modules. The 85% already allows for the shop and for reactor trips; the margin absorbs about 23 more team-months of lost rovers, and beyond that chapter 8 leaves the last site graded but unarmored, for the least sensitive cargo.

## The fleet grows instead of turning over

> Assumption: A mobile robot's frame and structure are rated for 10 years at construction duty, long enough to reach the crew's departure in November 2045. The parts that wear (drive modules, arm joints, batteries, seals) are replaced from spares on schedule, so each machine is rebuilt piece by piece. Justification: frames carry loads that do not wear the way bearings and seals do, and Opportunity's frame ran 14 years at low duty [15]; the dust-chamber life test before Wave 1 flies must confirm the rating.

On that basis the Wave 1 machines stay in service and the fleet adds mass with each wave: about 36 t of robots from January 2036, 48 t from October 2038, and 56 t from September 2040. Wave 2 adds capacity for the habitat and propellant-plant work and replaces the machines that the first three years of teardown data show to be weakest. The 2041 support flight can carry replacements for whatever class has failed fastest. If the life test shows frames failing sooner, Wave 3 must replace the Wave 1 heavy rovers and haulers, about 26 t, from its unitemized mass ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)).

## Designing for repair

### Every robot is built from swappable modules

The ISS is maintained by swapping orbital replacement units (ORUs): boxes designed to be removed and replaced as a whole, with standard handles, fasteners, and connectors, so that a crew member or a robot can change one without opening it. The colony's robots are built the same way. A drive module (motor, gearbox, bearing, brake, controller, wheel) comes off with four bolts and one connector. So do an arm joint, a battery pack, a camera head, and a compute box.

Robots have done this in orbit. Canada's Dextre, a 1,850 kg two-armed manipulator launched to the ISS in 2008 [4], cut 0.5 mm lock wires with Robotic Refueling Mission tools in 2012 [6], [7] and replaced a failed Main Bus Switching Unit in May 2019 [5]. Every step was teleoperated from the ground, so these operations prove the hardware interfaces and say nothing yet about autonomy.

### Robots repair robots in the garage

The mobile manipulators are the colony's mechanics. They work in the regolith-covered garage [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md) builds, which holds the tool-changer station, a clean cell for opening sealed modules, a test bench, and the printers. Heavy rovers drive in; manipulators swap modules; failed modules go to the clean cell, where a manipulator opens them, photographs the damage, and records the cause. That teardown record is how the colony learns its true failure rates.

Because every mobile robot shares the same modules, any manipulator can service any robot, including another manipulator. The four manipulators of Wave 1, six after Wave 2, repair each other, so the colony never depends on a single mechanic.

Robonaut 2, a humanoid launched to the station in 2011 and given legs in 2014, developed power-system faults and returned to Earth in 2018 for repair. Its project manager said that "as we continued to debug the problem more damage was inflicted" [8]. The colony's procedures therefore stop and isolate a machine instead of probing a fault that is not in the library.

On Earth, automation stops short of repair. Komatsu has commissioned its 1,000th ultra-class autonomous haul truck and says customers using its FrontRunner system have moved more than 11.5 billion tonnes [34], yet those mines keep human mechanics. Unattended instrument platforms on the Antarctic plateau run for a 5-year design life without visits, but only because they are simple [35]. Cabled seafloor observatories come closest to the colony: built from swappable packages with standard connectors, they are serviced by remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) from ships at least once a year [36], [37], while the colony's servicing robots stay on site.

### A few module types serve the whole fleet

Heavy rovers carry a quick-change coupler for blades, rollers, forks, buckets, and a crane arm, so five chassis cover the work of a dozen single-purpose machines. Manipulators change their own end effectors.

Every mobile robot in the fleet uses one of three drive-module sizes, one battery-pack design, one compute box, and one camera head. A spare drive module then fits every heavy rover and hauler, 12 machines, instead of one class of five. Owens's 2019 MIT doctoral analysis of crewed spacecraft supportability treats commonality as one of five main strategies, with level of maintenance, on-demand manufacturing, redundancy, and distributed functionality [9].

> Assumption: The fleet holds to that limit across all mobile classes. Justification: commonality cuts spares mass and lets any manipulator repair any robot; the cost is some mass penalty on the smallest machines.

## Spares: what the evidence says

Owens and de Weck cite an estimate that for 2012–2020 the ISS held more than 13,000 kg of spares on orbit and nearly 18,000 kg more on the ground, over 31,000 kg in all [10]. A NASA study they cite found that about 95% of the spares carried for corrective maintenance would never be used [11]. They are carried because nobody knows in advance which 5% will be needed.

Predicted failure rates are also often wrong. Harry Jones of NASA Ames, reviewing ISS life support data, reports that earlier analysis uncovered failure-rate underestimates that "would have resulted in an order of magnitude increase in risk had they not been discovered and corrected." Four ORUs of the station's urine processor failed 7.5–22 times more often than predicted; one had an estimated mean time between failures of 22,759 hours and a flight value of about 1,000 hours. If spares are sized to estimated rates but failures happen at flight rates, Jones found, "the probability that the system will fail due to insufficient spares can exceed 99 percent" [12].

Owens and de Weck sized corrective spares for a Mars transit habitat on an 1,100-day mission at about 10,100 kg for a 0.999 probability of having enough. Doubling every component's mean time between failures cut that mass to about 76%, and a tenfold improvement to just under 45%; halving it raised the mass to 135% [11]. In a second study, in-space manufacturing cut a Mars habitat's spares from 17,232 kg to 12,323 kg, about 28% less, and adding recycling brought it to 11,254 kg [10]. A planning heuristic quoted by Owens and colleagues in 2015 puts spares at about 5% of system dry mass per year [13].

Applied to the fleet as it grows, the 5%-per-year rule sets a floor. Its base counts the robots and also the 6 t of Wave 1 hardware that wears alongside them, the tool attachments (blade edges, bucket teeth, rollers) and the trailer; the chargers and the workshop kit draw on each wave's general spares in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s unitemized mass, outside this ledger:

The plan carries ~27 t of robot spares through Wave 3, about 14% above the floor at the crew's arrival, and runs down to the floor during the crew's stay, when the support flight's spares ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)) take over.

Against Jones's findings a 14% margin is thin, and without failure data for these machines no affordable mass would buy Owens and de Weck's 0.999 confidence. The plan therefore claims no confidence level. It bounds the exposure: each stock only has to last until the next window, and each window's manifest is rebuilt from rates measured on Mars, because Earth predictions for new machines in Mars dust are the least trustworthy numbers in this book. Wave 1's 8 t covers the first 33 months at up to 1.4 times the planning rate. At twice the planning rate it runs out around the end of November 2037, about ten months before Wave 2 lands; the colony then idles its least-needed machines as parts sources and slows construction, while critical systems keep their redundant units.

The list favors whole modules in Wave 1, when robots cannot yet diagnose below the module, and shifts toward parts (bearings, seals, motor windings) once the workshop can rebuild modules.

> Assumption: Robot spares are ~22% of robot mass in Wave 1 (8 t for 36 t) and ~27 t in total through Wave 3. Justification: the 5%-per-year floor plus about 14% margin, front-loaded in Wave 1 because its resupply is 33 months away and no Mars data stand behind it. The failure-rate risk rests on measuring rates on Mars and resupplying each window.

### Printers make the structural spares

Printing saves less spares mass than a tenfold gain in reliability would [10], [11], but reliability is fixed at launch and printing can grow on site. The ISS's first 3D printer made its first parts, including a ratchet designed on the ground and uplinked, in November and December 2014 [31]. The wire-fed metal printer of the European Space Agency (ESA), built by Airbus, was launched to the station in January 2024; each part takes two to four weeks, and the first metal part printed in space came back to Earth in February 2025 [32], [33].

What the colony can print depends on the part.

| Part type | Printable on Mars by 2040? | How |

| Tools, jigs, fixtures, grippers | Yes | Polymer and metal printers |

| Seals and gaskets | Partly | Casting from imported elastomer stock |

## The Orchestrator schedules maintenance like any other load

Most of the fleet's repairs are planned before anything breaks, on the same schedule as sintering and electrolysis. For each machine the Orchestrator tracks hours, kilometers, load cycles, thermal cycles, and live health signals such as motor current, vibration, temperature, and battery impedance. It plans module swaps at their rated life and pulls them forward when a signal drifts.

Three rules govern the schedule. Critical-system maintenance always comes first. Fleet maintenance is timed for spare capacity: at night, during dust storms when construction power is curtailed, and before conjunctions. And no class has more than one member in scheduled maintenance at a time, so every class of two or more keeps at least one machine ready. The single unloading crane is the exception, backed up by the heavy rovers' crane arms. The power for maintenance (garage heating, charging, and printers) sits inside [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s construction and robots allocation.

## Fault recovery without Earth

Solar conjunction cuts off Earth commands for about two weeks every 26 months [30], and outside it, light time of 3–22 minutes and limited relay passes mean Earth advice takes hours to days. The colony treats Earth as a slow consultant.

Fault handling follows three tiers:

1. Known faults with a procedure in the library (a worn drive module, a stalled joint, a failed camera, a tripped charger) are handled at A3. The Orchestrator matches symptoms to the library, pulls the machine from service, and dispatches a mechanic.
2. Unknown faults on a noncritical machine are contained. The machine is safed, powered down, and parked; its telemetry and teardown photos go to Earth; its work is reassigned. Nothing restarts until the fault is understood. A4 capability, when it exists, can diagnose and design a repair here.
3. Faults that threaten the reactors or a critical load (wells, habitat pressure and thermal control, life support) go first to independent safety controllers, which put the system into a safe state without waiting for the Orchestrator. Critical systems have redundant units, so the colony can wait for diagnosis.

Before each conjunction (dates in [chapter 10](10.%20Communications.md#distance-and-the-sun-set-the-limits)) the Orchestrator starts no new risky work, completes planned module swaps early, tops up the spares shelf, and checks that every critical system has its redundant unit healthy. The February 2043 conjunction falls inside the crew gate proving period.

## What would change this conclusion

Flight evidence from CADRE and later lunar construction robots on how fast machines wear in real regolith would confirm or overturn the service lives this chapter assumes. A demonstration of A4 repair on Earth would let the plan cut spares only after a qualification step on Mars. The 24–55% cuts in the Owens and de Weck studies come from more reliable components and from in-space manufacturing, for a crewed habitat's spares at 0.999 probability; they measure nothing about repair autonomy in a robot fleet. A4 repair would first have to show, in a window's teardown record on Mars, that repaired modules last, and the next manifest would then shrink by what that record supports.

## Autonomy

### What A3 must do

A3 must keep the fleet working for months without Earth, using known procedures: health monitoring on every machine, a fault library covering every module's anticipated failure modes, diagnosis to the module level, module swaps on schedule or on fault, and containment of anything outside the library. A3 also keeps the spares ledger and the failure-rate record that rebuilds each window's manifest. Earth engineers write and approve the procedures; the fleet runs them.

### What A4 adds

A4 diagnoses faults nobody anticipated and invents repairs for them. The A3 system in this chapter's opening scene logs a worn seal and reorders parts; A4 can read the same teardown, recognize a new wear mechanism, change the maintenance interval for a whole class, and design and test a printed part the library lacks. Once its repairs have held up on Mars it can cut spares mass, because it can repair parts that A3 can only replace, and it shortens the time machines spend waiting for Earth.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could redesign the fleet on site: study years of failure data, design a better drive module, build it from local metal and imported electronics, and decide which machines to build next. From then on the fleet would grow on Mars.

## Risks

Wear at construction duty is the least-tested estimate in this chapter: no machine has worked for years in Martian dust at [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)'s duty cycle, and failure rates may be far above prediction, as Jones found on the ISS. The spares section gives the date Wave 1's stock would run out at twice the planning rate. The mitigations are dust-chamber life tests before 2035, conservative ratings, and revised manifests before each window ([R35](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r35)).

Commonality makes a common-mode fault worse. A software update could brick every compute box, and a battery design flaw or dust in a connector type used everywhere could hit the whole fleet at once. Staged rollouts, a frozen known-good image on every machine, and two suppliers for critical module types limit the damage ([R12](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r12)).

A fault outside the library during conjunction could idle a machine for weeks, and Earth qualification may not predict how machines fail together on Mars. Redundant critical units, and the rule that every class keeps one machine ready, keep the colony safe while it waits ([R22](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r22)).

Losing every manipulator would leave no class repairable; Wave 2's two extra manipulators lower that risk ([R35](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r35)).

The Orchestrator's own computers wear ([R30](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r30)), its interaction with the independent safety controllers is a risk of its own ([R29](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r29)), and if autonomy stalls below A3, every repair outside the fault library waits for Earth ([R17](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r17)).

## Open questions

- What is the real service life of a drive module, bearing, seal, and frame at construction duty in Martian dust and cold? A multiyear life test in a Mars dust chamber, started before 2030, would answer it.
- How far can autonomous diagnosis go below the module level with A3 methods alone?
- Should the colony carry humanoids at all, or put their mass into more manipulators?
