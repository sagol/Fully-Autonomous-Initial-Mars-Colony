# 8. Manufacturing and construction

*Last reviewed: September 28, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, February 2038.* The second sintering rover backs off the core of Site 4 at local noon and parks its magnetron heads under a dust cover. Behind it lies a gray disk 40 m across, glazed a few centimeters deep. The Orchestrator logs the core as complete: 156 MWh over 297 sols, 253 of them spent sintering and the rest lost to maintenance and reactor trips. It checks the day's reactor output and sends the rover about 1 km east to help the first rover finish Site 3. Out to 100 m around the core, the rover teams finished laying sorted cobbles last month, over the whole circle where a Wave 2 ship might set down, because no one can promise that a 100 t ship will hit a 40 m target. Wave 2 is already in cruise, and four ships will need somewhere to stand in eight months. Once Site 3's core is done, the ledger says, all four sites will be ready with seven months to spare, as long as the rovers keep running.

The habitat in [chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md) lands in October 2038 on a foundation pad that robots began two years earlier; this chapter covers that earlier work. The colony's first construction job is to make the ground safe for the next landing: landing sites, blast berms, roads, and laydown yards come before any building. Regolith shelters come next, as unpressurized covers for pressure vessels brought from Earth. Almost every regolith process here was tested on lunar simulant, so each carries its test environment and technology readiness level (TRL).

In brief: [chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)'s landing bound, 99.7% of landings within 100 m, makes each landing site a circle 200 m across, too large to sinter, so each site pairs a sintered core 40 m across with rock armor over the rest. Four sites before Wave 2 lands need more than half of the first epoch's spare energy and most of the robot time: four rover teams and both sintering rovers, fed by a buried power line from the reactor field, must start in the first half of 2036, and the landing field must sit beside a crater's boulder field, the only rock source for the armor in reach. The plan builds four sites for Wave 2, four for Wave 3 plus a launch pad for the Mars ascent vehicle (MAV), two for the 2042 support landers, and a crew pad sintered out to 50 m, then after the crew gate one more for the 2045/46 food flight. Other regolith manufacturing stays at pilot scale, because none of it has been demonstrated with Martian soil under Martian conditions.

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) sets the envelope. Construction is a deferrable load of 20–30 kW until October 2038 and 30–40 kW after the second reactor arrives. It also soaks up spare reactor power, about 70–100 kW in the first epoch while the reactor runs, and is the first load to pause when power is short. Wave 1 lands about 65 t of robots and construction kit, broken down by machine in [chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md).

The program delivers, in order of need:

1. Four landing sites for Wave 2 by August 2038 and four for Wave 3 by July 2040
2. Site work before October 2038: for the habitat ([chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md)), grading, a foundation pad, utility and heat-loop trenches from the reactor field, and a covered trench for the water-bank bladders, with a second one for Wave 3's bladders dug before it lands in September 2040; for the greenhouse ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)), a module site and heat-loop connection
3. Blast berms between the landing field and the reactor field, wells, oxygen plant, and habitat, between neighboring landing sites, and between the MAV pad and the sites that land after the MAV
4. Graded haul roads and laydown yards, and a buried power line from the reactor field to the landing field, the MAV pad, and each landing site, with a second feeder to the MAV pad
5. Regolith covers: a berm around each reactor, bays for the propellant tanks and oxygen plant, a robot garage, and a berm around the habitat's lower walls after it lands
6. A launch pad for the MAV before it lands in late September 2040
7. Two landing sites for the support landers arriving in September 2042, one of them built like the crew pad, and a crew pad before the crew lands in September 2044
8. After the crew gate, one more hybrid site for the committed 2045/46 food flight, which lands about October 2046

Landing sites take most of the energy and rover time.

## What gets built first, and why

Two facts set the order: landing plumes throw rock, and the first ships land on unprepared ground.

### Landing plumes dig craters and throw rock

The pathfinder (if flown) and the three Wave 1 landers touch down on unprepared ground, because no robots are on the surface yet. Each landed ship stays where it lands, so every later landing needs a site of its own clear of the ships already down, and the landing field grows with each wave.

Landing plumes have moved soil at every scale flown so far. Apollo 12's plume sandblasted the Surveyor 3 lander 155 m away in 1969, pitting parts the astronauts brought home [1]. On Mars, Phoenix's thrusters blew the dust off a slab of ice beneath the spacecraft in 2008 [2], the engines that lowered Curiosity in 2012 "blew away some of the surface material" [3], and even NASA's small InSight lander dug pits with 3.6 kN of descent thrust [48]. A Starship-class ship lands up to ~100 t of cargo on roughly 2,000 times that thrust ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)).

On the Moon a plume mainly erodes the surface; on Mars the gas also pushes into the soil and can make the ground fail under the jet, so deep cratering dominates. Metzger and colleagues estimated that a human-class lander would make "a deep crater in the martian soil, blowing ejecta to approximately 1 km distance," and calculated a crater at least 2 m deep for a 40 t lander, a deliberate underestimate [4]. In their tests the crater under a supersonic jet kept deepening until it was about as deep as the jet was long, which for a 40 t lander would be 6–9 m [5], [58]. They expect deep cratering "at least when landing in mid-to-low latitudes where there is a deep bed of soil overlying the ice table" [4]. At the reference site the ice table lies less than a meter down (see the berms below), so a deep crater would reach it, as Phoenix's thrusters bared ice a few centimeters beneath that lander [2], and no test has shown how a plume acts on ice-cemented ground. A lander that digs a crater under one leg can tip over. Even for the Artemis lunar landers, NASA's inspector general reported in 2026 that plume predictions rest on Apollo reconstructions and remain "largely uncertain" [6]. No published model yet gives the ground pressure, crater, or burn time of a Starship-class landing on Mars. NASA's plume-surface interaction project is building its flight-scale predictions for the Artemis lunar landers first [57], and SpaceX plans to instrument its uncrewed lunar demonstration, which will also relight its Raptors on the surface for an ascent test [6], so the first data on a Raptor plume meeting the ground will come from the Moon.

Scaled from the largest cases published, a Starship-class landing digs as deep as the ground lets it. Near touchdown a single Raptor carries the ship (below) with 19–26 times the thrust of one engine on Metzger's four-engine 40 t lander, so its jet, and the crater it can dig, is several times longer (this book's scaling), while at the reference site only about 0.3–0.8 m of loose soil lies over the ice. An unprepared landing would bare the ice under the engine. On Starship's first launch in 2023 the exhaust cracked the pad, vaporized the shallow groundwater beneath it, and threw concrete at about 90 m/s as far as 817 m [59]. The same group estimates that a Starship-class ship landing 100 t on Mars, descending as slowly as Apollo's landers did, would throw debris just as fast from ground holding more than about 2% water by weight [60], and fresh craters at these latitudes expose clean ice under the soil [54]. In Mars's gravity and thin air a stone of 5–30 cm launched at that speed carries about 1.9–2.1 km (this book's calculation), twice the 1 km estimate above. With 5% water they put the speed at 125–140 m/s [60], which would carry such stones 3.6–5.1 km (this book's calculation); both estimates assume that all the water in the thrown material turns to vapor at once [60]. A landing site therefore needs hard ground wherever the ship may touch down.

> Hypothesis: On bare icy ground a Starship-class landing clears the loose soil down to the ice and stops there, and its heavier debris flies no faster than the Starship launch's. The crater is about as deep as the loose layer, 0.3–0.8 m, and cuts only centimeters into the ice. The loose soil flies out at low angles: pieces up to about 1 cm carry up to 1–1.8 km, stones of 5 cm or more no farther than about 0.4 km. Debris at the launch's 90 m/s needs water vapor trapped under a crust, which bare ice lacks, so this book plans for about 90 m/s and about 2 km as the upper case and treats 125–140 m/s as a bound. Basis: Phoenix's thrusters cleared the soil down to ice a few centimeters below the surface, and the ice limited the damage: "the ice was effectively the landing pad covered by a thin layer of soil" [80], [81]. NASA takes lunar regolith holding 4% water to be 1.5–2 MPa strong [76], at least twice the highest pressure under a Raptor near touchdown, and if a few tenths of a percent to a few percent of the jet's energy reaches the ground as heat, the last 5–8 s sublimate 0.3–2.5 cm of soil holding 13–15% water, about 13 cm even at a tenth (this book's estimate). On Apollo's landings dust left flat ground at 1–3° and a shallow crater at 7–11° [4], and Metzger's ballistics method, rerun with a Raptor's longer jet, gives the ranges above (this book's calculation from [4]). At the Starship launch only about 1% of the thrown mass became gas, because heat reached only the top of the wet sand under the cracked pad [59]. These analogs show the mechanisms at other scales: Phoenix's thrusters were small and pulsed, and the launch pad had a concrete cap over liquid groundwater. Test: Earth plume tests at Mars pressure over ice-cemented soil, before the Wave 1 target freezes in December 2034 and again for G2, and Wave 1's own craters and debris, mapped out to 3 km before G2. A crater that cuts more than about 0.1 m into the ice, or stones of a few centimeters beyond 2 km, would make 125–140 m/s the planning case.

> Assumption: The landing field sits at least 2 km from the reactor field, wells, and habitat, with berms on the line of sight to each. Justification: Metzger and colleagues put ejecta from a human-class lander on Mars at about 1 km [4], and NASA's human Mars lander study likewise kept landings 1 km from any surface asset [52]; a Starship-class ship is larger, debris thrown where its plume reaches ice may carry about 2 km (above), and distance plus berms protects against a spray pattern nobody has measured. Sites inside the field sit about 1 km apart, at the edge of the dry-ground estimate and inside the icy-ground one, which is one more reason the prepared sites must keep the plume off the ice. A berm therefore also stands between neighboring sites, though a high arc clears it, and the first landings must measure the spray.

> Assumption: Wave 1's three ships, which land on bare ground, touch down at least 2 km apart, and the reactor does not ride the first of them. Justification: a landing on bare ground reaches the ice, and its upper-case debris carries about 2 km (above), while the wider spacing costs Wave 1 only a few more kilometers of driving and towing if the reactor's ship lands nearest the reactor field. The first ship down lands with the least knowledge of the ground and stands in reach of both later plumes, so the reactor rides a later ship, as [chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md) plans; landing it last would put it out of reach of every other Wave 1 plume and give its aim point both earlier touchdowns, and this book prefers that wherever the launch order allows it. If the first ship's own cameras show its crater cutting more than about 0.1 m into the ice instead of stopping at it, the ships still in flight re-aim to about 4–5 km apart, the reach of the 125–140 m/s bound. The prepared sites stay about 1 km apart, because their armor rock must lie within about 1 km (below) and a 2 km field would double the feeds and roads; for them the protection is a recipe that keeps the plume off the ice, which the G2 tests must show. The alternative is to land Wave 1 about 1 km apart as well and accept the risk until its measured landings and the G2 tests show the reach.

Blast berms, ridges of piled regolith between a landing site and the assets it protects, cost little energy. NASA's Regolith Advanced Surface Systems Operations Robot (RASSOR) 2.0 excavator, a 66 kg prototype, was designed to dig at least 2.7 t of regolith per day and to draw about 4 W for each kilogram per hour of digging rate [7]. That works out to about 4 kWh per tonne moved. A berm 3 m high and 150 m long holds roughly 3,000 t, or about 12 MWh of excavation energy; this book assumes one such berm for each new landing site, set between it and its neighbors, one of the same size around each reactor, and one between the MAV pad and the sites that land after the MAV.

### Roads stay mostly unsintered

Haul roads cross the 2 km between the landing field and the working area. Sintering one road 2 km long and 6 m wide as thinly as the 20–50 m ring of a landing site (sizing table below) would cost about 500 MWh, two to three years of the first epoch's allocated construction power. Robots grade and compact the surface, clear rocks larger than a few centimeters, and sinter only turning pads and the laydown yards behind the berms, where heavy cargo waits for installation.

### A buried line carries power to the landing field

The sintering rovers work in the landing field, at least 2 km from the reactor field, and cannot carry their own power: a sol of sintering at 25 kW is about 620 kWh, some 3.7 t of batteries at [chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)'s 165 Wh/kg, six times the rover's own mass. The armor teams and excavators working in the field charge there too. So the landing field gets a power line of its own before sintering starts.

> Assumption: A buried aluminum line at 1,000 Vdc, the transmission voltage NASA's Mars power study assumed (chapter 5), runs about 2.5 km from the reactor field to a switching station beside the MAV pad, behind the pad's berm. It is sized for 100 kW, three sintering rovers plus the chargers for the robots working in the field, with 5% loss at full load. Buried feeds at the same voltage, about 11 km in all, run on from the station past every planned landing site, each sized for two sintering rovers. That is about 9 t of aluminum conductor, some 14 t with insulation and jackets, from Wave 1's unitemized mass ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)), and the line loses about 5–7% of the energy it carries. The MAV's cryocoolers draw their few kilowatts through the same station during its hold ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)), with a second feeder of their own as the backup (below). Justification: this book's estimate; chapter 5 allows more than 500 V only in buried or covered cable, and burial also protects the line from landing ejecta.

> Hypothesis: Buried in cold ground, the line loses about a third less than its planning figure: about 3–5% of what it carries instead of 5–7%, some 15 MWh less in the first epoch. Basis: aluminum's resistance falls as it cools, and at 200–214 K the conductor grade's resistivity is 62–68% of its value at 20 °C (this book's calculation from the recommended values for pure aluminum [86]); 0.4 m down, the ground's temperature averages over the seasons to its annual mean, which at 42°N is no warmer than Mars's global average of about 214 K [87], and at the loads it carries the line warms itself by some 5–15 K, since the dry soil around it insulates, which trims the gain to about 27–35% (this book's estimate). Test: the switching station meters the line's loss from first power in April 2036, and the planning figure stays until those readings replace it.

> Assumption: The trunk keeps the conductor sized for 5% loss at full load. Justification: a trunk sized for 3% would take about 2.5 t more aluminum, some 3 t with insulation, to save about 12 MWh of the first epoch's losses, 1% of its work, and only about 8 MWh in cold ground; the same 2.5 t of solar field, at [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s ~10 t per 400 kW, would add about 7–8 kW sol-average in clear skies, some 120–140 MWh over the first epoch at 85% availability (this book's calculation). NASA's study of lunar fission power transmission likewise sizes the cable for 95% efficiency over 3 km [88], as a 1994 study of lunar transmission lines asked for more than 95% [89]. The switch: if Wave 1 cannot carry more array, or the line's measured loss runs above plan, the thicker trunk is the fallback; a higher voltage would cut the loss at no mass, but NASA notes that flight-rated switches reach only 175 V and that radiation-hardened switches for 400–1,200 V still need development [88]; the line's own 1,000 V already rests on that development, and a higher voltage would add converter risk.

The trench, about 0.3 m wide and 0.4 m deep, also carries [chapter 10](10.%20Communications.md)'s fiber to the landing field. Its 13.5 km mean about 2,400 t of digging and about 10 MWh. Four of the eight excavators dig the trunk and the feeds to the first two sites in about a month from first power in April 2036, so the line is live before reactor 1 reaches full power in June; the feeds to the other two Wave 2 sites follow before their cores start, and the rest after the berms. In the same weeks the other four dig the trench for the water-bank bladders, about 1,050 t, done by mid-May, because the wells start banking water at first power; the few cubic meters made before then wait in the trench's finished end. All eight then cover the garage frame, about 800 t by this book's estimate, by the end of May 2036, months before the robots need the garage ([chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)).

> Assumption: A second feeder reaches the MAV pad on its own route: about 2.5–3 km of buried 1,000 Vdc cable from the other side of the ring to a small converter behind the pad's berm, apart from the switching station. The converter is rated 15 kW, room for two MAVs' coolers at 3–4 kWe each and their keep-alive, or for one MAV's coolers at the 10–13 kWe they would draw at NASA's 10 W/m² ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)); the cable is sized for the trunk's 5% loss at 10 kW and carries 15 kW at about 7.5%. Made like terrestrial 1 kV aluminum cable, whose insulation and sheath at this size weigh as much as the aluminum or more [109], that is about 0.9–1.2 t, from Wave 2's unitemized mass ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)), and some 450–540 t of trench, about a week of the ten excavators and about 2 MWh, dug after the Wave 3 berms and before the MAV lands in September 2040 (this book's calculation). Justification: with the trunk alone, a fault on it or at the switching station would leave the pad on its battery for about a sol, after which the MAV's tanks vent about 0.1–0.2 t of methane in two weeks ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)). The plant can remake that, but not on the branch where it has failed and methane from Earth has replaced it, and the MAV's own avionics need power throughout. The second path keeps the pad inside [chapter 5](05.%20Power.md#the-microgrid-120-vdc-on-two-independent-buses)'s rule that no single cable, converter, or bus fault cuts a critical load.

### Shelters and shields, not pressure vessels

In compression, sintered and printed lunar-simulant parts range from about 2.5 MPa (solar-sintered average) to about 94 MPa (laser-melted, with wide scatter). Like other ceramics, they are weak and brittle in tension [9], [45]. A pressure vessel works in tension, and no published test shows a printed or sintered regolith shell holding pressure. The colony therefore uses regolith only for shields and covers: a berm around each reactor, the arrangement fission surface power concepts use ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)); covered bays that damp thermal swings and keep dust off the propellant tanks and oxygen plant; an imported garage frame covered with regolith for [chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)'s workshop; and a berm around the habitat's lower walls, where its water shield is thinnest ([chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md)). Every pressurized volume is built on Earth or assembled from Earth-made membranes and liners.

## Processes and where they were tested

### Excavators exist only as ground prototypes

RASSOR, developed by the Swamp Works laboratory at NASA's Kennedy Space Center (KSC), digs with counterrotating bucket drums, which give "near-zero horizontal and minimal vertical net reaction force" [13], an advantage in low gravity. RASSOR 2.0 was built with Martian water mining in mind and demonstrated teleoperated and autonomous modes on Earth [7]. Its lunar successor, the Infrastructure Pilot Excavator (IPEx), is meant to dig up to 10,000 kg of regolith in one lunar day and reached TRL 5 in 2024 [14]. Neither has flown. The plan's drum excavators and heavier bladed rovers are design assumptions, yet every berm, trench, and road of the first epoch depends on them.

The only outdoor demonstration of landing-pad construction took place in Hawaii in October–December 2015, when the Pacific International Space Center for Exploration Systems and Swamp Works used a rover teleoperated from KSC to place 100 interlocking basalt pavers into a 3 m × 3 m pad inside a gravel apron 20 m in diameter [15]. The pavers were kiln-fired beforehand, so the test demonstrated robotic placement, not in situ manufacturing.

### Sintering and melting: microwaves lead

Sintering heats regolith until its grains fuse without fully melting; three energy sources have been tested, all on lunar simulant. Microwaves penetrate several centimeters and heat the soil through its depth; solid-state microwave heating of lunar simulant has made gravel at a temperature about 300 K below a conventional furnace's [16].

Concentrated sunlight was the basis of the European RegoLight project (2015–2018) [8]. It printed brick-sized parts and reached TRL 5 [44], but its solar-sintered parts averaged only about 2.5 MPa in compression, and none exceeded 5 MPa [9], [45]. The European Space Agency (ESA) made brick-scale solar-sintered blocks for a lunar shell concept; it did not make shells [17].

Lasers melt regolith instead of sintering it. A 2023 study melted lunar simulant EAC-1A with lasers of 3–10 kW at about 1 Pa, made 250 × 250 mm paving elements, and measured a mean compressive strength of 94 MPa with a standard deviation of 56 MPa [9]. The scatter matters more than the mean, because a structural engineer designs to the weak end of the distribution.

Mars simulant results are few: Gupta and colleagues pressed bricks of the Mars Global Simulant (MGS-1) with a polymer binder and sintered them at 1,150 °C in a furnace in air. Strength rose from 24 MPa after a 10-minute hold to a peak of 41 MPa. The same paper estimates that a brick needs about 6 MPa just to carry its own weight in a Martian structure [18]. Only one Mars-simulant study has fired parts in a Mars-like atmosphere [43].

> Assumption: Microwave sintering is the baseline for landing-site cores. Justification: it heats the full layer in one pass; in the only published cost trade it is the most favorable method for the inner zone of a lunar pad, "although other methods are within the range of uncertainty" [19]; and it does not depend on Mars's weak sunlight.

## Mars is not the Moon

Five differences decide whether a lunar result carries over.

The first is the atmosphere: several hundred pascals of CO₂ ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)) add conduction and convection losses to every hot process. Lunar sintering tests in vacuum found macropores and lower density than sintering in air [10]. On Mars simulant, the pores come largely from gas: sulfates, carbonates, hydrated minerals, and reduced iron oxides release it on heating, and parts bloat once the surface seals [43]. Perchlorates in real soil would add more gas; no test has included them. In that one Mars-atmosphere test, MGS-1 ceramics fired at 925 Pa of CO₂ bloated unless prefired in air, and after that change came within about 7% of the air-fired bending strength (53.5 against 57.6 MPa) [43].

The second is sulfate. Average Martian soil measured by rover X-ray spectrometers holds 4.9 ± 0.7 wt% SO₃, and airborne dust about 7.4 wt%, almost all as sulfate minerals; some sulfate-rich soils in Gusev crater exceed 30 wt% [11]. Heated sulfates release SO₂, which corrodes electrodes and furnaces and contaminates products.

The third is perchlorate ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md) covers its toxicity): 0.4–0.6 wt% at the Phoenix site [12] and about 0.4 wt% at Rocknest in Gale crater [11]. Heated, it releases oxygen and chlorine, which any furnace or electrolysis cell fed raw soil must handle.

The fourth is oxidation. Lunar soil contains metallic iron and almost no ferric iron. In typical Martian soil about a fifth of the iron is ferric (Fe³⁺), and the dust is more oxidized still [11]. Oxidation state changes melting behavior, microwave coupling, and the electrical work needed to reduce oxides to metal.

The fifth is sunlight and gravity. With 43% of Earth's sunlight at the top of the atmosphere, and far less in dust storms, solar sintering is harder than on the Moon, while 0.38 g gives excavators more traction and shrinks the advantage of lunar low-reaction-force designs.

> Assumption: Every lunar-simulant process in this chapter needs a Mars requalification campaign (Mars simulant with sulfate and perchlorate, CO₂ at Mars pressure, Mars temperatures) before its numbers enter the plan. Justification: the one sintering test in a Mars-like atmosphere worked only after a process change [43]; sulfur concrete made from the same simulant differs more than tenfold in strength between two labs and procedures, for reasons no study has isolated [20], [22]; and no test has used perchlorate-bearing feed.

## How large a landing site must be, and how long it takes

The only published estimate of pad construction energy and time is Metzger and Autry's trade study of lunar landing pads [19]. It finds the sintering hardware light; power and robot time are the limits. The study models a pad for a 40 t-class lander, with an inner zone of radius 12 m sintered 7.62 cm deep and an outer zone out to 27 m sintered 2.54 cm deep. With 200 kW and about 3.5 t of microwave hardware the pad takes under three weeks. Held to no more than 50 kW, the study's cost-optimized systems peak at 18–25 kW, carry only 316–445 kg of sintering hardware, and take 192–270 days. Those low-power cases need about 110 MWh, about a quarter more than the 87.5 MWh at 200 kW, and this chapter scales from them.

> Assumption: Each landing site is the full circle of 100 m radius set by [chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)'s landing accuracy, because a ship can touch down anywhere within 100 m of the beacon. The inner 20 m radius is sintered 7.6 cm deep, like the lunar inner zone; from 20 to 100 m the ground is graded, cleared of loose rock, compacted, and armored with sorted rock, following the study's gravel option. Justification: sintering the whole circle would take more energy than the first epoch has (table below). No published study sizes a Starship-class pad on Mars. The 7.6 cm depth was sized for a 40 t lander, so a Starship-class core may need to be thicker, at higher energy. The 100 m bound itself is unqualified for heavy landers with beacons; gate G2 reads Wave 1's touchdown errors against it (chapter 4).

> Assumption: Mars conditions raise the energy per cubic meter by 20%. Justification: soil starts near −60 °C instead of 127 °C, a 17% larger temperature rise to 1,200 °C, plus an allowance for decomposing sulfates and perchlorates and for heat lost to the CO₂ atmosphere.

Energy scales with the volume heated, not the area. The lunar pad's ~110 MWh sinters about 81 m³ (the 7.62 cm inner zone and the 2.54 cm outer zone), about 1.36 MWh per cubic meter, or 1.63 MWh with the Mars allowance.

In the lunar study, sorting and placing rock over the 1,838 m² outer zone took 35 days with two rovers and less than 1 MWh [19]; scaled by area to the 30,159 m² ring from 20 to 100 m, one two-rover team needs about 19 months and about 14 MWh per site. The study's rock layer is 22.86 cm thick: four layers of rock from 1.5 to 15 cm across, the smallest at the bottom, each held down by the one above it, raked from the top 10 cm of the soil and sorted by size [19]. Scaling by area keeps that layer, so this book takes it as the Mars armor until the G2 tests below confirm or change it. Packed at the study's 2.2 t/m³, it is about 15,000 t of rock per site, some 61,000 t for Wave 2's four sites, nearly three times the berms, and about 175,000 t for all twelve armored sites of the campaign, the 2045/46 food flight's included (this book's calculation).

Sintering the whole circle, about 1,400 MWh per site, is out of reach: one site would take more than all the first epoch's spare reactor energy (about 1,220 MWh at 85% availability, [chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)), and the plan needs four before Wave 2. Four hybrid sites need about 680 MWh, which fits. Cores twice as thick would not: four such sites need about 1,300 MWh, about 1,470 MWh with the rest of the first epoch's work, and the two sintering rovers would need about 40 months at 85% availability against the 26 months from June 2036 to the August 2038 inspection deadline, finishing a year after Wave 2 lands. Even a third sintering rover in Wave 1 would finish them only in late August 2038, too late to inspect, so a thicker core would need more sintering rovers and more power in the first epoch, or a later Wave 2. The rock armor is the constraint: four sites before October 2038 need about 76 team-months of rock work, so four teams must start in early 2036. Sintering is the second. The two sintering rovers ([chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)) carry 25 kW heads, the top of the study's cost-optimized range, and need about 17 months of running time for the four Wave 2 cores. At the 85% availability this chapter plans on (below), that is about 20 months: starting as soon as reactor 1 reaches full power in June 2036 and running on spare power through the line to the landing field, they finish around February 2038, about eight months before Wave 2 lands. At the study's low end, 18 kW, they would finish only about a week before Wave 2 lands, with no time left to inspect the sites, so 25 kW is a requirement on the hardware. Wave 2 brings a third sintering rover for the four Wave 3 cores and the MAV pad, about 810 MWh; with the two Wave 1 rovers starting on them in February 2038, that work is done by October 2039, about ten months before it is needed.

> Assumption: Four teams, each a heavy rover and a hauler from the Wave 1 fleet ([chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)), place rock armor full time from March 2036, joined by a fifth when Wave 2's rovers arrive. At 85% availability they deliver about 99 team-months before Wave 2, a margin of about 30%, and finish around January 2038. Rock for the armor lies within about 1 km of the landing sites, because the landing field is placed beside a rock field, the boulder field around a crater about a kilometer or more across, picked from HiRISE images before the Wave 1 target freezes and checked by the pathfinder or Wave 1's survey. The smooth, rock-poor ground [chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md) chose for landing holds far too little, and no such field lies near the working point (below).

Each hauler carries 1.5 t, so Wave 2's rock is about 40,000 loads: 18 a sol for each of the four armor haulers at 85% availability, or about 14 once the three berm haulers join them after the berms. From a source 1 km away that is 29–36 km of driving a sol, inside [chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)'s hauler duty but two to three times the fleet average behind its drive-module life; from 2 km it would be 57–72 km, beyond what the haulers can drive. The landing ground itself will not supply the rock. The lunar study took 4% of the soil by mass as usable rock, a model input tied to no measurement [19], and even at that content raking the top 10 cm clean yields only about 6 kg per m². The Mars plains chosen for landing hold less. SpaceX's site studies required rocks to cover less than 10% of the ground and favored Arcadia sites on a unit with "smooth, flat rock-free surfaces" [49]; on the smooth plains landers have visited, rocks cover about 1–4% of the ground at InSight and 2–4% at Phoenix [50], [51]. At those covers the rock-size model fitted to landing-site counts [50] puts only about 1–3 kg of stones 1.5–15 cm across on each square meter, so one site's 15,000 t would take 5–19 km² picked clean, against about 3 km² within 1 km of a site. Even ground as rocky as the Viking 2 site, about 20% cover [50], holds only about 9 kg per m² in that size range, some 1.7 km² per site and 7 km² for Wave 2's four (this book's calculation). The armor therefore needs a rock field within about 1 km. The 30% margin covers a layer up to about 30 cm thick if the G2 tests call for one, with the hauling growing in proportion.

The data show no rock field near the working point. Thermal maps, which count the rocks that stay warm at night, put rocks over about 5% of the ground within 20 km of it (2–8%) in the newer map and 8–12% in the older, coarser one, and over no more than about 20% of any pixel in the 40–44°N band [63], [64]; at face value that is about 2–6 kg of armor-size stone per m² (this book's analysis of the maps). On these plains rock fields ring young craters. At the InSight site, fresh craters wider than about 200 m scatter boulders out to about one crater diameter beyond the rim, and rocks cover up to about 36% of the ground next to it [65], [66]. In Arcadia a crater must first dig through the ice-rich layer, whose base lies some 40–50 m down [67], so at an excavation depth of about 0.084 times the crater's width [54] only craters of roughly half a kilometer or more can throw rock (this book's estimate). The Robbins crater catalog lists just 27 craters of 1 km or more in the whole band [68]. The nearest lie 29 km (a worn crater 4.6 km across) and 37 km from the working point, and the CTX mosaic shows one smaller crater, about 0.5 km across, 9 km to the west [69] (this book's reading). HiRISE resolves boulders larger than about 1.5 m, which the rock model ties to the smaller stones [50], and it has imaged the crater 37 km away, about 1.3 km across. Counted in that image, its boulders amount to about 15–30% rock cover out to half a crater radius beyond the rim and 11–17% at one radius, falling below about 7% one crater diameter out, while the plains around it show few or none; a very fresh 1.5 km crater 211 km away is somewhat less rocky (this book's count of HiRISE images [70], [71]). By the model that field holds some 29,000–39,000 t of armor-size stone within one crater radius of the rim and 54,000–79,000 t within one diameter, about two to five sites' worth (this book's calculation). The thermal maps do not show it, because it covers a few square kilometers inside pixels about ten times larger, so only HiRISE can find such fields.

> Hypothesis: Near the working point a crater's rock supply grows much faster than its area, because only the part of its cavity below the ice-rich layer holds rock. So the landing field goes to the rock, beside a crater's boulder field chosen with the final site. By the rock model, Wave 2's four sites need about one field like the 1.3 km crater's, and all twelve armored sites about three, or one crater about 2 km across (this book's calculation). Fresh ejecta may hold fewer small stones than the model predicts from its boulders [65], though at the InSight site the first counts that pointed that way came from images too noisy to catch the smaller rocks, and better images found far more of them [65]. The counts here show how much rides on the answer. Near the 1.3 km crater's rim, boulders of 3 m and more are a third or more of those of 1.5 m and more, where the model allows under 1%, so the model does not describe these boulders, and what lies below 1.5 m depends on how the sizes continue (this book's count). If the boulders' own slope held down to pebbles, as a single slope does from 1 mm to about 1.7 m on the ejecta of the young lunar crater Tycho, where Surveyor 7 counted 2,077 fragments [82], the field would hold only about 1,000–6,000 t of stones 1.5–15 cm across within one crater diameter of its rim, less than half of one site's rock. On the older lunar mare sites the same counts fall off more steeply, with slopes of 2.1–2.7 [82], which on these craters' boulders would leave from about a sixth to nearly three times the model's small stone (this book's calculation). On Earth, the ejecta of Lonar crater in India, 1.8 km across and dug into basalt, are sand and pebbles over cobbles, with boulders of a decimeter to a meter common near the rim; field counts give their pebbles and cobbles a slope of 2.5 and their boulders 3.4 [95]. Below these craters' counted boulders, that shape would give five to eight times the model's small stone, but the boulders' own slope down to about 25 cm with Lonar's 2.5 beneath would give only a quarter to under half (this book's calculation), so the answer lies in the sizes between about 25 cm and 1.5 m, most of them below what HiRISE resolves. The ejecta of Meteor Crater in Arizona, 1.2 km across, run from millimeter chips to blocks several meters across [84]; about half their volume is coarse blocks set in finer crushed rock, and weathering washes the fines away and leaves a surface of coarse debris [83]. Counting every stone from 1.5 cm to 1.5 m, and crushing those larger than 15 cm, the field holds at least about 25,000–95,000 t within one crater diameter of its rim unless the sizes thin out below the boulders HiRISE counts, because most of that mass lies in stones of 0.4–1.5 m just below them (this book's calculation). A thinner layer and more sintering remain the fallbacks if even that falls short.

> Assumption: Before gate G2 in June 2037 the armor recipe passes two tests ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). On Mars, the robots build an armored test strip in 2036 and fire a gas jet at it at the ground pressures and durations that plume models give for a Starship-class landing, while the craters under the Wave 1 landers, which land on unprepared ground, calibrate those models. The jet is CO₂ frozen out of the air by a small cryocooler like the oxygen liquefier's, warmed in two closed tanks of about 2 m³ each to some 4 MPa, and blown through a nozzle on a heavy rover's crane arm; with its valve, mount, and instruments the rig is about 0.4–0.6 t from Wave 1's unitemized mass. For a Raptor that patch is 5–8 m wide, and the ship's weight on it averages about 15–45 kPa, with peaks of about 30–90 kPa, close to Metzger's figures; only as the nozzle nears the ground does the pressure beneath it approach the thrust over the nozzle's exit area, about 0.5–0.7 MPa, the measure Dotson and colleagues used for Raptor's plume [59] (this book's calculation). NASA's human Mars lander designs hold a steady 2.5 m/s over the last 12–20 m, about 5–8 s of burn near the ground [52], [56], though the plume moves soil long before: Curiosity's descent stage started moving soil about 63 m up, some 25 s before touchdown, or about 315 nozzle widths [62], which for a Raptor spans the whole terminal descent (this book's scaling). The rig is sized to hold about 0.3 MPa on its spot for those 5–8 s, several times the patch's peaks: the jet then takes about 40 kg of gas a second, and the two tanks give a shot of about 5–9 s, or 20–40 s at 71 kPa for the long approach and 2–4 s at about 0.7 MPa for the final second, when the nozzle is nearest the ground; the freezer refills them in two to four weeks, enough for 10–20 shots before G2 (this book's estimates). A spot that small tests the armor at full pressure but not at a plume's full size, and the gas flow through the soil that drives deep cratering may not scale from small jets to large ones [4], so the Earth tests carry the scale. If the armor fails, the colony sinters rings out to 50 m, which puts about three landings in four on sintered ground at about 270 MWh and about nine months of both sintering rovers' time per site.

The first epoch's berms (~90 MWh at 4 kWh/t) mean about 22,000 t of regolith, and none of them can wait for Wave 2: the line-of-sight berms protect hardware already on the ground, and the berms between sites protect each Wave 2 ship from its neighbors' landings. > Assumption: Each berm is built from a shallow borrow ditch dug beside it, on the side away from the landing sites, so haulers carry its regolith a few hundred meters instead of the 2 km between the landing field and the working area. The ditches take only the loose soil above the ice table, stopping short of the ice that the survey scouts' radar maps along each berm line. The plan takes that soil as about 0.3 m deep, so a 3,000 t berm draws a ditch about 45 m wide, or about 67 m where only 0.2 m lies over the ice; the drums cut thin layers in repeated passes, so a wider, shallower ditch moves the same tonnage in about the same time (this book's estimate). Justification: a berm needs no sorted material, and the short run lets the three haulers not tied to the rock armor keep ahead of the excavators ([chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)). RASSOR's 2.7 t per day is a design minimum, tested on regolith simulant [7]; ice-cemented ground digs far slower (below), so the plan does not dig it. At Phoenix, the scoop's titanium blade "did little to excavate into the icy surface" [53].

The ice lies close to the surface at the working point. Fresh craters in the Arcadia region find the top of the ice 0.26–0.58 m down at 44–46°N and 0.69–1.1 m down at 39°N, and the depth varies over tens to hundreds of meters [54]; thermal mapping shows ice within 30 cm over much of the region ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md)); at 68°N, Phoenix found icy soil 1.3–11 cm down, 4.6 cm on average [53]. The SWIM project's thermal layer, the average of three thermal ice maps, is sharper at the working point: in its 3 km pixel no map puts the ice shallower than 30 cm and at least one puts it within 50 cm, while pixels 3–5 km to the north and west have one map at about 30 cm [72], [73]. The neutron maps, blurred over hundreds of kilometers, give the ground there about 13–15% water by mass below a dry top layer, about half the ~25% at which ice fills the soil's pores and above which it must be excess ice [74] (this book's analysis of the SWIM products). Together they put the top of the icy ground at the working point about 0.3–0.5 m down, shallower in patches a few kilometers away, and the ice that craters expose about 0.45–0.8 m down (this book's reading of them), so a ditch 0.5 m deep would meet icy ground over much of the field and one of 0.3 m would just clear it at the working point. At 0.3 m the first epoch's berms take about 49,000 m² of borrow ground, about 73,000 m² at 0.2 m, and the digging time does not change; where the loose soil is thinner still, a berm draws from farther away and the haul lengthens. How much of the landing field is like that is a survey question for the pathfinder or Wave 1's scouts, answered before the berm lines are fixed.

The same limit bounds the other digging: the 0.4 m power-line trench, and the bladder trenches beside a well field sited on shallow ice by design, will meet icy ground in places, and digging into it costs far more. Freezing makes soil 10–100 times stronger [75]. NASA takes lunar regolith with 4% water to be 1.5–2 MPa strong and with 10% water 20–35 MPa [76]. In its Break the Ice finals, on a 1.5–2 MPa concrete standing in for the weaker lunar case, robots of 250–770 kg dug 12–312 kg an hour at 0.8–39 kWh a tonne, the one with RASSOR-style drums 170 kg an hour at 2.4 kWh [77]. Machines on Earth spend about 1–90 MJ per cubic meter cutting frozen soil [75], [78], against 0.02–0.1 MJ for a bucket in loose simulant [79]. If the ice lay 0.2 m down along the whole power line, half the trench, about 1,500 t, would be icy. At the drum robot's pace, scaled to the 0.2 t excavators, four of them would need about 4.5 months and some 4 MWh for it in ground like the stand-in, and years in ground ten to twenty times stronger, as soil with ice-filled pores at Mars temperatures may be (this book's estimate). A bladder trench dug 1 m deep over ice 0.3–0.5 m down would be 50–70% icy: two to three months of digging for the first one instead of about five weeks at the weak end, and years at the strong end.

> Assumption: The trenches follow the berms' rule. Where the scouts' radar finds ice shallower than a trench's planned depth, the route bends to deeper ice where it can, and otherwise the cut stops at the ice and the cover is built up above grade: the cable keeps its cover of loose soil, and the bladders lie in a wider, shallower cut under a raised cover. Justification: chapter 5 allows more than 500 V only in buried or covered cable, and the bladders need cover more than depth; the extra soil comes from the borrow ditches, and a raised cover near the landing sites may need armor against plumes (this book's estimate).

> Hypothesis: Along a berm or trench line the depth to the icy ground varies by up to about a quarter of its local median over tens to hundreds of meters, and more where rocks or polygons break the ground. Basis: clusters of fresh craters near 44°N and 46°N found the ice 0.26–0.55 m and 0.28–0.58 m down around means of 0.47 and 0.42 m, which, with a measurement error of about ±30% per crater, bounds the spread (this book's reading of [54]); at Phoenix the depth changed more than tenfold from trench to trench [53]; and in Antarctica's University Valley the ice table deepens from 0 to about 60 cm along a valley 1.5 km long [91]. At 42°N the ice lies equatorward of about 49°, the latitude down to which ground ice is stable in today's climate, and an ice table near that boundary retreats [90]; the southernmost exposed ice, near 39°N, may be subliming now [54], so the depth here reflects the dry layer left as it retreated rather than a local balance, which is why it varies. > Assumption: Each cut is dug to the depth the scouts' radar reads along its own line, less 0.1 m, and never deeper than planned; where that leaves less than about 0.15 m, the line moves or the cover is built up, and a drum that meets ice anyway, as its motor current shows, stops and steps up. Justification: a radar like ExoMars's WISDOM resolves the shallow subsurface to about 3 cm [92], so with the depth read along the cut a 0.1 m margin leaves at most a few percent of it on ice, against about 7–21% when the depth comes from a grid whose lines lie farther apart than the depth changes (this book's calculation). Phoenix read icy soil from the motor current of its rasp [53], and Antarctic field teams work the same way: in University Valley they remove the dry soil down to the ice table and core the ice-cemented ground below with a drill [91]. Digging through the ice anyway costs, per tonne, about the loose-soil energy at 40% of the pace in weak icy ground, and ten to twenty times both in strong ground; thawing it first would take about 30 kWh of heat a tonne to melt its 13–15% ice, or 120–135 kWh to drive it off as vapor, which only waste heat beside the heat loops could supply (this book's calculation).

A heat-loop pipe run near the ice does not thaw it. The insulated line loses about 16 W a meter, which warms ice-cemented ground 0.1–0.3 m away by some 5–12 K, while that ground sits 60–70 K below melting on a planet whose mean surface temperature is about 214 K [87] (this book's calculation). On Earth the case is reversed: where the Trans-Alaska Pipeline crosses permafrost its heat could thaw, the line runs above ground on supports [93].

Each needs an imported input or a factory the colony lacks. Sulfur concrete and metals come closer.

Sulfur concrete needs no water: sulfur melts at about 115–120 °C, the concrete is placed at 130–140 °C, and it gains most of its strength within hours. Unlike on the Moon, sulfur does not sublime away at Martian pressure [20]. Two facts limit it.

First, the soil holds sulfur as sulfate, and no Mars-relevant extraction reactor has been built [21]. Soil with 5 wt% SO₃ holds 2 wt% sulfur, so a concrete that is 60 wt% sulfur needs the sulfur from about 30 t of soil per tonne, even at full recovery. Curiosity found pure sulfur stones in Gale crater in 2024 [42], but a site plan cannot assume such a deposit.

Second, results swing widely. From the same simulant, JSC-Mars-1A, sulfur concrete reached about 50 MPa mixed in air [20] but only 3.66 ± 0.25 MPa when heated under flowing CO₂ at about ambient pressure in another lab. That lab ran no in-air control and blamed decomposing minerals and uneven binder, not the gas [22]. Mixes of MMS-1 and MMS-2 made in air reached 43–76 MPa [23]. In this plan sulfur concrete serves only for small parts and as grout between pavers, a use the lunar pad study proposes [19].

Three regolith reduction processes make metal, all tested on lunar simulant. They also make oxygen, but Mars gets oxygen more cheaply from its air: [chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)'s CO₂ electrolysis is the process the Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment (MOXIE) demonstrated on Perseverance [31].

Molten regolith electrolysis passes current through regolith melted at about 1,600 °C. Oxygen forms at an inert anode, where iridium is the demonstrated choice, and mainly iron and silicon at the cathode; aluminum and titanium need deeper reduction [26]. NASA has modeled a lunar plant [27]. Martian sulfate and chlorine would release SO₂ and Cl₂ at the anode unless the feed is cleaned first.

In the Fray-Farthing-Chen (FFC) Cambridge process, Lomax and colleagues' inert tin-oxide anode in molten calcium chloride removed 96% of the oxygen from lunar simulant at 950 °C and left a mixed-metal alloy powder. Only about a third of that oxygen reached the off-gas as O₂; the rest corroded the reactor vessel [28]. ESA follow-up work with graphite anodes, which release the oxygen as CO and CO₂, removed 40–85% of it [29].

Carbothermal reduction heats regolith with carbon, which on Mars can come from atmospheric CO₂. Sierra Space's Carbothermal Oxygen Production Reactor (COPR) passed thermal-vacuum testing at about 1,800 °C in 2024, maturing its key hardware to TRL 6 for lunar use [30].

> Assumption: The colony's first metal line is a single carbothermal or electrolysis pilot plant delivered in Wave 3, sized for tens of kilograms of iron-silicon feedstock per month, with a front end that bakes off sulfate and perchlorate. Justification: metals are useful for spares and tooling ([chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)) but lie off the critical path to the crew gate, and no process has been run on Martian feed.

Large-scale printing is still a lunar program. NASA's Moon to Mars Planetary Autonomous Construction Technology (MMPACT) project is maturing autonomous construction for lunar conditions, including in-process verification of printed structures [37], [38]. A lunar flight demonstration that was planned in 2021 for 2026 [46] still had no date in 2026 [37]. ICON's Project Olympus works under MMPACT on a $57.2 million Small Business Innovation Research Phase III contract through 2028 [39], and its Duneflow experiment compared simulant with Apollo regolith in about two minutes of simulated lunar gravity on a suborbital flight in February 2025 [37]. ICON's goal of building on the Moon by 2026 [47] was not met, and neither program has flown hardware to another world.

## What would change this conclusion

Everything here assumes rock armor survives a Starship-class plume on Mars, where loose rock can become projectiles. The pathfinder and Wave 1 ships land on unprepared ground and measure the plume's ejecta; Wave 2's landings are the first on armor, after it passes the tests that gate G2 requires. If it fails them, the rings above set the pace, and the whole campaign moves.

Between a failed test and Wave 2's landing, the two sintering rovers finish rings on one or two sites, so only one or two Wave 2 ships fly, carrying reactor 2, the habitat, and the third sintering rover; the rest wait for 2039, when three rovers can ring their sites in time. Wave 3's four ringed sites and the MAV pad need about 2,170 MWh more, far beyond what three rovers can add before the 2039 window, so Wave 3 moves to 2041, the support flight to 2043, and the crew to the 2045/46 window, departing about January 2046. Even that holds only if the Wave 2 ships that wait for 2039 carry three more sintering rovers: three rovers would finish about half of Wave 3's sintering by mid-2042, while six, drawing about 150 kW at the top of the two-reactor headroom, finish all of it; with three, Wave 3 and the crew slip another window. Ringed sites still leave about one landing in four beyond 50 m, on the armor that failed, so the recipe must be revised and retested before the 2039 landings, and the crew pad is sintered out to the full 100 m, about 1,400 MWh, which six rovers finish in about 15 months ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). In the other direction, beacon-guided landings accurate to about 20 m (3σ) would shrink each prepared site about 25-fold in area and free most of the rover fleet.

The rock supply is the second hinge. If the survey's sieve count on the chosen field finds less than about 6 kg of armor-size stone per m², Wave 2's sites depend on the crusher station this book's working assumption puts on Wave 1, fed with stones it takes whole, and on a field rich enough for that; on a poorer field they depend on breaking boulders at a pace no autonomous breaker has yet published, or on a second field, and at that sieve count Wave 3's sites depend on such breaking too, unless Wave 2 brings the larger crusher, which cuts it to a third or less. A HiRISE campaign that finds no field of the needed size within reach of flat landing ground would move the working point toward one, while ejecta as rich in pebbles and cobbles as Lonar's would leave the station little to do.

## Autonomy

### What A3 must do

Construction at A3 ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md)) is a repeatable job shop. The Orchestrator, holding the site plan and the power ledger, breaks each landing site into grading, compaction, sintering passes, rock placement, and inspection tasks, assigns them to robots, and moves the heaviest loads to hours when the oxygen line or the wells need less power. It inspects each sintered pass with ground-penetrating radar, thermal imaging, and load tests, and resinters patches that fail. Earth engineers approve the site plan and recipes before each conjunction; the fleet executes without them. No robot team has yet coordinated its work on another world; chapter 1 tracks Cooperative Autonomous Distributed Robotic Exploration, the NASA multi-robot mission whose lunar flight now targets early 2027 [40], [41].

> Assumption: Construction robots operate at A3 by Wave 1 departure in June 2035. Justification: this is a program target that no robot has yet demonstrated; chapter 1 sets the autonomy roadmap.

### What A4 adds

A4 answers novel problems with a new recipe or layout without waiting for Earth: a buried ice lens under a landing site, a boulder field, or a sulfate-rich layer that foams when heated. It can tune sintering from the colony's own measurements when Martian soil does not behave like the simulant, and redesign the landing field after studying the first real plume erosion from Wave 1, which could cut the rock work per site.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could run the materials program this chapter leaves open, from sulfur extraction to new structural materials, and qualify them faster than an Earth review cycle allows. The largest change would be judgment: deciding, and answering for the decision, when a new material is safe for a structure people will use.

## Risks

- No vehicle of Starship's size has landed on Mars, and if the armor fails, plume damage could hit the lander or the site; where the plume reaches the ice, debris may carry about 2 km, so Wave 1's ships, landing on bare ground, touch down at least 2 km apart. Distance, berms, and data from the first landings are the mitigations ([R14](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r14)).
- Rock armor needs about 76 team-months and some 61,000 t of rock before Wave 2; lost rovers, or no rock field within about 1 km, would leave the last site graded but unarmored, for the least sensitive cargo. No rock field lies near the working point, so the landing field must be sited beside a crater's boulder field, the nearest known being 37 km away, and the loose stone on it may be under a tenth of the model's estimate, leaving much of the rock to the crusher station this book's working assumption puts on Wave 1 and, for Wave 3's sites or on a field poorer than planned, to breaking boulders at a pace no autonomous breaker has yet published, which a second breaker and a larger crusher on Wave 2 cut to a third or less for Wave 3 ([R35](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r35)).
- A reactor outage of several months in 2036–2038 would leave one Wave 2 site without a core, and a longer one two ([R10](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r10), [R27](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r27)).
- Martian soil may not sinter like the simulants; the requalification campaign and an on-site test program reduce this risk without removing it ([R34](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r34)).
- Abrasive dust wears excavator drums, seals, and bearings ([chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md), [R35](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r35)).
- Sulfur concrete, geopolymers, and local metals may never pass pilot scale; they are off the critical path by design ([R34](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r34)).

The first three decide whether Wave 2 has safe ground to land on.

## Open questions

- How thick must a core be, and does rock armor stop deep cratering at Mars pressure, over dry and ice-cemented soil, as the G2 tests must show before any ship lands on it? How far does debris fly when an unprepared landing bares the ice, as Wave 1's landings will show? Does the crater stop at the ice, as Phoenix's did, and does any stone of a few centimeters land beyond 2 km, which would make 125–140 m/s the planning case?
- Which crater's boulder field will the landing field sit beside, and does its ejecta hold as many 1.5–15 cm stones as the rock model predicts from its boulders? Twelve armored circles need about 175,000 t at the lunar study's layer, about three fields like the one 37 km from the working point by the model, or two to seven if every stone must be crushed, while the smooth landing plains hold only about 1–3 kg of armor-size stone per square meter. This book's working assumption puts a crusher station of about 16–19 t on Wave 1 before the sieve count is known; should it fly, and how fast can a robot break basalt boulders without an operator? If the stone is scarce, will the station's first year on Mars show breaking of at least about 5 t an hour, or must Wave 2 bring the larger crusher for Wave 3's sites?
- How deep is the icy ground along the landing field's berm lines and cable and bladder trenches, and is there at least about 0.3 m of loose soil above it for the borrow ditches? SWIM's thermal maps put it about 0.3–0.5 m down at the working point and shallower a few kilometers away; the scouts' radar maps it line by line, and its strength at Mars temperatures decides what a crossing would cost. How much does the depth vary along one line, and does a 0.1 m margin under the radar's reading keep the drums off the ice, as the first berm lines and test pits of 2036 will show?
- Is one full-scale landing on the crew-pad recipe in 2042, plus about two touchdowns on sintered cores, enough to qualify the crew pad ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md))?
- How much do sintering energy and strength change between simulant in Earth's air and real Martian soil under Mars-pressure CO₂?
- Can elemental sulfur be extracted from Martian sulfates at a useful energy cost?
