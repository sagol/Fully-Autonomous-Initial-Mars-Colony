# 7. Habitat

*Last reviewed: September 26, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, July 2043.* The torus has held 70 kPa of crew air for two and a quarter years. Inside, nothing moves except air and a slow drift of dust in a shaft of pale light from the slots near the floor. Around it, 3 m thick over the sleeping quarters, lie 650 t of ice and water that the wells pulled from the ground one cubic meter at a time. The Orchestrator reads 214 strain gauges, 72 gas-cell thermometers, and a leak-rate trend that has been flat since the proof test. Cell 17 froze cloudy in 2039 and was isolated; the robots left it frozen, recorded it, and moved on. The crew gate asked for 12 months of flat lines; the habitat has given it 27.

The first crew will spend most of their surface stay in one building, and that building has to exist, hold pressure, and cut the cosmic-ray dose before anyone launches. Three other chapters feed it: power for its heaters ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)), water for its shield and buffer gas for its atmosphere ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)), and robot hours for its assembly ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)).

This chapter adopts the Mars Ice Home, a NASA Langley Research Center concept from 2016–2017, as the reference habitat. It is an inflatable torus that robots deploy, pressurize, and then surround with frozen water pumped from the colony's wells. The inflatable carries the pressure; the ice is shielding and stored water.

In brief: one habitat of the Ice Home class lands with Wave 2 in October 2038. Its shield holds about 650 t of water, from a bank of about 530–590 t that the wells fill before the habitat lands and from their output during the fill, with more than 3 m over the crew quarters. That roughly halves the modeled cosmic-ray dose, and the inner core doubles as the storm shelter. Keeping the water liquid and the ice frozen takes about 5 kW. The habitat must then hold its operating atmosphere for at least 12 months before the crew launch decision in August 2043.

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) sets the numbers this system must meet:

- Crew of four, landing September 2044.
- Habitat shells and membranes land with Wave 2 (October 2038) within a ~40 t planning allocation; outfitting lands with Wave 3 (September 2040).
- Shield water: ~650 t for one unit, needed by 2040; about 530–590 t is banked by October 2038, and four wells supply the rest during the fill.
- Habitat thermal control and pressure hold: a critical load of 5–10 kW in 2038–2040 and 10–15 kW from 2040.
- Crew gate criterion 2: the habitat held at its operating atmosphere (70 kPa, 26.5% O₂) for at least 12 months, with leak rate within specification, at the August 2043 launch decision.
- All of it achievable at autonomy level A3 ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md)).

Criterion 2 paces the chapter: everything else must be done in time to leave a full year of pressure-hold record before the launch decision.

## Why water, and why an inflatable

Galactic cosmic rays (GCR) are the steady radiation load on the Mars surface: Curiosity's Radiation Assessment Detector (RAD) measured 0.64 mSv/day at Gale crater ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)) [7]. Solar particle events add rare, intense bursts, which the habitat's storm shelter handles. Over a stay of more than a year, the habitat must cut the steady background.

A NASA Langley study summarized in the Ice Home's concept of operations (ConOps) found that aluminum stops improving beyond about 20 g/cm², while hydrogen-rich polyethylene, a stand-in for water, keeps reducing dose because hydrogen produces fewer damaging secondary particles [6]. Water is the hydrogen-rich material the colony already produces. Shipping 650 t of polyethylene from Earth would fill six or more reference landers; producing 650 t of water costs well time and heat that the plan already provides (chapter 2).

The Ice Home team wanted a structure simple enough for basic robotic systems to predeploy, with a large pressurized workspace and daylight inside [6]. An inflatable suits that aim, because a membrane folds to a fraction of its deployed volume. The ConOps expected the stowed system to be a cylinder about 6 m in diameter and 9 m tall, a packing ratio near 2:1, with a total system mass goal under 18 t [6]. The deployed habitat has about 198 m² of floor on two levels [6] and, by this chapter's estimate, about 530 m³ of pressurized volume.

## The Ice Home, not the printed Ice House

Two concepts carry the word "ice," and they are easy to confuse. The **Mars Ice House** came first. The Space Exploration Architecture (SEArch+) and Clouds Architecture Office team created it for the 2015 design phase of NASA's 3D-Printed Habitat Challenge, where it took first place and a $25,000 prize [1]. Robots would 3D-print nested ice domes, about 0.6 m thick at the base, inside a transparent membrane of ETFE (ethylene tetrafluoroethylene) reinforced with Dyneema strands. The interior would hold 70 kPa, and an aerogel layer would insulate it [2].

The **Mars Ice Home** followed: a 2016 feasibility study at NASA Langley with the same team [4], then a 2017 risk-reduction study of water-cell films and low-pressure freezing [5]. Its ConOps, MIH.ConOps.001 of December 21, 2017, supplies most numbers in this chapter [6]. The Ice Home prints nothing; its built-in cells fill with water and freeze in place.

This book uses the Ice Home: its water can later be drawn down for propellant or life support [6], and its study went further into deployment, freezing, and shielding. No prototype has been built. The risk-reduction work consisted of film tests and small freezing tests [5], which leaves the integrated habitat at technology readiness level (TRL) 2–3.

## A 12 m torus with a six-layer wall

The Ice Home is a torus 12 m across, built from a tube about 6 m in diameter. Inside, two floors share about 6 m of height; with the ice in place the structure stands about 10 m tall [6]. Upper and lower hubs at the center restrain the torus hoop loads and hold the pumps, blowers, manifolds, and avionics [6]. The wall, from the inside out:

| Layer | Material | Size | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Interior abrasion layer | Removable liner | | Protects the bladder; removable so crews can reach the bladder for repair |
| 2. Pressure bladder | Gas-tight membrane | Encloses the habitable volume | Holds the cabin atmosphere; the only airtight layer |
| 3. Inner gas cells | Filtered Mars atmosphere (CO₂) | ~13.2 cm; 72 individually controlled cells | Insulation; sets freeze rates |
| 4. Water cells | Clear polymer film: Tedlar led after testing, ahead of Teflon FEP (fluorinated ethylene propylene); ETFE and Mylar also considered | 36 vertical slices; 2 m of ice plus 1 m of liquid over the crew quarters, thinner in the walls | Shield and stored water; cells inside the insulation stay liquid, cells outside it freeze |
| 5. Outer gas layer | Mars atmosphere | Filled or emptied as needed | Controls freezing and, later, melting |
| 6. Restraint layer | High-grade fiberglass webbing | | Carries the pressure load; chosen for low creep, a wide temperature range, chemical and ultraviolet (UV) resistance, and light transmission |

Source: ConOps [6].

Fully filled, the shield uses about 655 m³ of water [6], roughly 650 t; freezing expands it but does not change its mass. That is the ~650 t in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md).

> Assumption: The colony's habitat is one unit of the Ice Home class, with the ConOps geometry and layer stack. The ~40 t Wave 2 allocation covers the unit (goal under 18 t), two airlocks, a connecting node, ground cover, fill and drain hardware, and spare water cells. One unit gives four people about 50 m² of floor and about 130 m³ of volume each, five times the 25 m³ per crew member the study used as its minimum [6].

## Operated at 70 kPa, designed for 101 kPa

The Ice Home study considered 8, 10.2, and 14.7 psi (55, 70, and 101 kPa). It called 70 kPa a good compromise, then used 101 kPa as the structural design load for margin [6]. [Chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md) chooses 70 kPa with 26.5% O₂ on flammability grounds. NASA flew this mix as the staged atmosphere on 34 Shuttle missions, about a week each flight, and its exploration atmosphere work rates it physiologically equivalent to the 56.5 kPa, 34% O₂ atmosphere it recommends for vehicles with frequent extravehicular activity [8].

> Assumption: Reference operating atmosphere 70 kPa with 26.5% O₂ (chapter 6), with the structure designed and proof-tested against 101 kPa. The fallback, 56.5 kPa at 34% O₂, would shorten prebreathe for suited work ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md)) at a cost in fire-safety margin; this chapter's loads cover both.

At 70 kPa and 22 °C, the torus's 530 m³ of gas weighs about 500 kg: roughly 130 kg of oxygen and 370 kg of nitrogen and argon. The 2 t of buffer gas that [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) requires in storage at the crew gate, after the first fill and its leakage have been drawn from about 3 t captured, would cover about five more fills of the inert share.

## The restraint layer carries the pressure

The ConOps is explicit that the ice "is not critical for structural support unless there is a catastrophic loss of internal pressure," and that "under Mars gravity the internal pressure will support tens of meters of water or ice" [6]. The 2016 Ice House paper makes the same point about its own membrane, which carries the overall load while the ice mainly supports its own weight [2].

A thin-walled tube of radius r under pressure p carries a hoop tension of roughly p × r per meter of wall. With r = 3 m:

| Cabin pressure | Hoop tension, estimate | Ice needed to balance it | Share offset by the 3 m overhead shield (~10 kPa) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 56.5 kPa | ~170 kN/m | ~17 m | ~19% |
| 70 kPa (reference) | ~210 kN/m | ~21 m | ~15% |
| 101 kPa (design load) | ~300 kN/m | ~30 m | ~10% |

The overhead shield (2 m of ice at 917 kg/m³ and 1 m of water, under Mars gravity of 3.71 m/s²) presses down with about 10 kPa against 70 kPa pushing outward. At the reference pressure, each meter of restraint webbing holds a tension that on Earth would hang about 21 t, the load class of NASA's TransHab and later softgoods test articles, which use woven restraint layers of Kevlar or Vectran to carry it [9]. The ConOps adds that hoop stress rises linearly with torus radius, so doubling the radius needs a restraint layer twice as strong [6].

The ConOps holds the water cells at a much smaller 3 psi (20 kPa) of gauge pressure while they fill, enough to keep their shape; below 0.5 psi (3.4 kPa) they go slack [6]. Airlock cycles and temperature swings make the habitable volume breathe, and the inner gas cells let it move without cracking the ice around it [6].

## The shield roughly halves the cosmic-ray dose
<a id="radiation-shielding"></a>

The Ice Home team ran NASA's TARIS 4.0 radiation code on the design [6]. The inputs lean conservative: the Badhwar-O'Neill 2014 GCR model at the 1977 solar minimum, when cosmic rays peak, and a site at 0 km elevation. The reference site lies 3.5 km lower, under more air. The result is whole-body effective dose on a female voxel phantom, a digital model of the body's organs [6].

| Shielding case | Effective dose (mSv/yr) | Change |
|---|---:|---:|
| No shield (Mars atmosphere only) | 178 | baseline |
| 1 m water sphere | 107 | −40% |
| 2 m water sphere | 75 | −58% |
| 3 m water sphere | 50 | −72% (high uncertainty) |
| First Ice Home design, crew area / lower level near hatch | 118 / 130 | −33% / −27% |
| Updated design, >3 m overhead, crew area / near hatch | 89 / 93 | −50% / −48% |

Source: ConOps TARIS assessment [6].

The 178 mSv/yr is a modeled effective dose at solar minimum and 0 km; Curiosity's 0.64 mSv/day is a measured dose equivalent at Gale crater [7]. The two cannot be subtracted, so this book carries forward only the percentage reduction.

> Assumption: For dose planning, this book credits the habitat with the 2 m water-sphere case, −58%, for the 90% of surface time the crew spends inside, as [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md)'s dose cases do. The ConOps design cases give a little less: about −50% in the crew quarters and −25% to −50% elsewhere inside. At −50% the surface dose would rise by about 19 mSv over the 410-day stay (this chapter's calculation), about 2% of a mission total that the transit legs dominate (chapter 12).

NASA's crew health standard caps a career at 600 mSv [10], [14]. The transit legs alone exceed that limit (chapter 12), so the crew flies above it under individual waivers whatever the habitat does. The ConOps team asked the habitat to keep the surface share as low as reasonably achievable with the water the colony can collect [6].

The table shows gains out to 3 m, though the 3 m result is uncertain. The ConOps geometry and the water and freezing time available before 2040 set the thickness; a thicker shield would need a larger torus, a stronger restraint layer, and more months of freezing. A regolith berm from [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md) covers the lower walls, where the ConOps design is thinnest.

Solar particle events deliver a large dose within hours or days, and NASA's standard sets a design limit of 250 mSv for one [14]. Chapter 12 makes the habitat's inner core, under the thickest overhead water, the storm shelter; its 2–3 m of water exceeds NASA's recommended shelter mass by a factor of 20 or more. This chapter's design criterion is that a crew member sheltering there through the design event stays below 250 mSv. The colony builds no separate buried shelter; the shelter check belongs to crew gate criterion 7.

## About 5 kW keeps the water liquid and the ice frozen

A layer of CO₂ gas cells 13.2 cm thick needs about 5 kW of internal heat to keep the inner water cells liquid and the outer ice cells frozen, according to the ConOps's preliminary thermal analysis [6]. That figure is the basis for [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s habitat allocation; the rest of the 5–10 kW (2038–2040) and 10–15 kW (2040 onward) covers blowers, pumps, gas makeup, sensors, and warming the interior toward the ConOps design temperature of 22.2 °C [6].

The gas cells also control ice quality. A slow freeze gives clear ice with larger grains, better strength, and low creep; the risk-reduction tests at Brown University showed that clear ice can form in bags frozen at pressures down to 1 psi (6.9 kPa) [6]. The robots set freeze rates by adjusting air temperatures in individual inner and outer gas cells from a central manifold [6]. The same system can run in reverse: by thinning the inner insulation and thickening the outer, operators can melt ice for filtering or to draw water for propellant [6].

Before the crew arrives, the largest heat use is keeping the liquid cells from freezing; once people live inside, the ConOps expects crew and equipment waste heat to cover most heating [6].

> Assumption: The habitat takes most of its heat from a reactor waste-heat loop ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)), with electric heaters sized to carry the full ~5 kW alone. Heater power paces the start of filling: reactor 2 lands in October 2038 but, like reactor 1, needs a few months to reach full power (chapter 5). Until then the habitat inflates and warms on reactor 1 and batteries, and the first water cell fills only once firm power exists, about January 2039.

Two thermal hazards remain. Condensation will form where cold water cells face a warm cabin, and the ConOps suggested more insulation there [6]. The other is solar melting: even in summer, temperatures rise above freezing for no more than a few hours a day, and a reflective outer coating can limit solar heating on the worst days [6].

## Daylight through the wall, and the dust that dims it

The Ice Home team made daylight a design requirement, challenging designers to pass more than 10% of visible light through the full wall, and chose a fiberglass restraint and a transparent gas insulation partly for that reason [6]. The vertical-slice design lets light in through adjustable slots near the bottom, trading some shielding for daylight; fibers or regolith slurry in the ice would cost clarity [6]. Mars daylight is 43% of Earth's at the top of the atmosphere ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)). Ten percent of it marks day and night and shows the sky, but it is far too little for crops, which [chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md) grows under light-emitting diodes. Interior lighting should follow the solid-state lamps with adjustable spectrum and intensity that NASA tested on the International Space Station (ISS) for visual performance and circadian regulation [12].

Outside, dust dims that light and wears the webbing. The ConOps lists wind-blown abrasion, radiation, perchlorates, and atomic oxygen among the external threats to the restraint layer, alongside internal abrasion from crew activity and moving ice [6]. Dust also comes in on machines: if robots are maintained inside the habitat, their bays must be partitioned from living areas, with positive pressure and filtering [6].

> Assumption (proposal): A small wall-climbing or tethered robot cleans the upper restraint layer after dust events, and interior light sensors measure transmission. No source demonstrates this. If cleaning harms the webbing, the colony accepts lower light and relies on interior lighting.

## Every water cell is a contained volume

The habitat is a large, warm, wet structure landed on Mars, and it will carry Earth microbes. The ConOps planned for this in two ways. Membrane materials that cannot be wiped clean would receive dry heat microbial reduction (DHMR), typically around 125 °C, or antimicrobial coatings where heat would damage them [6]. The ConOps also expected algae and microbes to grow in the water cells over time and planned for cells to be drained, filtered, and refilled [6].

The 2026 planetary protection policy of the Committee on Space Research (COSPAR) asks human missions to monitor the microbes they carry [13], and [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md) applies it to a crewed base. A habitat built of water cells needs four design rules to meet it:

- Membrane and water-cell films are qualified for DHMR or an equivalent treatment before launch.
- Fill water is filtered and disinfected before it enters any cell.
- Every water cell is a monitored containment volume; a cell leak is logged as a potential forward-contamination release as well as a structural event.
- Drained cell water returns to the colony's treatment loop and never to the ground.

> Assumption: These rules satisfy the planetary protection plan for the first crewed mission, which is negotiated with the authorizing agency and COSPAR ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md), [chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md), and chapter 12).

## Sublimation and why ice goes in last

Mean surface pressure is about 610 Pa at the Mars datum, barely below water's triple point at 611.657 Pa; at the reference site, 3.5 km lower, it is nearer 850 Pa ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)). Dryness destroys exposed ice there: the air holds only about 210 parts per million of water vapor [15], a partial pressure of roughly 0.1–0.2 Pa, far below the vapor pressure of ice at daytime temperatures, so exposed ice sublimates at any elevation [16]. Phoenix watched centimeter-scale chunks of ice in a freshly dug trench vanish within about four sols in June 2008 [11].

Both ice concepts therefore keep all their ice inside a sealed, pressurized envelope; the Ice House paper requires a pressurized interior before any ice is printed [2]. In the Ice Home, the water cells sit inside the restraint layer at about 20 kPa of gauge pressure [6]. The CO₂ layer insulates but does not seal, so a leaking water cell loses ice to the atmosphere whatever the gas layer does.

That fixes the deployment order. Ice poured first onto an unpressurized structure would sublimate from any exposed face, and nothing could slip a gas layer or a bladder underneath it afterward. The sequence runs:

1. Place the stowed package in the habitation zone, lay ground cover, and inspect it visually [6].
2. Connect a power cable and a data link; heaters bring the package to working temperature [6].
3. Inflate slowly with integrated pumps using filtered Mars atmosphere, watching pressure, temperature, strain, and cameras [6].
4. Bring the habitable volume to operating pressure and hold it while checking leak rate.
5. Fill the CO₂ insulation cells and establish the inner and outer temperature zones [6].
6. Accumulate water in heated holding cells in the hub, then fill and freeze water cells in sequence, overhead cells first [6].
7. Proof-test the pressure envelope above operating pressure, measure permeability, and test relief valves [6].
8. Begin outfitting; the ConOps expects deployment and outfitting to overlap because filling takes years [6].

Step 6 takes most of the calendar.

## Freezing sets the fill rate
<a id="filling-the-shield"></a>

The ConOps assumed water would trickle in from its supply: at its planning rate of 0.5 m³/day, 655 m³ takes 1,310 days, and it recommended an even lower 0.25 m³/day [6]. It concluded that an Ice Home must be predeployed at least two launch opportunities ahead of its crew [6]; this plan deploys it three ahead (2037 departure for a 2043 crew).

This plan has most of the water before it needs it, and the wells supply the rest as the fill goes. At the 85% availability this book plans on, [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s two wells bank about 530–590 t by October 2038, before the habitat lands; the lower figure applies without the pathfinder, which starts the wells about three months later. That is less than the shield needs, but four wells then make about 1.5 m³/day while running, about 1.3 m³/day on average, a little more than the fill takes, so the bank only buffers the fill. If the wells delivered only the 80% of their modeled output that gate G2 accepts, the bank would still hold about 400–450 t when the shield is full (this chapter's calculation).

> Assumption: The bank is held in collapsible, insulated bladders, about 700 m³ in all, laid in a covered trench beside the well field, with fill and transfer pumps and a heated transfer line along the utility trench to the habitat, about 1 km away ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). This book allocates about 3–5 t for the bladders and pumps from Wave 1's unitemized mass; the figure is this book's estimate for coated-fabric bladders, because no flight bladder of this size exists.

Water in the bladders may freeze, so reactor waste heat melts it before transfer: about 65–72 MWh for the whole bank, or about 5 kWt averaged over the fill (this chapter's calculation). That heat comes from the loop that feeds the wells, not from the habitat's ~5 kW heater budget, and [chapter 5](05.%20Power.md#waste-heat-melts-the-wells) counts it there: wells, bank, and line losses together take about a fifth to a quarter of one reactor's rejected heat.

The heat of fusion has to leave through the outer gas layer, and a slow freeze makes the clear, strong, low-creep ice the design needs. The schedule below fills the shield between January 2039 and mid-2040, about 18 months, which needs an average of about 1.2 m³/day. Overhead cells go first, as the ConOps advises when water is short, because they buy most of the dose reduction early [6]. No test has frozen clear ice at 1.2 m³/day in cells of this size; the 2017 tests used small bags [5], [6]. At the ConOps rate of 0.5 m³/day the shield would be full only in August 2042. That is two years before the crew lands but too late for two gate checks: gate G4 in July 2041 wants a filled shell and would find it about 70% full, and crew gate criterion 7 needs a Mars year of dosimetry under the full shield before the August 2043 decision, which would end only in mid-2044. That case moves the crew one window, to January 2046 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). The crew date holds only if the shell is full by about the end of September 2041, an average of about 0.66 m³/day.

If water itself falls far short, the fallback is a partial fill (overhead cells only), a thicker berm, and a shorter first surface stay.

If the wells work, the opposite problem comes first. By the time the shield is full in mid-2040 the bladders are full too, and together they hold about 1,370 t; from then the wells throttle to the little the propellant plant draws. Ladder rung 3 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#the-sustainability-ladder)) asks them to run for 12 more months, so that water needs somewhere to go.

> Assumption: Wave 3 brings about 550 m³ more of the same bladders, about 2–4 t from its unitemized mass (this book's estimate, scaled from the first set), laid in a second covered trench beside the first within weeks of landing. They hold 12 months of four wells at their full modeled rate, less the propellant feed, and the water in them becomes a reserve for the crew era, well above the ~150 t of reserve and start-up water chapter 2 requires.

The habitat can be pressurized with filtered Mars air during filling, as the ConOps planned for inflation [6]. The pressure-hold record that counts for the crew gate should be run in the gas the crew will breathe, because leak rates depend on the gas and the mix must be verified in any case.

A freeze at the ConOps rate would stretch the fill to August 2042. If the proof test then had to wait for a full shield, the 12-month record would run from August 2042 to August 2043 and end just before the August 2043 launch decision, leaving almost no margin on the record itself. The record could still pass, but criterion 7's dosimetry could not, and that is what moves the crew to January 2046 ([R15](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r15)).

> Assumption: Leak-rate specification of no more than 1 kg of atmosphere per sol (about 0.2% of the habitat's gas mass). At that rate the colony replaces about 360 kg per Earth year from its buffer-gas and oxygen stores. [Chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md) uses the same figure.

The ConOps assumed ground controllers would command fill sequences and verify each step [6]; at light-time delays of 3–22 minutes each way ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)), the colony runs that loop itself and reports.

## Burial and printed shells remain alternatives

The long-standing alternative is to bury an inflatable or rigid module under enough soil. The Ice Home team's objection was the robotic effort of moving meters of regolith, especially at rocky sites [6]. Hydrogen-poor materials shield GCR less well than water at depth [6], and dry regolith is one of them, though ice-bearing ground at a site like Arcadia Planitia holds more hydrogen, and [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md) counts on regolith to moderate neutrons. Chapter 12 also gives the depths: about 1–1.6 m of cover for less than 100 mSv/yr, with a dose peak at 30–40 cm, so thin cover can raise the dose. Burial takes no well water and no heater power.

Printed regolith shells are the approach the 3D-Printed Habitat Challenge rewarded. AI SpaceFactory's MARSHA won its final phase in 2019 with a prototype printed from basalt fiber and a plant-based biopolymer [3]. That binder would come from Earth or from a chemical plant the colony does not have. [Chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md) covers sintered and printed regolith for pads, berms, and covers.

Buried or trenched modules and natural cavities trade construction effort for shielding. They suit storage better than a first home with daylight, so in this plan burial supplies only the berm around the Ice Home's lower walls.

## What would change this conclusion

The Ice Home loses its place as the first home in three cases. A bank far below the planned ~530–590 t by 2038, together with well output far below the fill's ~1.2 m³/day afterward, would leave the shield incomplete when the crew launches. Large-scale film or webbing tests might fail. Or the robots of [chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md) might demonstrate a much lower-cost regolith shelter that replaces or supplements the Ice Home for the first crew.

## Autonomy

### What A3 must do

At A3 ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md)), the fleet executes the eight-step deployment sequence, schedules fills against the water bank and thermal margins, controls freeze rates in 72 gas cells, runs the proof test, and keeps the pressure-hold record. Anticipated faults include a stuck valve, a slow leak located by pressure decay, a heater string failure, a cloudy or cracked cell, and a dust storm that forces load shedding. A failed water cell gets the ConOps response, isolation and later repair [6]; the design tolerates one or more failed cells [6].

The Orchestrator coordinates this work, but the pressure-relief valves, heater thermostats, and gas-cell overpressure protection run on independent safety controllers.

### What A4 adds

The ConOps defers repair of failed water cells until human crews arrive [6]. An A4 system could invent repairs the designers did not script, such as patching a film through an abrasion layer or resequencing fills after an unexpected crack pattern, and move water toward where the crew will spend time. Such repairs need ground testing before they are trusted.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could redesign the habitat class itself: a second unit with a larger radius and a locally made restraint layer, ice reinforced where analysis shows it pays, and water placement tuned to the site's measured radiation field.

Robotic deployment and fill is the least mature step (TRL 1–2): no Ice Home has been built, and no robot has inflated, filled, or frozen one at any scale.

## Risks

[Appendix A](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md) carries the full register. The habitat's own risks:

- Wells that underperform before 2038 leave a smaller bank; four wells make up the difference later, and the partial-fill fallback still shields the crew quarters ([R05](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r05), [R36](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r36)).
- A leaking water cell loses ice to sublimation and opens a contamination path; a bladder leak threatens crew gate criterion 2. Pressure decay, strain gauges, and cameras detect both, but no one has shown repair beyond isolation without people ([R15](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r15)).
- The restraint webbing must hold ~210 kN/m for years at low temperature under UV and dust, and no long-duration creep data at Mars conditions exist for this layup ([R15](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r15)).
- A multi-sol loss of heat would freeze the inner water cells and could stress the structure; the colony demonstrates recovery from a heater loss before the pressure-hold clock starts ([R32](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r32)).
- Clear ice may freeze slower than the ~1.2 m³/day the schedule needs. Below about 0.7 m³/day the shell is not full at gate G4, and below about 0.66 m³/day it is full too late for criterion 7's Mars year of dosimetry; at the ConOps rate of 0.5 m³/day the crew moves to January 2046 ([R15](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r15)).
- The TARIS results beyond 2 m of water carry high uncertainty [6], so the dose reduction may be smaller than modeled ([R01](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r01), [R19](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r19)).
- Microbial growth in water cells, followed by a leak to the surface, could conflict with the colony's planetary protection plan ([R09](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r09)).

Most of these risks surface during the fill and the pressure-hold record, years before the crew gate, which is why the habitat lands three windows ahead of its crew.

## Open questions

- Would the colony ever raise the cabin to sea-level pressure? A move to 101 kPa raises restraint tension by about 45% over the 70 kPa reference, within the ConOps design load but with less margin for creep.
- Can a restraint layer and bladder be qualified for 12 or more years of creep, UV, dust, and thermal cycling at Mars conditions?
- How fast can cells of this size freeze clear ice, and does a faster freeze cost strength or creep resistance?
- How clear can a 3 m shield be, and does 10% transmission survive dust?
- Can robots repair a water cell or a bladder, or must the habitat carry enough spare cells to tolerate failures until humans arrive?
