# 3. Site selection

*Last reviewed: September 27, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, 2034.* The pathfinder has been down for five months. Its survey rover has crossed the landing ellipse 11 times, dragging a ground-penetrating radar behind it, and the drill has made nine holes. Hole 6 hit clean ice at 70 cm and stayed in ice to the bottom of the string at 8 m. Hole 8 found only cemented soil to 5 m. The Orchestrator has folded all of it into a single map, colored by one question: where can a heated well run for five years without its cavity collapsing or its casing striking rock? Two patches, each about 1 km across, stay green through every model run. The Wave 1 landing points move 3 km east to sit between them.

Every plan in this book depends on one place. A colony built by robots before any person arrives has to live off the ground it lands on, and on Mars that means water. Water becomes the habitat's ice shell, the ascent vehicle's methane, the crew's drinking supply, and the greenhouse's start-up inventory. [Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) budgets about 830 t of it by 2043, and nearly all of that has to come from buried ice pumped up through heated Rodwell wells. A site without reachable ice would turn the water ledger from a surplus into a deficit.

The site must also let 100 t-class landers come down safely, give solar panels enough light for deferrable loads, stay clear of places that planetary protection rules put off-limits, and offer good science. These goals conflict. Ice lies closer to the surface the farther north a site lies, while sunlight, warmth, and landing performance all favor the equator.

In brief: the working reference site is the northern plains of Arcadia Planitia, in a band at about 40–44°N and 185–200°E, at an elevation of −3 to −4 km. Of all the regions inside NASA's landing limits, it has the most independent lines of evidence for thick, shallow, relatively clean ice. The working point, 42°N, lies north of the 40° limit in SpaceX's 2020 site studies in exchange for surer ice, and the vehicle must still accept it. Drills from the optional 2033 pathfinder would confirm the ice early enough to retarget Wave 1; without a pathfinder, Wave 1's own first months must do it, before Wave 2 commits.

## Requirements

From [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) the site takes five numbers:

- about 830 t of water by 2043, ~650 t of it for the habitat shield ([water budget](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#water-budget))
- Rodwells at a modeled ~0.38 m³/day and ~10 kWt each, heated by reactor waste heat
- 13–14 landings of up to ~100 t by the crew's arrival (11 cargo landers, one or two support landers, and the crew ship), 14–15 with the January 2046 food flight, plus an optional pathfinder
- a solar field of 25–64 kW sol-average for deferrable loads
- survival of the 120-day design storm at optical depth τ≈5

Chapter 2 also fixes the layout: a landing field at least 2 km from the habitat, reactor field, and wells, with pads about 1 km apart inside it and berms between them ([reference site](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#reference-site)).

This chapter adds its own. Ice must lie within reach of a drill and a cased Rodwell. Landing zones must be flat, low, and nearly rock-free. The work zone must not overlap a natural Special Region (defined below). The ice must be verified on the ground before Wave 2 commits the habitat and propellant plant, and a fallback must exist inside the same exploration zone.

> Assumption: "Within reach" means the top of the ice body lies less than 5 m below the surface across most of the planned well field, and the ice below it is at least 10 m thick. A 5 m casing depth matches the bottom of the 1–5 m band in NASA's ice-mapping project, the deepest band above its open-ended >5 m class [2]; 10 m of ice gives a Rodwell cavity room to grow for several years.

## The selection criteria and their trade-offs

### Accessible ice: shallow, clean, and thick

Three properties of the ice drive the choice. Depth sets how much overburden a drill must case through. Purity sets how much water each cubic meter of melt yields and how much sediment clogs the well. The best case is **excess ice**, ice that takes up more volume than the pore space of the soil around it, so its melt is mostly water. Thickness sets how long one well can run before its cavity reaches the base of the ice.

A 2016 NASA assessment of water extraction found that an open-pit ice mine moves less material than a gypsum mine only while the overburden stays under 2.2 m, and less than the regolith cases only under 5.2–6.4 m [30]. Rodwells change that arithmetic because they need only a narrow cased hole through the overburden. They still need a mechanical drill to penetrate the soil, and enough clean ice below the casing to keep the melt cavity from collapsing [30].

### Latitude: ice favors the north, sunlight the south

Most of the trade-offs run along latitude. Buried ice is stable near the surface closer to the poles, and the most recent probabilistic synthesis finds near-surface ice "likely present poleward of ∼45°" in both hemispheres, with the data unable to settle the question closer to the equator [5]. Impact craters agree. New craters imaged by the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO) have exposed clean ice at depths of less than 1 m, with such exposures common between 39° and 50°N [8]. The lowest-latitude example, a 150 m crater near 35°N, showed an ice deposit already vanishing [9].

Sunlight falls the other way. This book calculated the daily-mean values below, at the top of the atmosphere, from Mars's orbit:

| Latitude | Northern winter solstice | Annual mean |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0° | 204 W/m² | 179 W/m² |
| 18°N | 148 W/m² | 170 W/m² |
| 40°N | 72 W/m² | 142 W/m² |
| 45°N | 54 W/m² | 133 W/m² |
| 50°N | 37 W/m² | 123 W/m² |

> Assumption: The values above use an obliquity of 25.19°, an eccentricity of 0.0934, and perihelion at a solar longitude of 251°. They are top-of-atmosphere figures before dust extinction and are meant to compare sites, not to size arrays.

At 40°N the annual mean is about 80% of the equator's, but the winter solstice value falls to about a third. NASA's comparison of solar and fission power for Mars found that "solar power masses increase as landing sites are selected further from the equator" [31]. This plan accepts that penalty because [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) puts every critical load on fission from January 2039 and uses solar only for deferrable work.

Latitude also limits landing. NASA's 2015 call for human landing site proposals restricted candidates to within ±50° [19], and ascent vehicle mass grows sharply at very high latitudes [26]. The reference vehicle has a tighter limit of its own ([the latitude trade](#the-latitude-trade)).

### Elevation: low ground helps landing and shielding

A heavy lander needs as much atmosphere as possible above the site to slow down before its engines take over. The 2015 workshop limited proposals to sites below +2 km relative to the Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter datum [19], and the Subsurface Water Ice Mapping (SWIM) project cut its maps off at +1 km "due to entry, descent, and landing" [3]. A NASA sensitivity study found that descent-system mass can vary from −15% to +30% depending on the landing site [26]. North of 30°N, surface elevations span −4 to +4 km [26], so elevation still matters within the northern midlatitudes.

Low ground also shields better. The atmosphere's scale height is about 11.1 km [41], so a site at −3.5 km has 35–40% more air overhead than one at the datum.

> Assumption: The extra air trims the galactic cosmic ray dose. This book keeps the dose rate Curiosity measured at about −4.4 km in Gale crater (0.64 ± 0.12 mSv/day [37]) as the design value. The reference site sits about 0.9 km higher, under less air, so its true dose is slightly higher; [chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#the-waiver-needs-allowances-above-the-cases) carries about 4% more surface dose for it as an allowance in the dose budget (this book's rough estimate), until on-site dosimetry measures it.

### Terrain: Arcadia's plains already imaged for heavy landers

A lander of Starship's class needs a flat, firm, rock-poor patch, and a colony needs several of them spaced far enough apart that engine plumes do not damage what has already landed. The 2015 call asked for about 25 km² of generally level terrain with slopes under about 10° and no thick layer of fine dust [19]. It also asked that the construction area lie within 5 km of the landing site [19]. By May 2020 the High Resolution Imaging Science Experiment (HiRISE) camera on MRO had imaged 15 prospective Starship sites in Arcadia Planitia and northern Erebus Montes and one in Phlegra Montes; six more Phlegra sites were added in August 2020. The Arcadia and Erebus sites later downselected lie at 39.8–40.8°N [43]. One image, at 39.8°N, 192.1°E, resolves the surface at 31 cm per pixel [27], fine enough to count boulders of about 1 m. Those sites lie 1–2° south of the 42°N working point, which has not yet been imaged at boulder scale.

### Dust: moderate at Arcadia, planet-wide in a storm

Loose surface dust can hide small hazards and erodes under rocket plumes; airborne dust cuts solar output and coats radiators. Orbiters gauge surface dust by thermal inertia, a measure of how quickly the ground heats and cools; fine, loose material has low values. The Arcadia exploration zones proposed in 2015 have thermal inertias of about 60–130 J m⁻² K⁻¹ s⁻½, which the proposers called "moderately dusty" [21]. That is acceptable for landing pads that robots will build anyway ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)). The storm design case in chapter 2 is planet-wide, so no midlatitude site escapes it. Multiyear dust climatologies from orbit [38] show where regional storms recur; a site-specific dust comparison remains open.

### Communications: relay coverage no worse in the north

Latitude matters for communications mainly through relay geometry. MRO, still active in 2026 [39], and the other relay orbiters fly inclined or near-polar orbits whose ground tracks converge toward high latitudes. By that geometric argument, not a measured pass count, relay coverage at 40–44°N is no worse than at the equator. A commercial relay orbiter, funded under U.S. law for delivery by the end of 2028 [35], would add to that coverage; [chapter 10](10.%20Communications.md#the-mars-telecommunications-network) covers its contract status and the colony's own relays.

### Planetary protection: the wells will likely count as Special Regions
<a id="planetary-protection-and-special-regions"></a>

The Committee on Space Research (COSPAR) sets the international standard for protecting Mars from Earth life and Earth from anything brought back. Its current policy, the 2026 version approved by the COSPAR Bureau on November 7, 2025, defines a Special Region as "a region within which terrestrial organisms are likely to replicate," using a lower limit of water activity of 0.5 and a lower temperature limit of −28 °C [28]. Both limits sit below the reported limits of terrestrial biology, and they trace back to a 2014 review of the evidence by a joint panel of COSPAR and NASA's Mars Exploration Program Analysis Group [29]. The policy notes that "no Special Regions are currently identified on the basis of possible Martian life forms" [28]. Spacecraft that land in or reach a Special Region fall into a stricter category with a surface bioburden limit of 30 spores for the whole system or the parts that make contact [28].

Two parts of the policy bear directly on siting. First, "spacecraft-induced Special Regions are to be evaluated . . . on a case-by-case basis" [28]. A Rodwell makes liquid water well above −28 °C, so its melt pocket will likely count as one. Second, for crewed missions the policy states that "any uncharacterized Martian site should be evaluated by robotic precursors prior to crew access" [28]. The thesis of this book already requires that evaluation; the colony's robots are the precursors.

> Assumption: The colony keeps its wells as closed, heat-sterilized systems, logs their bioburden, and stays clear of candidate natural Special Regions such as recurring slope lineae and active gullies. The northern plains at 40–44°N have neither in the planned work zone. This reading of the COSPAR policy is the author's interpretation and will need agency review.

### Science value decides only close calls

Ice-rich midlatitude sites hold a climate record in their layered ice, and their buried ice interfaces are among the places astrobiologists most want to sample. Sites like Jezero crater hold older rocks: an ancient lake delta whose sediments "contain clays and carbonates" [33], which Perseverance has studied since February 2021. The colony's first job is to survive, and science value decides between sites only when the ice and landing criteria are close.

## Data sources: a blind spot at well depth
<a id="data-sources"></a>

No single instrument can see buried ice, and each sees a different depth, so the evidence for every candidate comes from several.

| Method | What it sees | What it misses | Key result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neutron spectroscopy (Mars Odyssey) | Hydrogen in the top meter or so | Detail finer than hundreds of kilometers | Equatorial hydrogen anomalies [32] |
| Thermal mapping | Depth of the ice table, from how fast the surface heats and cools (buried ice conducts heat better than dry soil) | Ice much below 30 cm, where sensitivity drops quickly [6] | NASA's 2019 "treasure map": "widespread water ice at latitudes as low as 35°N/45°S buried sometimes a few centimeters below sand-like material" [11], [12] |
| Shallow Radar (SHARAD, on MRO) | Layers tens to hundreds of meters down, and their dielectric constant, which tells ice from rock | The top few meters | Ice inside the Deuteronilus aprons [14]; a thick ice-rich layer under Utopia [17] |
| HiRISE imaging, ~30 cm per pixel | Ice landforms (terraced craters, polygons, scalloped ground) and fresh craters that expose ice, whose depth is the crater's | Ice with no surface expression | Clean ice less than 1 m down at 39–50°N [8], confirmed spectrally by the Compact Reconnaissance Imaging Spectrometer for Mars in the first such craters [10]; scarps at 50–61° expose ice more than 100 m thick from 1–2 m down [8], [13] |

The gap between thermal mapping and radar, from about 30 cm to a few meters, is where the wells would work. A 2026 study recommended that a future orbiter carry a high-frequency radar probing 1–5 m [6].

SWIM, led by the Planetary Science Institute and managed by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory [4], combines these layers. Its central product is an **ice consistency index** for each 3 km pixel, from +1 (fully consistent with ice) through 0 (no indication) to −1 (inconsistent with ice), used as "a universal currency with which to integrate the five individual layers": neutron, thermal, geomorphic, radar surface power, and radar subsurface dielectric [2]. SWIM grew from a 2018 pilot over Arcadia to hemispheric maps, and in 2023 added separate maps for 0–1 m, 1–5 m, and deeper than 5 m [2], [3], [4]. Its authors state that the maps are probabilities and need ground truth.

The last source is the human-mission planning record. NASA's First Landing Site/Exploration Zone Workshop for Human Missions to the Surface of Mars, held in Houston in October 2015, defined an **exploration zone** as a collection of regions of interest within about 100 km of a central landing site [19] and received 47 proposals [20]. Several targeted the regions this chapter compares [21]–[25], and the follow-up imaging list included Phlegra Dorsa, Erebus Montes, Deuteronilus, and Protonilus [42]. The call asked for a water feedstock able to yield more than 100 t [19]; this colony needs roughly eight times as much.

For Arcadia Planitia, a 2015 study used terraced craters and a SHARAD reflector to find "a widespread, decameters-thick layer that is excess water ice ~10⁴ km³ in volume" [7]. Thermal mapping shows ice within 30 cm across much of the region, and NASA's 2019 release called "a large portion of . . . Arcadia Planitia" the most tempting target [12]. The SWIM team's 2021 synthesis concluded that "the broad plains of Arcadia and the extensive glacial networks across Deuteronilus Mensae match the greatest number of remote-sensing criteria for accessible ice-rich, subsurface material" [1]. Three 2015 exploration zone proposals sat in or next to Arcadia [21], [23], [25]. One, along Phlegra Dorsa, estimated about 60% ice by volume in the top 60–80 m. Another spanned Arcadia and Amazonis, with a landing point at 46.2°N, and a third paired Erebus Montes and Acheron Fossae at elevations of about −3.2 and −4.0 km.

Deuteronilus and Protonilus Mensae hold the purest large ice bodies in reach of a landing zone. Radar shows their lobate debris aprons to be "composed predominantly of water ice" [14], more than 80% ice by a later estimate, with a combined minimum volume of about 0.9–1.0 × 10⁵ km³ [15]. The clean ice lies inside aprons flowing off mesas, so a colony would land on the plains and drill or pipe from the apron edges, with overburden anywhere from 0.5 to 10 m [22].

Phlegra Montes is a 1,400 km mountain chain [16]. Its lower-latitude end is attractive for sunlight, and SpaceX's site studies downselected two sites there, at 35.2° and 36.4°N [43], but the terrain is rugged and the debris cover deeper than Arcadia's.

Utopia Planitia's deposit covers about 375,000 km² and holds about as much water as Lake Superior [17], [18], but much of its 1–10 m soil cover lies beyond this chapter's 5 m reach limit [18]. Erebus Montes is best treated as part of the Arcadia ice province; its small massifs break up the flat ground that heavy landers need.

Jezero crater, or any site near the equator, stands in for the opposite strategy: land where science and sunlight are best and get water from minerals. Neutron data show hydrogen at low latitudes, locally more than 40% water-equivalent by weight at Medusae Fossae, which implies buried ice there [32], but its depth and form are unknown. At Jezero the water would come from clays, carbonates, or soil. Curiosity found about 2% water by weight in wind-blown soil at Gale crater [40]. Getting [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s 182 t of nonhabitat water (propellant feed, reserve, and life support start-up) from such soil means heating roughly 9,000 t of regolith, before any water for an ice-shell habitat. NASA's ice-mining study found that "subsurface ice cases . . . would involve less mass and energy . . . compared to any of the mineral cases" [30].

## The reference site: Arcadia Planitia at 42°N
<a id="the-reference-site"></a>

> Assumption: The reference site is a landing and work zone on the Arcadia Planitia plains between about 40° and 44°N and 185° and 200°E, at an elevation of −3 to −4 km. The working point for planning is 42°N, 192°E, at about −3.5 km. The latitude sits where ice-exposing craters are common [8] and inside the ±50° landing limit [19]; the elevation follows the values measured for exploration zones in the same region [21].

The choice rests on four arguments. Arcadia has more independent lines of evidence for shallow ice than any other region: radar, thermal mapping, geomorphology, neutron data, and fresh craters that show ice directly. Its plains give room for a landing field with bermed pads about 1 km apart, at least 2 km from the habitat, reactor field, and wells, and for a construction area inside 5 km ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#reference-site)). Its low elevation helps every heavy landing. And fission-first power makes its weak winter sun affordable.

### The latitude trade

Inside the band, moving north raises confidence that ice lies within 1 m and is continuous, but costs about 25–31% of winter sunlight per 5° of latitude; moving south reverses both. The vehicle adds a third term: SpaceX's 2020 site studies required a latitude below 40° for solar power and thermal management, yet kept two Arcadia sites just north of that line, at 40.02° and 40.75°N, among the seven they downselected [43]. The heavy-lander sites imaged so far lie at about 40°N ([Terrain](#terrain-arcadias-plains-already-imaged-for-heavy-landers)).

The 42°N working point is therefore a deliberate trade: about 2° beyond the vehicle constraint as stated, and about 1.3° beyond the northernmost site the study kept, for more certain ice in the band where exposing craters are common [8]. The plan carries the constraint as a risk to resolve before the Wave 1 target freezes in December 2034 ([R14](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r14)). Either the lander provider accepts 42°N with full payload, or the site moves south to about 40°N, still inside that crater band, where the survey must then find enough ice. Within whichever band survives, drilling decides the final ellipse.

## Verifying the ice before commitment

The ice is the largest unknown among [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s assumptions, so the plan buys certainty in stages. The optional pathfinder departs in April 2033 and lands in January 2034, 17 months before Wave 1 departs. Its site survey must answer four questions: how deep the top of the ice is across the planned well field, how thick and clean the ice is, whether the overburden can be drilled and cased with the Wave 1 drill, and whether a small heated well behaves as the models say. The survey also maps loose rock within about 1 km of the landing sites, because the smooth, rock-poor ground chosen for landing may not supply the roughly 175,000 t of stone that armoring the pads takes ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)).

> Assumption: The pathfinder carries a survey rover with ground-penetrating radar and a neutron spectrometer, a drill able to reach 10 m, and a subscale Rodwell of about 1–2 kWt. Ground radar resolves the top few meters that SHARAD cannot [6]; the drill provides ground truth for both.

> Assumption: The Wave 1 target is frozen six months before launch, in about December 2034. That leaves the pathfinder roughly 11 months of surface work, enough for a radar grid over each candidate well field and 10–20 drill holes. Before the freeze, HiRISE images the final ellipse at boulder scale, which no survey has yet done at 42°N. HiRISE flies on MRO, launched in 2005, and no other orbiter in this plan carries a camera that resolves boulders; if MRO has stopped working by 2034, the ellipse is known only at coarser scale until the first landing there. Imaging the whole 42°N band now, while MRO works, would remove that dependence, and the plan has not yet committed to it.

If the pathfinder does not fly, Wave 1 carries the same survey tools and lands at the working point, and its first six months go to the same survey. Chapter 2 gives Wave 1 two wells and ~0.75 m³/day, but their main job is to prove the well field before Wave 2 commits.

Without the pathfinder, the survey ends about July 2036, some three months after reactor 1 reaches first power, and a reactor that has run cannot easily be moved. Reactor 1 is then sited from the survey's first weeks of data, and if the well field the survey finally picks lies more than about 300 m from it, the wells need longer heat lines, which lose about 33 kWt per kilometer, or electric heating of about 10 kWe each, taken from construction ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md#waste-heat-melts-the-wells)). Holding reactor 1 until the survey ends would instead delay first power by about three months (this chapter's calculation); the plan has not chosen between the two. The armor teams face the same order: they start in March 2036, about four months before the survey has mapped the loose rock they need ([chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md)).

> Assumption: The go/no-go criterion for Wave 2 is a mapped, drilled ice volume within 5 km of the pads able to supply ≥3,000 t of water, more than three and a half times the ~830 t budget, and two wells that have delivered ≥80% of their modeled output for ≥6 months.

The commitment is gate G2 in June 2037 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#exit-criteria-for-gate-g2-june-2037)), three months before Wave 2 departs. The wells take their heat from reactor 1, which gives first power about April 2036, so with a pathfinder their six qualifying months can end about October 2036, eight months before G2. Without one, if the survey takes its full six months, the wells start about July 2036 and qualify about January 2037, five months before G2. Both dates assume full output from the first day; the model builds each well's pool over months ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)), and a well that took two months to reach its modeled rate would qualify about March 2037 without the pathfinder, three months before G2 (this chapter's calculation). Either way the chain closes, but without the pathfinder a slow survey or a slow well start eats most of the margin. The late start also leaves a smaller water bank when Wave 2 lands, about 530 t against about 590 t ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#water-budget)).

## If the ice underdelivers

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#water-budget)'s wells can deliver about 2,900–3,500 t by 2043 at 85% availability against a need of about 830 t, but real extraction is bounded by the ice mapped, at least 3,000 t at the go/no-go. The responses escalate with the shortfall.

| Shortfall | Response | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Wells produce less than modeled | Add wells; each reactor rejects several hundred kWt of waste heat, far more than 10 kWt per well | Drilling time and pipe |
| Ice deeper than expected but still thick | Drill deeper casings; a Rodwell needs only a narrow hole | Mostly drilling time for ice that starts 5–10 m down |
| Ice patchy | Move the well field to an alternative the survey mapped inside the exploration zone, which extends about 100 km by definition [19] | An insulated pipeline or water trucks for a field 5–10 km from the pads, within chapter 8's construction fleet; at that distance a heat loop from the reactor field would lose 165–330 kWt, so the wells need electric heating, about 10 kWe each and 20–60 kWe in all from construction, or a reactor sited beside them (chapter 5) |
| Ice too dirty for Rodwells | Excavate ice-rich soil and melt it in a heated plant | Energy and wear, still less than minerals when the overburden is shallow [30] |
| No usable ice in reach | Take the 182 t for propellant, reserves, and life support from hydrated minerals or soil | Far more energy; the ~650 t habitat shield cannot be made, and the habitat falls back to regolith shielding (chapter 7) |

The pathfinder exists to rule out the last row before Wave 1 departs.

## The backup region and what the pathfinder buys

Deuteronilus Mensae is the backup region. It has purer ice and higher science value, but costs more in landing-site complexity and well-field layout. One pathfinder lander surveys one region, so nobody drills the backup before Wave 1.

The pathfinder buys a drilled primary site and the option to retarget Wave 1 before its launch. If its Arcadia survey fails the [go/no-go test](#verifying-the-ice-before-commitment), Wave 1 can still be sent to Deuteronilus, where it lands at a site known only from orbit, spends its first months on the same survey, and faces the same Wave 2 decision. Without the pathfinder, Wave 1 commits to Arcadia with its ice unconfirmed until its own survey reports.

After Wave 1 lands, the colony can still move its well field anywhere inside the exploration zone, as the table above allows. A move to another region would take later waves, and Wave 1's 250 t of reactor, wells, and robots would be largely stranded, because a colony cannot move that hardware thousands of kilometers. [Appendix A](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md) carries this cost of an optional pathfinder under [R05](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r05).

## What would change this conclusion

Pathfinder drilling that finds Arcadia's ice at the working point thinner than 10 m, dirtier than about 50% ice by volume, or deeper than 5 m across most of the ellipse would shift the reference to Deuteronilus Mensae. A lander that cannot reach −3 km-class sites north of 40°N with its full payload would force a search farther south, where the ice evidence is weaker.

## Autonomy

### What A3 must do

At A3 (site autonomy for known work), the fleet runs a predesigned survey and drilling campaign with no real-time help from Earth. It drives radar grids, picks drill points from a ranked list inside geofenced zones approved on Earth, logs drill cores, and runs standard assays for water content, salts, and perchlorates. It fuses the results into a subsurface model, flags any hole that disagrees with the model beyond a set threshold, holds for Earth review before moving a pad or well field, and keeps the planetary protection log for every wellhead. Teams can rehearse all of it at permafrost drilling sites on Earth.

### What A4 adds

At A4 (open-ended autonomy) the Orchestrator can handle what the plan did not foresee: a buried lens of dirty ice, a casing that jams in cemented soil, a melt cavity that grows sideways into a sediment layer. It can redesign a well's heating profile or a casing procedure and rearrange the well field without waiting through a conjunction, removing most Earth review cycles of days to weeks.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could read the site as geologist and mining engineer at once: turn thousands of drill logs and orbital data into a three-dimensional model of the ice province, predict cleaner ice before drilling, and argue that a science target such as an exposed ice scarp deserves protection from the colony itself.

TRL is NASA's technology readiness level.

Every siting row rests on orbital data. The Rodwell row holds the plan back: it stays at TRL 4–5 until a heated well runs on Mars.

## Risks

Ice at the site underdelivers in depth, purity, or continuity; the pathfinder or Wave 1 survey retires most of this risk before Wave 2 commits ([R05](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r05)).

Well cavities may behave differently on Mars than in models, and only a subscale test on Mars retires that risk ([R36](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r36)).

Planetary protection review may treat heated wells as spacecraft-induced Special Regions and impose cleanliness rules the drilling equipment cannot meet without redesign; the mitigation is agency review of the well design before the pathfinder flies ([R09](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r09)). A life detection or a Special Region ruling could also halt the wells from outside ([R40](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r40)).

The lander provider may not accept a site north of 40°N, which would move the site south toward weaker ice evidence; landing ellipses may also hide hazards below HiRISE resolution, which the pathfinder's landing will test, and the imaging itself depends on MRO still working in 2034 ([R14](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r14)). If the site moves north of 44°N instead, it loses more winter sunlight, which only deferrable loads would feel.

## Open questions

- How clean is Arcadia's ice at the working point, and how thick is it below the first meter? Only drills will say.
- Will the lander provider accept 42°N with full payload before the Wave 1 target freezes in December 2034, or must the site move south to about 40°N?
- Should the reference site move north toward 45°N for more certain ice, at the cost of winter sunlight and colder ground?
- Will any orbiter with a shallow-sounding radar fly before 2033? A 2026 SWIM study recommended such a radar [6], and the International Mars Ice Mapper concept was designed for it [34], but no such mission has launched.
- How will agencies apply the case-by-case Special Region rule to a working well field, and will the 50-year period of biological exploration in COSPAR's policy [28] constrain a colony that plans to operate for decades?
- Do regional dust storms recur more often over Arcadia than over the other candidates?
