# 12. Human readiness and health

*Last reviewed: September 28, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, September 2044.* The crew vehicle is still 11 hours out when the Orchestrator starts the last checklist it will ever run for an empty base. It warms the habitat's sleeping quarters to 21 °C, flushes the potable loop one more time, and posts the morning's perchlorate assay on the wall display: below detection. A rover has swept the landing zone twice. When the four astronauts step through the suitports, dizzy after 10 months of weightlessness, the first thing they find is a clinic that has already scheduled their bone scans and a kitchen that has already made tea.

Chapters 1–11 build a base that runs itself. This chapter asks whether four people can safely live there for about 410 days, between a 305-day flight out and a 220- to 235-day flight home, and what "safe" can mean. The technical crew gate in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#crew-gate) checks power, pressure, propellant, and consumables. It says nothing about the people: their radiation dose and medical care, their bones at 0.38 g and their minds at a 3- to 22-minute delay from home, and the microbes they carry.

Life support, dust control, and planetary protection are engineering problems that robots can solve and verify before anyone launches. Medicine and psychology can be prepared but not proven until people are there. Radiation is the one limit robots cannot move: with the dose rates measured by Curiosity, a conjunction-class mission exceeds NASA's current career limit even with a well-shielded habitat.

In brief: this chapter adds nine human criteria to the crew gate. Most of the dose comes in transit, so the trip itself exceeds NASA's 600 mSv career limit whatever the robots do on the surface; closing that gap needs faster transits or an explicit, informed decision to fly above the limit.

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#crew-mission-and-radiation-dose) fixes the crew mission: four crew depart on November 15, 2043, reach Mars about September 16, 2044, and leave in early November 2045, about 940–950 days in all. They return by a Mars ascent vehicle (MAV) to an Earth return vehicle (ERV) waiting in Mars orbit since September 2042, with a second MAV load in tanks as a lifeboat reserve. Surface stocks, including ~5 t of breathing oxygen and a 9.6 t food reserve, are sized for about 1,000 days as margin; outbound food travels in the crew vehicle and return food in the ERV. The habitat holds a 70 kPa, 26.5% oxygen atmosphere from mid-April 2041 ([chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md)). A medical bay arrives with Wave 3 in September 2040, and the greenhouse supplies about 20–25% of calories ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)).

## Radiation dose budget

### NASA caps a career at 600 mSv

NASA-STD-3001 Volume 1 states in section 4.8.2 that a crew member's "total career effective radiation dose due to spaceflight radiation exposure shall be less than 600 mSv," for all ages and sexes [1]. The rule was adopted in Revision B (2022) and kept in Revision C (2023). The limit keeps each crew member below a 3% mean risk of exposure-induced cancer death and counts past flights plus the next mission; section 4.8.1 adds that every exposure be kept as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA).

The same volume sets the other limits this plan uses [1]. The design solar particle event, the sum of the October 1989 events, is limited to 250 mSv (section 4.8.3), the storm-shelter criterion here. Dose from nuclear power sources is limited to 20 mSv per mission year (4.8.4). Systems supporting crew for more than 60 days must hold the galactic cosmic ray (GCR) effective dose below 1.3 mSv/day in free space and 0.9 mSv/day on a planetary surface (4.8.5).

### Curiosity measured the dose rates

Two measurements anchor the budget, and [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#constants-of-the-place) fixes both. Curiosity's Radiation Assessment Detector (RAD) measured a GCR dose equivalent of 0.64 ± 0.12 mSv/day on the surface at Gale crater [3]. During the 2011–2012 cruise inside the Mars Science Laboratory (MSL) spacecraft, RAD measured 1.84 ± 0.30 mSv/day [4]. The planet blocks half the sky and the atmosphere adds shielding, hence the lower surface rate. These are dose equivalents from one instrument, while NASA's standard uses a model-based effective dose, so the numbers below are planning estimates (±16% for cruise, ±19% for the surface).

### No case fits under the limit

The habitat credit comes from the Ice Home concept of operations [5]. Its model gives a whole-body effective dose of 178 mSv/yr on the unshielded surface (0 km, solar minimum), 75 mSv/yr inside a 2 m water sphere (−58%), and 89 mSv/yr for the updated Ice Home design (−50%).

> Assumption: Crew spend 90% of surface time inside volumes shielded like 2 m of water (−58%) and 10% at the full surface rate: 0.64 × (0.9 × 0.42 + 0.1) = 0.31 mSv/day. The 90% is a planning value for time indoors, not a measured one. The −58% comes from a modeled effective dose and is applied to RAD's measured dose equivalent as an approximation; [chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md#radiation-shielding)'s design credit of about −50% would add about 19 mSv to cases B and C, about 2% of their totals.

Cases A–C fly the 2043 window's lowest-energy path, a Type II transfer that carries the ship more than halfway around the Sun in about 305 days ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)). Cases D and E fly a faster Type I transfer, less than halfway around. They launch on the same date, arrive about July 12, 2044, after 240 days, and leave Mars on the same date, so the stay grows to about 478 days. By this book's two-body calculation that transfer costs little more launch energy, a C3 of about 10 km²/s² against 8.97, but it enters Mars's atmosphere at about 6.7 km/s, just above the 5.5–6.6 km/s the landers are built for; leaving about a week later brings those to about 9.0 km²/s² and 6.5 km/s.

Totals are computed before rounding. Cases A–C span about 810–1,260 mSv, chapter 2's range.

Case C is a design target. The standard's 1.3 mSv/day is an effective dose evaluated against the worst-case 2009 solar minimum [1], whereas RAD measured its 1.84 mSv/day as a dose equivalent in 2011–2012 [4], and no transit vehicle has yet shown the lower rate.

The Southwest Research Institute team that ran RAD estimated about 0.66 Sv for two 180-day transits [6], or about 960–990 mSv scaled to this plan's 525–540 transit days, matching case A's transit dose. The National Academies committee that reviewed NASA's limit put a 1,000-day Mars mission at about 1.07 Sv (solar minimum, behind 5 g/cm² of aluminum) and concluded that a Mars mission is "likely to exceed the exposure standard by up to 150 percent" [2].

Transit dominates: in case B about 89% of the dose comes from the 525–540 days in space, and even perfect surface shielding would bring it only to 966–993 mSv.

Even case E is 24–27% over 600 mSv. The windows fix both departure dates, so each day cut from the outbound leg adds a day on the surface, as cases D and E show. At 1.3 mSv/day in transit and 0.31 mSv/day on the surface, 180 days each way would still give about 630 mSv; the legs would have to shrink to about 166 days each way, with a stay of about 550 days, to reach 600 mSv. The 2043 Type II transfer takes about 305 days outbound alone.

Arrival speed rules out legs that short. By this book's two-body calculation, a 180-day outbound in the 2043 window needs a C3 of about 14.8 km²/s² and about 660 t of propellant, well inside the ship's 1,600 t. It reaches Mars at a v∞ of about 7.1 km/s, though, and would enter at about 8.6 km/s; braking to the landers' 6.6 km/s first would take about 1,700 t by [chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)'s method. A 180-day return from the November 2045 window would enter Earth's atmosphere at about 14.3 km/s, the same on JPL's DE441 ephemeris, against the 12 km/s limit NASA's Design Reference Architecture 5.0 recommended and the 11 km/s or so of every crewed entry so far (chapter 4).

The committee concluded that "for a mission to Mars to proceed, NASA would need to seek waivers to the radiation health standard both for the mission and for each astronaut" [2]. Criterion 7 adopts that framing: each crew member's projected dose is known, documented, and accepted in writing, with the ALARA measures below applied in full.

### The waiver needs allowances above the cases

Cases A–C count galactic cosmic rays at RAD's mean rates and nothing else. A waiver written at their top, about 1,260 mSv, would cover case B, the plan, only up to about 0.8 standard deviations of rate error, with nothing for a solar particle event, the reactors, or the site's elevation. The waiver therefore adds four allowances to the top of case B:

| Allowance | Basis | Added to case B |
|---|---|---:|
| Rate uncertainty | One standard deviation on both RAD rates (±16% cruise, ±19% surface) | 181–186 mSv |
| One design solar particle event (SPE) in transit | A transit shelter holds the event below NASA's 250 mSv limit (see below) | up to 250 mSv |
| Reactors | 20 mSv per mission year at the habitat, over 410 days | 22 mSv |
| Site elevation | About 2 g/cm² less air than at Gale ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md)), about 4% more surface dose | 6 mSv |
| **Upper bound** | Case B (1,091–1,119 mSv) plus the four allowances | **1,551–1,583 mSv** |

Each crew member's waiver covers about 1,600 mSv, about 2.6 times the career limit. That ceiling also holds case A at mean rates (1,256 mSv) and chapter 7's −50% habitat credit (19–22 mSv more). The elevation term is this book's rough estimate: it interpolates linearly between RAD's cruise rate, halved for the planet's shadow, and its surface rate under about 23 g/cm² of air, and a radiation transport model should replace it. Criterion 7's habitat dosimetry measures the site's own rate before the gate.

A missed return window ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md#return-windows-and-stay-length)) keeps the crew on the surface about 1,190 days instead of 410. Case B then rises to about 1,330–1,360 mSv at mean rates, and to about 1,890–1,920 mSv with the same allowances, the reactor term growing to 65 mSv. Once the window is missed the crew has no earlier way home, so each waiver also covers that case, up to about 1,900 mSv, and is signed before launch.

Two ERV cooling failures in a row could keep the crew longer still. If the ERV's cooling fails after the crew has launched and the replacement ERV's own cooling then falls short, the crew stays on the surface until about April 2050, about 2,030 days, and goes home on a third ERV ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#schedule-logic-what-slips-and-by-how-much)); with two coolers on each ERV that happens in about 2–3 missions in 1,000 at the flown cooler failure rate, and in up to about 2 in 100 at that rate's 90% bound. Case B then comes to about 1,570 mSv at mean rates, inside the waivers' 1,900 mSv, and to about 2,240 mSv with the same allowances, the reactor term growing to about 110 mSv. That upper figure, about 3.7 times the career limit, lies above what the waivers cover (this book's calculation).

By this book's scaling of the basis NASA gives for its limit, a 3% mean risk of dying of a radiation-induced cancer at 600 mSv for a 35-year-old woman, the most sensitive case [1], [2], case B gives such a crew member a mean risk of about 5.5% for the planned mission, 7% after a missed window, and 8% for the stay to 2050, or about 8%, 9.5%, and 11% with the allowances. The estimates the National Academies cite for a Mars mission carry an uncertainty of about a factor of three either way [2]. The doses also reach the range where tissue effects begin. With the quality factors RAD measured, about 3.8 in cruise, 3.05 on the surface, and about 1 for the protons of a solar particle event [3], case B's cosmic rays give an absorbed dose of about 0.3 Gy for the planned mission, 0.4 Gy after a missed window, and 0.45–0.5 Gy for the stay to 2050, and the allowances add about 0.3–0.5 Gy, more than half of it from the design SPE (this book's calculation). The ICRP puts the threshold for cataracts at 0.5 Gy whatever the dose rate, and says the threshold for circulatory disease may be as low as 0.5 Gy to the heart or brain [67], [68]. NASA's standard relies on its 600 mSv limit to keep the heart inside its circulatory limits of 250 mGy-Eq in any 30 days, 500 in a year, and 1,000 over a career, absorbed dose weighted by how much each kind of radiation harms tissue [1]; weighted between that standard's factors for protons and for heavy ions, 1.5 and 2.5, the heart's dose with the allowances comes to about 800–1,200 mGy-Eq for the planned mission, 1,000–1,550 after a missed window, and 1,200–1,900 for the stay to 2050 (this book's calculation). Each waiver therefore accepts a risk of cataracts and heart disease as well as of cancer.

> Assumption: Each crew member's waiver keeps its ceiling of about 1,900 mSv, and the consent signed with it names the stay to about April 2050 as a potential exposure, a dose that only equipment failures could bring, with its probability, its dose, what that dose would mean for health, and the steps below that would lower it. Justification: radiation protection handles such doses apart from its dose limits. The ICRP applies dose limits only to doses expected with certainty; it judges a potential exposure by its probability times the risk it would carry and asks for safeguards in proportion to that probability [69]. The IAEA's safety standards let a potential exposure assessed in advance be handled, if it happens, as part of the planned situation or as a declared emergency [70], and the Institute of Medicine's ethics framework has NASA disclose to each astronaut, before flying, the types, sizes, and possible consequences of known and uncertain risks beyond the standard [71]. None of these addresses a crew stranded beyond a signed waiver, so the rule is taken by analogy. Weighted by its probability, the stay adds about 4–7 in 100,000 to each crew member's risk of a fatal cancer at the flown cooler failure rate, about a tenth of what the missed window adds. The ICRP's generic risk constraint for workers' potential exposures, 2 in 10,000 a year, applies to the yearly chance of such an exposure times the risk it carries [69], so this book spreads the stay's weighted risk over the years in which its two failures can happen, the four from the crew's launch to the end of the replacement's hold in December 2047; the stay's later years add dose, not chances. That gives about 2 in 100,000 a year at the flown cooler rate, a tenth of the constraint, or a fifth with the allowances and a fault both ERVs share. At that rate's 90% bound it gives about 1 in 10,000 a year, and nearly 2 in the worst combination, up against the constraint. The missed window can be set off only by a failure within about a year of launch, so its weighted risk, about 5 in 10,000, runs some 2.5 times the constraint a year; it is too likely to treat as a potential exposure, and the waiver covers it as it covers the plan (this book's calculation). Switch: if the ERV coolers' life test measures a rate near the flight record's 90% bound when gate G4 reads it in July 2041, this book flies the four coolers on each ERV that the evidence in [chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md#the-earth-return-vehicle) favors, which all but remove the stay; if the ERVs kept two, the waiver itself would name the stay, up to about 2,240 mSv.

If the stay happens, the crew can lower its dose without new hardware. The 2049/50 window allows a return of about 165–170 days, leaving about April 20–22, 2050, and entering Earth's atmosphere at less than 12 km/s, within the ERV's propellant for its departure and alignment burns even at the upper alignment cost; against the 212-day return from April 4 it saves about 70–80 mSv at mean rates. Moving from 90% to 98% of surface time indoors, with robots doing the outdoor work, saves about 50 mSv over the stranded years. Sleeping under the thickest water saves nothing more, because the crew already sleeps there, and the habitat model gives those quarters about −50%, less than the −58% the dose cases count ([chapter 7](07.%20Habitat.md#radiation-shielding)). Together the shorter return and the time indoors take about 120–130 mSv off the mean figure and about 140–150 mSv off the upper one, which still leaves about 2,090 mSv, above the waiver (this book's calculation).

### What reduces the dose

The levers, in order of effect:

1. Shorter transits. A day cut from the return saves 1.3–1.8 mSv; a day cut from the outbound saves 1.0–1.5 mSv, because the crew spends it on the surface instead. Arrival speed sets the limit: the landers enter Mars's atmosphere at up to 6.6 km/s, and the planned return already enters Earth's at 12.1–13.7 km/s, faster than any crew has entered (chapter 4). This book's working assumption takes the return that reaches Earth about June 23, 2046, at no more than about 12.5 km/s, which keeps the dose within the planned range but rules out a shorter return.
2. Transit shielding. The rationale for section 4.8.5 notes that 10–40 g/cm² of shielding cuts the unshielded free-space GCR effective dose of 1.5 mSv/day by about 15%, to 1.3 mSv/day, and that 40–100 g/cm² has "either a negligible or negative impact" [1]. So case C is the best a shielded vehicle can do. NASA's Human Research Roadmap favors polyethylene and water against solar particle events and notes that GCR "would require enormous mass to shield" [9]. Water, food, and waste stowed around the sleeping quarters are the cheapest shield.
3. Crew selection. Prior flights count against the career limit, so crew with little previous flight time keep more of the 600 mSv.
4. Surface layout. Crew sleep and work in the best-shielded volumes, EVAs follow a dose budget, and the reactors sit where they add less than 20 mSv per mission year [1].
5. Regolith, used with care. Regolith moderates neutrons, but dose equivalent peaks at 30–40 cm depth, so thin cover can raise dose [10]; about 1–1.6 m is needed for less than 100 mSv/yr [11]. The Ice Home's water shell, hydrogenous as the standard advises for long surface stays [1], already exceeds that.

### Solar particle events and the storm shelter

Solar particle events (SPEs) are bursts of protons from flares and coronal mass ejections. During the MSL cruise they spiked dose rates 10–100 times above background yet gave only about 5% of the cruise dose [8]; the danger is that one large event can deliver in hours what GCR delivers in months.

On the surface, the atmosphere helps. In the September 10–12, 2017, event, RAD sat under about 23 g/cm² of carbon dioxide; its dose rate rose 2–3 times above background for about 12 hours, the event total was 418 µGy, and the 30-day dose was "well below" NASA's limits [7]. Earth and Mars were then about 155° apart in heliospheric longitude [7], so forecasts from Earth cannot be the only warning.

The shelter must hold the design event below 250 mSv (section 4.8.3), and the standard's guidance for a surface mission of any length is 10 g/cm² of water equivalent around each crew member (Table 4.8-4) [1]. The Ice Home shell, at 2–3 m of water, exceeds that by a factor of 20 or more, so the habitat's inner core, under the thickest ice, is the shelter (chapter 7), with life support for four crew for 7 sols.

The crew spends 525–540 days in transit, where no planet or air helps. The crew vehicle and the ERV each carry a shelter built from the water, food, and waste stowed around the sleeping quarters, and each must hold the design event below the same 250 mSv limit, because the waiver's SPE allowance assumes it. Criterion 7 checks the habitat shelter on Mars and the two transit shelters by design review.

> Assumption: Every crew location on the surface is within 30 minutes of shelter, including EVA sites and the pressurized rover's range. The rover is allocated to Wave 3 from its unitemized mass (chapter 2) and carries a water-jacketed compartment with 10 g/cm² for four crew. EVAs stop and crews return on a local alert, without waiting for Earth. The 30 minutes is this book's planning choice; the compartment's 10 g/cm² is the standard's guidance above [1].

Wearable shielding helps against SPEs only. The AstroRad vest, fit-tested on the International Space Station (ISS) [38] and flown on the uncrewed Artemis I mission, would by extrapolation cut effective dose by about 60% in an August 1972-type event and by just under 40% in an October 1989-type event, NASA's design case [39], [40].

## Life support ready for people

[Chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md) designs the life support system; criteria 8 and 9 set what "ready for people" means. The ISS reached 98% water recovery only in 2023 [23], with resupply several times a year. NASA analyses find its oxygen generation assembly inadequate for a Mars transit [24] and say deep-space life support needs far higher reliability, because spares cannot be shipped on demand [25].

The Mars base has time that a transit vehicle lacks: by the launch decision the habitat has held crew air for more than 2 years, and criterion 8 uses that time. From 2042, human metabolic simulators run in the habitat, consuming oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide, water vapor, and heat at the rate of four people. They also feed the water recovery system urine and wash-water simulants at four people's daily rate, so the urine and brine processors run under the load they will carry; on the ISS, four urine-processor units failed 7.5–22 times more often than predicted ([chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)). The Orchestrator logs every fault and repair, and the crew launches only if the system has run for 12 months without an unrecovered fault and its spares consumption matches the stock on hand.

Trace contaminants need separate attention, because new materials outgas and water loops grow biofilms; criterion 9 sets the 6-month test. It runs while the simulators breathe and robots cycle suits through the suitports, so the air is judged under a crew's load of contaminants and dust. Six months of clean air in the empty habitat, possible from October 2041, would show only that it outgasses little.

The habitat's air also needs a test on Earth. Its buffer gas is about 43% argon by volume, about 22 kPa of argon at 70 kPa. People have lived in argon-rich air: in Russian fire-safety studies six men spent 60 days in air with 30–35% argon and about 14% oxygen at normal pressure without unacceptable effects on health or work, and others 10 days in 35–45% argon [49], [50]. None of them then decompressed to suit pressure, and [chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md) notes that NASA's Exploration Atmospheres Working Group left decompression sickness with argon present as an open question. Criterion 9 therefore includes a chamber study on Earth in which people live in the habitat's gas for weeks, at least three days before the first decompression in case argon leaves the body half as fast as nitrogen, as NASA's analysis assumes [46], and then make walking EVAs at suit pressure on the planned protocol. It can follow NASA's own prebreathe tests: 11-day series of eight people, each making five 6-hour simulated EVAs with bubbles in the heart checked by ultrasound every 15 minutes [51]. The study passes if the protocol meets NASA's limits in this gas, at 95% confidence no more than 15% of exposures with decompression sickness and 20% with the worst grade of bubbles, and no serious (Type II) case [44], [51], and does no worse than NASA's tests of a similar nitrogen cabin [52]. That allows at most two cases in about 50 exposures, and a direct comparison with nitrogen would take several hundred exposures a side (this book's calculation). NASA needed two series a year apart to reach 50 exposures [51], so the study starts by about 2037. It also measures how fast argon leaves the body, the half-time on which every prebreathe estimate in this chapter rests. The result is due at gate G3 in June 2039, while Wave 3's manifest can still change: if argon fails, Wave 3 carries a larger capture unit and a small column that separates the argon out ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)). A failure found after G3 has no hardware on Mars until the support lander of September 2042, too late for criterion 2's 12-month pressure hold, which must begin in the crew's gas by about August 2042 ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#the-crew-gate-2043)), so the crew would move one window.

## Medicine far from Earth

### The base is the hospital

NASA's standard requires "medical evacuation to a location with a higher level of medical care" and in section 3.1.6 admits the Mars problem: "a return to Earth may not be possible, or may take such a long time as to be rendered futile" [1]. The ERV cannot leave for Earth before early November 2045, so all care happens at the base. Its medical bay needs imaging (ultrasound at minimum), a clinical laboratory, oxygen, intravenous therapy, anesthesia, surgery, dental care, a pharmacy, and a crew able to use them.

> Assumption: The crew includes one physician with surgical and emergency training and a second member trained to assist in surgery and anesthesia; all four hold advanced first aid and ultrasound qualifications.

### Telemedicine with a delay

On the ISS, telemedicine works in near real time. In 2019–2020 a research ultrasound found a blood clot in an astronaut's jugular vein; radiologists on Earth guided follow-up scans live, the astronaut started an anticoagulant from the station kit, and a later cargo flight brought another drug and reversal agents [15]. At Mars neither live guidance nor quick resupply exists: a cargo flight takes a launch window and 7–10 months. Across a 6- to 45-minute round trip, physicians on Earth can still review images and advise on slow problems; fast problems belong to the crew and the Orchestrator, and around conjunction even slow advice stops for 2–3 weeks.

### Surgery falls to the crew and a supervised robot

No major surgery has been performed on a human in space. In February 2024, surgeons in Nebraska remotely controlled a small robot, spaceMIRA, on the ISS to cut simulated tissue, with a signal delay of about two-thirds to three-quarters of a second [16], [41]. At Mars delays, teleoperated surgery from Earth is impossible; the crew physician operates, supervises an autonomous surgical robot, or both. The only physician can also be the patient, as Leonid Rogozov showed in 1961 when he removed his own appendix at a Soviet Antarctic station [17]. The second crew member trained to assist covers that case.

### Drugs expire before the crew gets home

Drugs age. In a 2019 review by NASA and university researchers, 87% of the medications flown on the ISS had labeled shelf lives of less than 24 months, and ground and flight studies found degradation in antibiotics, levothyroxine, aspirin, ibuprofen, and several others [18]. The mission lasts about 31 months, so the pharmacy flies with the crew instead of with Wave 3. Packed at launch, a drug with a 24-month label stays in date until about the crew's departure from Mars in November 2045, seven months before the crew reaches Earth, and no resupply can close that gap: the next flight, in the 2045/46 window, lands about October 2046, after the crew has left. Stocking return drugs in the ERV would make them older, not newer, since it leaves in 2041. Labels, however, understate how long most drugs last. The Shelf Life Extension Program, which the U.S. Food and Drug Administration runs for the Department of Defense, tested 3,005 lots of 122 drug products kept in their original packaging and extended 88% of them by at least a year past their labels, by 66 months on average; the gain varied from lot to lot, so only testing each lot assures it [48]. The pharmacy therefore flies on tested dates. If the crew misses its return window, the 2045/46 flight lands while they are still there and carries a pharmacy resupply ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md#return-windows-and-stay-length)), about 34–35 months after launch and 10–11 months after the 24-month labels lapse; the same testing covers those months, and the resupply's own 24-month labels, counted from its launch in December 2045, run only to about the late departure in December 2047, so the same testing also covers the way home.

> Assumption: The crew pharmacy flies with the crew in November 2043, packed from the freshest available lots in their original, unbroken packaging, in radiation-shielded, temperature-controlled stowage, with extra stock of drugs known to degrade and of those needed on the return. Sister lots of the same batches stay on Earth in the same conditions and are tested at intervals from before launch, as the Shelf Life Extension Program tests its lots [48]: criterion 10 requires every lot to be dated through the planned return to Earth in mid-2046, 31 months after launch, by its label or by that testing, and a lot that cannot be is replaced by another batch or formulation before launch. The testing goes on through the mission and extends dates by uplink. Earth storage does not reproduce the flight's radiation, so the medical laboratory also checks the potency of critical drugs before use. Medical equipment and consumables with long shelf lives arrive in Wave 3 and are checked by robots. On-site synthesis of simple drugs remains a research objective outside the baseline.

## Nobody knows what 0.38 g does to the body

Criterion 10 also asks for countermeasures, and here the evidence is thinnest. The crew will spend about 525–540 days in weightlessness and about 410 days at 0.38 g, where no human has ever lived; the longest stay in partial gravity is Apollo 17's three days on the Moon.

On the ISS, crew lost areal bone mineral density at about 0.9% per month in the spine and 1.4–1.5% per month in the hip [12]. The Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's mouse experiments on the ISS, using an onboard centrifuge to simulate 1/6 g, found that lunar gravity prevented atrophy of the soleus muscle but not the shift in muscle fiber type [13]. A 2021 conference paper by NASA Ames engineer Harry Jones concludes from indirect evidence that partial gravity below 0.4 g "will be insufficient to maintain musculoskeletal and cardiopulmonary conditioning over the long term" [14], as did a 2017 systematic review [42].

The human dose-response curve between 0 and 1 g is unknown, as is whether exercise at 0.38 g can replace what is lost, or how 0.38 g affects the fluid shifts and eye changes of long spaceflight. The Moon and ISS programs of the 2030s may narrow these gaps, but this plan does not count on answers before the crew flies.

The crew arrives deconditioned and recovers on exercise equipment that came with Wave 3 and passed robot load tests. Bone density scans run at arrival and every 90 days, and the pharmacy carries drugs used against bone loss on Earth.

> Assumption: The crew performs no heavy manual work in the first 30 sols after landing. Robots do every task on the arrival checklists. The 30 sols is a planning choice: nobody has measured how long people take to recover at 0.38 g after some 305 days of weightlessness.

## Dust, perchlorate, suits, and suitports

### Apollo showed that dust harms people too

[Chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md#dust-sets-the-service-life) covers what Apollo dust [26], the only human experience with planetary dust, did to machines. For crews the record adds inhalation and irritation: Harrison Schmitt described "lunar hay fever" on Apollo 17, and all 12 moonwalkers reported some irritation [27]. Mars dust adds chemistry: a 2025 review lists its toxic components as perchlorates, silica, nanophase iron oxides, gypsum, and trace metals such as chromium, beryllium, arsenic, and cadmium whose abundances are still debated [31].

### A pinch of dust a day reaches the perchlorate limit

Phoenix measured 0.4–0.6% perchlorate by mass in soil at its landing site [28], and perchlorate is widespread in Martian soils at roughly 0.5–1% [29]. Perchlorate blocks iodide uptake by the thyroid and so reduces thyroid hormone output [29]. The U.S. National Academy of Sciences recommended, and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency adopted in 2005, a reference dose of 0.0007 mg per kg of body weight per day [30].

For a 70 kg adult the reference dose is 0.049 mg per day. At 0.5% perchlorate, that is reached by swallowing or inhaling about 10 mg of dust a day, a barely visible pinch. Dust tracked into a habitat, carried on hands to food, or suspended in cabin air could reach that level without anyone noticing.

Suits stay outside. Well water is treated for perchlorate before it enters the potable loop and assayed under criterion 9. Air filtration and surface cleaning run continuously, the crew's thyroid function is tested at intervals, and the pharmacy carries potassium iodide. Biological removal, which would also recover oxygen [29], stays in the laboratory until planetary protection rules allow it.

### Suitports keep suits and dust outside

No spacesuit has been designed for Mars. Axiom Space's AxEMU for Artemis III is built for the lunar south pole's vacuum and cold [33]; a Mars suit must work in 610 Pa of carbon dioxide, which conducts some heat, and in fine, charged dust, for hundreds of EVAs over about 410 days.

A suitport attaches a rear-entry suit to the outside wall of a habitat or rover, so the suit never comes inside. NASA engineers expect suitports to cut pre-EVA time from hours to less than 30 minutes, reduce airlock gas losses, and keep dust out of the cabin; chamber tests in 2012–2013 showed that crew could don and doff a pressurized rear-entry suit through prototype suitports [32]. Suitports are the baseline for this plan, with a conventional airlock kept for suit maintenance and emergencies.

The 30-minute figure assumes NASA's 56.5 kPa, 34% oxygen exploration atmosphere, which was designed to need little oxygen prebreathe ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md#habitat-atmosphere-70-kpa-chosen-for-fire-safety)); NASA's model of a 15-minute prebreathe from it put the risk of decompression sickness at 6–12% per EVA [45], and in 2022–2023 NASA validated a 20-minute prebreathe from it with people making walking EVAs in a chamber: two mild cases in 50 exposures, 4% [51]. This habitat runs at 70 kPa and 26.5% oxygen, the Shuttle's staged atmosphere, with about 51 kPa of nitrogen and argon. NASA sizes prebreathe by a tissue ratio: the inert gas left in a slow body tissue with a 360-minute half-time, divided by suit pressure [43]. Its standard accepts decompression sickness in at most 15% of exposures and no serious (Type II) cases [44]. NASA's Exploration Atmospheres Working Group modeled four-hour walking EVAs with exercise, a worst case for the Moon or Mars, after a 60-minute prebreathe. For this atmosphere and the 29.6 kPa suit of the Shuttle and the ISS its model gives an 18% risk [43], above that limit, and NASA's 2023 estimate of the prebreathe from a cabin like this one into that suit is 130–240 minutes [44]. Argon makes every option worse. It is about twice as soluble as nitrogen in fatty tissue, and NASA's analysis of Martian nitrogen-argon habitats assumes it leaves more slowly, with a 720-minute half-time, which adds about 50 minutes to the 29.6 kPa prebreathe and 25 to the 34.5 kPa one; it concludes that argon "significantly increases the risk" of decompression sickness [46]. In a U.S. Air Force chamber study, subjects who breathed 62% argon and 38% oxygen at an intermediate stage before going to 24 kPa got decompression sickness 78% of the time, against 55% on oxygen [47]. > Hypothesis: Kilopascal for kilopascal, argon dissolved in the crew's tissue counts about 1.5 times as much as nitrogen toward decompression sickness, but it leaves the body about as fast as nitrogen does. Basis: NASA's table of gas properties gives argon about twice nitrogen's solubility and 1.8 times its permeation into a bubble, but nearly the same blood-to-fat solubility ratio, 0.220 for argon against 0.237 for nitrogen, which by that analysis's own washout formula makes the two half-times nearly equal; its 720-minute half-time for argon is an assumption [46]. Under this hypothesis's own washout, then, the one human study points below 1.5; the weight takes the rats' upper figure, which errs toward a longer prebreathe. Animals carry the claim only part of the way, and the one human study loaded argon for four hours; nobody has been saturated with it and then decompressed. Test: criterion 9's chamber study, which measures the decompression outcome after at least three days in the gas and argon's washout in the exhaled breath, by G3 in June 2039; a result like nitrogen's lets a 41 kPa suit go without prebreathe, and one worse than the bound sends Wave 3's argon column ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)).

Switch rule: if full-suit tests over repeated walking EVAs, or criterion 9's study, show hand or forearm injury at 41–43 kPa that lower pressures avoid, the plan drops to 34.5 kPa and accepts about 1.4–3 hours of oxygen per EVA. It does the same if cycle tests show that a suit whose joints are built for 41 kPa still cannot reach the life NASA asks of exploration suits: 200,000 cycles for its bearings over about three years [61], and for its gloves the planetary life NASA's glove team set, up to 576 suitport EVAs of 45 minutes in six months [63]. Wear that more spares or heavier joints can cover does not move the suit, since at 34.5 kPa each EVA costs about 1.4–1.9 more hours of oxygen (this book's calculation).

Basis: a pressurized joint's bearing carries the suit pressure times its area, so at 41 kPa it carries about 1.4 times its load at 29.6 kPa. By Hertz's contact theory, which NASA's bearing tests use, the stress in the race rises with the cube root of the load, about 12% [61], and by the standard bearing-life law fatigue life falls to about a quarter to a third [62] (this book's calculation). In NASA's tests titanium races survived 528,000 cycles at titanium's yield stress and began to fail at 125–135% of it, and NASA limits long-term designs to 100–115% [61]; a race sized to that limit at 29.6 kPa would sit at 112–129% at 41 kPa, reaching the band NASA keeps for short off-nominal use and, at the top, the level it says should not be used for design. Built for 41 kPa from the start, a bearing needs about 40% more load capacity: more or larger balls, or the stainless steel races that titanium replaced to save about 10 kg per suit [61]. NASA has built suit joints for more: the Z-1 prototype, whose bearings those tests studied, was designed to operate at 57 kPa (8.3 psid) [66], and the xEMU's cycle requirements include pressurized time at 56.5 kPa (8.2 psid) [65]. Joints wear out even at 29.6 kPa: in NASA's 2022 cycle test of its xEMU suit, the only pressure the facility could test, the thigh bearings were nearly unusable without maintenance after about 140 hours of pressurized time, the life set for an initial lunar mission, and returned to normal torque once cleaned and fitted with new plastic balls; NASA's engineers suspect corrosion between steel balls and titanium races and high contact stress [65], so a Mars suit needs joints it can service or replace at any pressure. Gloves wear mostly where their outer cover and restraints rub: after 50 pressurized sessions at 29.6 kPa in lunar dust simulant, the cycling of a six-month planetary mission, NASA's test gloves finished without failing but with holes in the outer cover and restraint webbing worn through, and no damage to the pressure bladder [63]; the restraints' tension rises with pressure. Test: NASA's elevated-pressure series of full-suit, cycle, and high-tempo tests, its full-suit tests under way in 2026 [60], [64]; a rig test of the colony suit's bearings at 1.4 times their 29.6 kPa load through 200,000 cycles, before the suit design is frozen for Wave 3, about 2038; and the suits cycled through the suitports for criterion 11 from September 2040, run at the working pressure with joint and glove wear logged. Joints or gloves built for 41 kPa that still fall short of NASA's life refute it.

## Planetary protection for a crewed base

People change the planetary protection problem, because a human body cannot be sterilized.

The Committee on Space Research (COSPAR) policy, in its 2026 version approved November 7, 2025, states that "planetary protection goals should not be relaxed to accommodate a human mission to Mars." The goals "become even more directly relevant to such missions," even if "specific implementation guidelines should differ" [34]. Its crewed-mission guidelines call for monitoring the microbes crews carry, "a quarantine capability and protocols for the crew," robotic evaluation of any uncharacterized site "prior to crew access," Category V "Restricted Earth return" handling for pristine samples, an onboard crew member responsible for planetary protection, and a conservative approach for initial missions. Special Regions, the places where Earth organisms could replicate, keep their Category IVc access controls [34] ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md)).

NASA's own rules are in transition. Its planetary protection standard, NASA-STD-8719.27 (2022), is written mainly for robotic missions [35]. The NASA interim directive (NID) for human missions to Mars, NID 8715.129, took effect on July 9, 2020, and was extended to September 30, 2025 [37]. NASA canceled it before it expired and, on September 29, 2025, removed references to it from its planetary protection procedures [36]. NASA's Office of Planetary Protection described work in April 2025 toward a "performance-based policy framework" [37], but in September 2026 no binding NASA rulebook for a crewed Mars base exists.

The zoning map of criterion 13, built from more than seven years of mapping, draws keep-out zones around any ground where water ice is warm or brines might form. It separates the crew area, where human microbes are expected, from science zones, and records every robot and crew path. The habitat has a quarantine module: a sealed room with its own air handling where a crew member can be isolated. The crew commander designates the onboard planetary protection lead, as COSPAR recommends. A finding of life or a Special Region ruling near the wells could halt them and cut off the base's water ([R40](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r40)).

The greenhouse, the water loop, and any biological perchlorate process stay sealed ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)); deliberate release of Earth organisms into Martian soil has no approval path under current policy.

## Crew and Orchestrator protocols

Criterion 15 rehearses four rules for sharing the base with the Orchestrator, written for its most capable form, an A5 mind ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md)), and applied unchanged at A3.

1. Life-safety authority rests with the crew commander. Safety-critical controllers for pressure, oxygen, power, and the reactors stay independent of the Orchestrator at every autonomy level, and the crew can command them directly.
2. Medical consent belongs to the patient. An A5 mind may advise, may operate under the physician's supervision, and may perform emergency care when no human can, but a conscious crew member can refuse treatment by it.
3. The mind's own standing is respected. An A5 mind may decline a task it judges wrong or too hazardous to itself or to others, and it must say so and explain why. The crew and Earth then decide what to do.
4. Nothing depends on A5. Every crew gate criterion must be met at A3.

> Assumption: These four rules are this book's proposal. No space agency has published rules for crew interaction with a self-aware artificial mind.

## What the crew must find on arrival

By the launch decision the Orchestrator has run the habitat as if people were inside, with simulators breathing and shelter drills timed. On arrival the landing zone has been surveyed and marked with beacons and a backup site, the pressurized rover waits there charged, and air and water assays cover the last 30 sols. The shelter is stocked for four crew for 7 sols, and the medical bay is calibrated. The greenhouse is already producing, which offsets the vitamins stored food loses over 1,000 days ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md#what-four-people-need)).

The crew also finds records of every sensor reading and repair since 2036, so the physician can check years of air and water data and the commander can see how the base behaved in the last dust storm and conjunction.

> Assumption: Crew members join the program no later than 2039 and train with the base's digital twin, the operations team, and each other from then on. They review the base's records at each gate before committing to fly. The year is a planning choice, about four years before launch, not an agency's training rule.

## What would change this conclusion

Transits of about 165 days each way, in a vehicle held to NASA's 1.3 mSv/day limit and able to enter at the speeds such transits arrive at, would bring the mission dose to about 600 mSv; extra shielding mass beyond 10–40 g/cm² would buy little. Evidence on whether 0.38 g protects bone and muscle would change the countermeasure plan, and a binding planetary protection framework for crewed missions would replace the provisional zoning rules. A chamber result that argon in tissue acts like nitrogen would remove the hour of oxygen before a 41 kPa EVA; one worse than this chapter's bound would take the argon out of the cabin instead, and hand injuries at 41 kPa, or joints and gloves that cannot reach NASA's exploration life at that pressure even when built for it, would move the suit to 34.5 kPa.

## Autonomy

### What A3 must do

At A3 the Orchestrator runs known procedures for weeks without contact: dosimetry and local SPE detection with its own shelter call, fault recovery for life support, scheduled assays, dust cleaning, stock control, the prearrival checklist, and medical logistics. When a crew member falls ill, an A3 system is an instrument and a record-keeper; the physician decides.

### What A4 adds

At A4 the Orchestrator becomes a clinical decision partner: reading ultrasound images, suggesting diagnoses, checking drug interactions against a pharmacy whose contents have aged, and, with the physician supervising, driving a surgical robot through steps too fine or too long for tired hands. It can diagnose a novel life support fault and have robots build the part.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could practice every medical specialty, operate with steady hands, know the base after nine years of living in it, and design and synthesize drugs on site, closing the pharmacy gap. The four rules above bind it as they bind any Orchestrator.

Two items hold back the crew's readiness most: a Mars suit, still undesigned, and supervised robotic surgery, at TRL 4–5.

## Risks

Crew dose over the career limit is near-certain; the controls are faster transits ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)), crew selection, and informed waivers ([R19](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r19)). The acute SPE hazard is controlled by the habitat and transit shelters, verified against the 250 mSv limit, and by local warning ([R01](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r01)).

A medical emergency beyond the base's capability has no evacuation option during the surface stay, and pharmacy aging on the return leg, or on the surface after a missed return window until the resupply lands, is part of the same risk ([R23](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r23)).

Behavioral health under isolation ([R24](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r24)) and deconditioning at 0.38 g ([R25](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r25)) depend on data that does not exist yet.

Suitports, water treatment, and monitoring control perchlorate and dust toxicity ([R20](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r20)). Food shortfalls, including nutrient loss in stored food ([R33](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r33)), and a long loss of habitat heating ([R32](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r32)) bear directly on crew health. Life support reliability ([R03](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r03)) and habitat leaks ([R15](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r15)) are tested by 12 months at human load before launch.

Planetary protection has a policy gap on the NASA side, even though the COSPAR goals are clear ([R09](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r09)), and an outside ruling could halt the wells ([R40](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r40)). Governance conflict with an A5 mind is a new category of risk, discussed in [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md) ([R21](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r21)).

## Open questions

- Will the agency or company flying the crew accept a mission dose of up to about 1,600 mSv (cases A–C with allowances), and about 1,900 mSv after a missed return window, with individual waivers, or require faster transits? It is the question most likely to move the crew departure date. Will the flyer also accept a consent that names the rarer stay to about April 2050, up to about 2,240 mSv, as a potential exposure outside the waiver, or want the waiver itself to cover it? And what do absorbed doses of 0.3–1 Gy, mostly from cosmic rays, do to the lens and the heart, when the human data at such doses come from gamma rays on Earth?
- What does 0.38 g do to bone, muscle, and eyes over about 410 days?
- What suit pressure and oxygen prebreathe does walking EVA from the 70 kPa cabin need: about 29.6 kPa with roughly three to five hours of oxygen before each EVA, 34.5 kPa with about one and a half to three, or 41 kPa, this chapter's working assumption, with none to about an hour, and how much does the argon add? Can gloves at 41 kPa be used day after day without hand or forearm injury, and do the suit's joints and gloves, built for that pressure, last the stay's EVAs?
- How much surgery can a supervised autonomous robot perform by 2043, and who certifies it?
- Whose rules govern planetary protection for a crewed base now that NID 8715.129 has been canceled?
- How will four people and an artificial colleague share a small base for 410 days?
