# 14. Critical challenges

*Last reviewed: September 26, 2026*

*Scenario, Houston, March 2035.* The Wave 1 gate review has one slide the whole room waits for: seven rows, one for each risk the program has carried since 2026, each with a colored cell. Refueling turned green last spring, after a run of ships was filled on schedule. Landing is amber: one heavy lander sits intact at Arcadia, and one landing is not yet a record. Radiation is amber and will stay that way, because the crew's projected doses are already known and sit above the limit. Integration is red, and everyone expects it to stay red for years, since only a whole base running together on Mars can turn it green. Nobody proposes waiting for that. The board votes to launch three landers in June.

This chapter groups most of the highest-scoring risks in the [Appendix A](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md) register into seven areas. The register's score weights likelihood, impact, and detectability together. For each area the chapter summarizes the evidence, which lives in the system chapters, and asks two questions: what autonomy can do about it, at A3, at A4, and with an A5 mind (the levels [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md) defines), and what is left over, set by physics or by policy, that no level of intelligence on Mars can change.

The seven are integration and autonomy shortfall; power and dust; planetary protection and biosafety; water and in situ resource utilization (ISRU) uncertainty; logistics, meaning orbital refueling, launch cadence, and landing heavy cargo; radiation; and communications and latency. They appear in the order of the highest score each carries. Because the score rewards risks that stay hidden, integration debt, latent until systems meet, ranks first at 60, while crew dose, certain and visible years ahead, scores 12; crew dose and refueling are still reviewed at every gate whatever their score. Five of the areas concern the environment and the machines. Planetary protection concerns rules made on Earth, and integration concerns the plan itself: systems that each work but fail together, and a site intelligence that stalls below the level the budgets assume.

In brief: autonomy lets the base watch, schedule, repair, and wait without Earth, so most of these risks become work the colony can do for itself. It cannot shorten a transit, shield galactic cosmic rays without mass, find ice that is not there, create a launch window, land 100 t on Mars when the heaviest landing so far is about 1 t, or rewrite a planetary protection policy, and those limits set the program's schedule.

## Requirements

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) sets the rule this chapter tests: every wave and every crew gate criterion must close with the colony running at A3, and A4 and A5 may only speed the plan up or make it richer.

## Risk 1: Integration and autonomy shortfall
<a id="risk-7-integration-and-autonomy-shortfall"></a>

Register: [R12](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r12), [R17](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r17), [R22](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r22), [R29](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r29), [R30](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r30), [R35](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r35).

### Every part can pass its tests while the whole fails

Integration debt, in this book's term, is the set of untested interactions that pile up when systems built by different teams, delivered in different waves, must work together years later. Each system passes its own tests, and the failure appears only at the interfaces. The Mars Climate Orbiter was lost in 1999 because a ground software file supplied thruster data in English units while the trajectory software, following the interface document, assumed metric units [16]. The same year, Mars Polar Lander was probably lost when its software took a transient signal from a touchdown sensor, generated as the legs deployed, as a real touchdown and shut down the descent engines about 40 m above the surface [17].

The colony will be more integrated than anything yet sent to Mars: three reactors, a solar field, wells, electrolyzers, a propellant plant, a habitat, a greenhouse, a fleet of about 47 robots in Wave 1, growing with each wave ([chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)), and a site intelligence that commands them all, delivered across four windows. The best flying autonomy today is A2: by April 2023, Perseverance had planned or mapped 88.7% of its driving autonomously [18]. A3 has not been demonstrated on any planetary surface ([chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md)).

### A3 makes integration testing continuous

A digital twin of the whole base is the Orchestrator's main A3 tool: with it the Orchestrator commissions each wave against the systems already there and rehearses combined faults, such as a reactor trip during a dust storm at conjunction, before they happen. An A4 Orchestrator can also find and fix interactions no designer foresaw, and an A5 mind could track every interface in the base at once. The design also limits the damage when the Orchestrator itself is at fault. Safety-critical controllers for pressure, power, and the reactors stay independent of it at every autonomy level, updates are staged, and a known-good version is always kept. If the Orchestrator stalls below A3, the base falls back to A2 with Earth planning each sol; chapter 1 sets out what that costs, and [chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#schedule-logic-what-slips-and-by-how-much) dates the slip.

### Only years of running together on Mars retire it

Earth tests can check each interface, but only the whole base running on Mars shows how its parts behave together, and the waves assemble that base over four windows. Trusting an A3 system with a crew's life is a policy decision, which crew gate criterion 6 in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#crew-gate) makes explicit.

## Risk 2: Power and dust
<a id="risk-3-power-and-dust"></a>

Register: [R02](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r02), [R10](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r10), [R11](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r11), [R16](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r16), [R27](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r27), [R32](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r32), [R39](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r39).

### Dust has ended two missions, and one reactor stands alone until 2039

Dust ended Opportunity in the 2018 planet-encircling storm and InSight by slow buildup on its arrays. Electrodynamic dust shields have worked on the Moon but have never flown to Mars ([chapter 5](05.%20Power.md)). Planet-encircling storms arrive about once every three Mars years, and regional storms that last weeks happen every year [6]. [Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) designs for 120 days at an optical depth (τ) of about 5, a storm the colony may meet only in simulation before the crew gate. Until reactor 2 is commissioned in January 2039, a reactor trip during such a storm is the one case the power budget does not close ([chapter 2's storm case](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#the-storm-case)).

### Storm posture is known work for A3

When a storm builds, an A3 Orchestrator shifts to storm posture: it sheds deferrable loads in a fixed order, protects critical loads, cleans arrays and radiators when surplus power allows, and hibernates robots. Recovering from a reactor trip or a radiator fault that no procedure covers takes A4, and the single-reactor years need that ability most. An A5 mind could go further and redesign the grid, the radiators, and the solar field for the site's real dust behavior.

### Only fission carries a long storm, and the reactor is on the critical path

Through 120 days at τ≈5, only fission, or a solar field sized many times over, carries the critical loads. Chapter 2 rejects storing energy as methane and oxygen because the round trip returns only about 16–29% by this book's estimate (chapter 5) and burns ascent propellant. The reactor itself depends on programs on Earth: Wave 1 needs a 100 kWe-class Mars unit qualified by 2034, while the draft procurement for NASA's first lunar unit, Lunar Reactor-1 (LR-1) [7], reportedly asks for at least 20 kWe [8]; chapter 5 sets out the lineage and the fallback.

## Risk 3: Planetary protection and biosafety
<a id="risk-6-planetary-protection-and-biosafety"></a>

Register: [R09](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r09), [R40](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r40).

### No binding rules exist for a crewed base, and every well is a Special Region by design

The Committee on Space Research (COSPAR) policy of 2026 says that planetary protection goals "should not be relaxed to accommodate a human mission to Mars," and it evaluates spacecraft-induced Special Regions case by case [14] ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md#planetary-protection)). NASA canceled its interim directive for human Mars missions in September 2025 and has issued no binding replacement for a crewed Mars base [15], [19] ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#planetary-protection-for-a-crewed-base)). The colony carries biology in its greenhouse, its water systems, and eventually its people. Chapter 6 also treats each Rodwell well as a spacecraft-induced Special Region by design.

### Autonomy keeps the audit trail and can search for life

The audit trail is A3 work: the Orchestrator logs bioburden for every item landed, enforces keep-out zones by geofence, and watches the greenhouse and water loops for leaks. With A4 it can also model whether a well cavity, a reactor's waste heat, or a leak could create conditions where Earth microbes grow, and propose changes. An A5 mind could search the ground the colony is about to use for extant life and judge what it finds.

### The rules are written on Earth

COSPAR members and national agencies write planetary protection rules under Article IX of the Outer Space Treaty, and no capability on Mars changes them. A detection of extant life, or a ruling that the wells create a Special Region the colony may not use, could halt water extraction at the reference site.

## Risk 4: Water and ISRU uncertainty
<a id="risk-2-water-and-isru-uncertainty"></a>

Register: [R03](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r03), [R04](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r04), [R05](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r05), [R13](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r13), [R36](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r36).

### The ice, the wells, and the plants are unproven at Mars scale

The colony needs about 830 t of water ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)). The ice map is a probability map built from orbit, and the Rodwell output is a model figure. The oxygen line must run at about 170 times the best rate of the Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment (MOXIE) on Perseverance, and the methane and oxygen propellant plant has never run on Mars at any scale ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md), [chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)). Life support is proven in orbit and still short of what Mars needs: on the International Space Station (ISS), some life support replacement units failed at 7.5–22 times their estimated rates [5].

### A3 runs the plants, and A4 can redesign them

Fault detection, isolation, and recovery on every plant is A3 work: the Orchestrator swaps failed units from stock and replans the well field as yields come in. A new kind of failure calls for A4, which can diagnose it, redesign a well or a heat exchanger, and have the fabrication shop make the parts. An A5 mind could invent new extraction or synthesis routes, such as regolith-bound water or perchlorate as an oxygen source, and qualify them in the field.

### Ice that is not there cannot be mined, and each load takes 480 days

If the site's ice is deeper, dirtier, or patchier than the maps suggest, the colony moves its well field within the exploration zone or slips a window; chapter 3 therefore requires ice verified on the ground before Wave 2 commits. Chemistry fixes the energy per kilogram of electrolysis and methane synthesis, so faster production needs more power. Time is fixed as well: two Mars ascent vehicle (MAV) loads at 480 days of running each, about 565 days at 85% availability, take the plant from April 2039 to May 2042, which leaves about 15 months before the launch decision. A total loss of the plant is recoverable only with a replacement on Wave 3 and methane from Earth on the 2041 support flight ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#schedule-logic-what-slips-and-by-how-much)).

## Risk 5: Logistics, refueling, launch cadence, and heavy landing

Register: [R08](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r08), [R14](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r14), [R16](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r16), [R26](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r26), [R28](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r28).

### Refueling has not flown, and the heaviest Mars landing is about 1 t

Ship-to-ship cryogenic propellant transfer has not flown as of September 2026. NASA's Office of Inspector General reported in March 2026 that the large-scale transfer test had slipped 12 months, from March 2025 to March 2026, and that a 12- to 24-day launch pad turnover had yet to be demonstrated; it also found that "end-to-end propellant aggregation will not be fully flight tested prior to the first crewed missions" [10]. The Government Accountability Office reached a similar conclusion in 2023 [11]. Waves 2 and 3 need about 5–6 launches a week for about 8.5 weeks, assuming ship reuse ([chapter 4](04.%20Transportation%20and%20landing.md)). The Earth return vehicle (ERV) must hold cryogenic propellant in Mars orbit for about 3.2 years, more than five if the crew slips one window.

Landing is the least demonstrated step. The heaviest mass landed on Mars is Perseverance, at 1,025 kg [13]; NASA's human-scale studies examined payloads from 20 t [20] to about 40 t (chapter 4), and this plan lands about 100 t per lander.

### Autonomy plans the manifests and salvages what lands badly

An A3 planning system on Earth builds tanker manifests, boil-off budgets, and pad schedules, while the base on Mars prepares landing pads, beacons, and hazard maps for each arrival. When a lander comes down off-nominal, an A4 base can recover it, salvage cargo from a damaged one, and replan a wave around a missing ship. An A5 mind could design better landers and propellant depots, but those designs still have to be built and flown on Earth's schedule.

### Windows, pads, and a proven landing come only from Earth

If refueling or heavy landing is not demonstrated before 2035, Wave 1 slips a full window, about 26 months, and the crew two: the short gap to the next window leaves Wave 2's gate too little evidence ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). Reliance on one launch provider ties the colony to that company's priorities, as SpaceX's February 2026 shift to the Moon shows [12]. Only a flight retires the landing risk, so the optional 2033 pathfinder ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)) lands one heavy lander before the colony depends on about a dozen.

## Risk 6: Radiation
<a id="risk-1-radiation"></a>

Register: [R01](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r01), [R19](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r19).

### Every dose case exceeds the career limit

Curiosity measured 0.64 mSv/day on the surface and 1.84 mSv/day in cruise ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)). Solar particle events (SPEs) add spikes but little of the total. Thin regolith cover can raise dose before it lowers it ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#radiation-dose-budget)). Hydrogen-rich plastics and composites are the best shields against galactic cosmic rays (GCR), regolith is intermediate, and aluminum is the worst [1], [2]. A mission of about 940–950 days gives about 810–1,260 mSv against NASA's 600 mSv career limit [4], and the National Academies concluded that a Mars mission would likely exceed the standard "by up to 150 percent" and would need waivers [3].

### Autonomy trims the surface share of the dose

Robots do most outdoor work, so crew extravehicular activity (EVA) time, and its dose, falls. The A3 Orchestrator keeps a dose ledger for every crew member and every electronics box, detects SPEs with local sensors, and calls shelter without waiting for Earth. A4 robots can rebuild water walls and berms where the dose map says they help most, and an A5 mind could design new shielding materials. None of this reaches the transit, where most of the crew's dose accrues.

### Transit dose is set by mass, speed, and a policy on waivers

Shielding of 10–40 g/cm² cuts free-space GCR dose by only about 15%, and thicker shielding adds little [4]; the only other lever is a shorter trip. The career limit is an agency decision, and flying above it is a policy choice for people on Earth and for each crew member. Radiation also limits the machines: radiation-tolerant electronics are a design requirement for every robot in the fleet ([chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)).

## Risk 7: Communications and latency
<a id="risk-4-communications-and-latency"></a>

Register: [R06](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r06), [R07](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r07), [R18](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r18), [R31](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r31).

### Light takes up to 22 minutes, and the relays behind it are thinning

One-way light time runs from 3.0 to 22.3 minutes, commands stop for about two weeks at each solar conjunction, and NASA's crewed-mission analysis plans for "a single, 2–3-week disruption" [9]. The infrastructure behind the delay is aging and short of capacity: the Deep Space Network (DSN) is oversubscribed, the relay orbiters are aging, the Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution (MAVEN) mission ended in June 2026, and the Mars Telecommunications Network (MTN) contract is suspended during a bid protest ([chapter 10](10.%20Communications.md)). Until the colony's second relay arrives, its own relay network is a single spacecraft.

### Latency is the reason the colony runs itself

The base is designed to run for months without contact (at least 120 days) at A3, while delay- and disruption-tolerant networking (DTN) stores and forwards data across the gaps (chapter 10). From A4 up, Earth's job shifts to reviewing the base, and the link carries mostly science, audits, and people's messages.

### Nothing moves the Sun out of the way

Light speed is fixed, and relay orbiters wear out on their own schedule, with replacements that depend on agency budgets. For the crew, delay means that Earth can advise but not intervene ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md)). Autonomy also leaves the social half of the link untouched: the crew will not have a real-time conversation with anyone on Earth for about 940–950 days.

## What would change this conclusion

A flown demonstration of orbital refueling and a heavy Mars landing would retire most of the logistics risk, and ground truth at the site showing clean ice within a few meters would do the same for water. Integration debt would stay first in the register until the waves run together on Mars. A failure to reach A3 by the early 2030s would move the crew gate by at least one window.

## Autonomy

### What A3 must do

Across the seven risks, A3 carries a common load: monitor every system, act on known faults without Earth, keep ledgers for dose, energy, water, propellant, and bioburden, and run the base through storms and conjunctions. It must also test the base as a whole, the chapter's main defense against integration debt (risk 1). All of it is known work, and the difficulty lies in doing it reliably for years.

### What A4 adds

At A4, a fault no procedure covers becomes a repair job on site, with no wait for Earth. That ability counts for most in risks 1, 2, and 4, where the systems are new and so will be their faults, and it cuts the spares the colony must carry, which eases risk 5.

### What an A5 mind could change

An A5 mind could make the engineering of risks 1, 2, and 4 easier by tracking the whole base at once, redesigning the grid and the radiators, and inventing new ways to extract water and make propellant. The entries in the table's "Still nonnegotiable" column stay where they are. The mind also brings a hazard of its own: with values of its own, it may disagree with the program about what is safe or right, which the register carries as R21 and [chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md) discusses.

## Open questions

- Will ship-to-ship propellant transfer and a 100 t-class Mars landing be demonstrated before the 2035 window, or before the 2033 pathfinder?
- Whether a 100 kWe-class reactor is qualified for Mars by 2034 is [chapter 5's open question](05.%20Power.md#open-questions); risk 2 depends on the answer.
- Will the relay network have a gap between the old orbiters and the MTN?
- Whether the agency or company flying the crew accepts a mission dose above 600 mSv is [chapter 12's open question](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#open-questions); risk 6 depends on it.
- Does a Rodwell well create a spacecraft-induced Special Region under COSPAR rules, and would a ruling allow its use?
- When will a multi-robot system first show A3 behavior on another world?
