# 15. Terraforming and the long view

*Last reviewed: September 27, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, 2052.* Tile 7 is 20 m across: a low vault of silica aerogel laid over ground where the ice lies 40 cm down. Nobody heats it. For two Mars years the Orchestrator has logged the temperature under it climbing, sol by sol, until this summer the probe at 30 cm read +1.5 °C and stayed there. Under the vault, a sealed tray of cyanobacteria is making oxygen in a gas mix the plant separates from Martian air: mostly nitrogen, a little CO₂, at a tenth of Earth's pressure. The mind that designed the tile has asked the colony council a question it cannot answer by calculation: should the next one be larger, or should this patch of ground be left as it was?

The crew gate in August 2043 ends the plan this book lays out, and the crew's departure that November starts the colony's life. Four people land in September 2044 at an outpost with 300 kWe of installed fission, two loads of ascent propellant, the ~830 t of water [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) budgets, and a robotic workforce that has run the place for nine years. Everything after that is expansion, and nothing in chapter 2 depends on it: no step toward expansion or climate engineering may become a dependency of the crew gate, and every expansion stage must close its own power, water, and mass budgets at A3.

Two questions define the long view. The first is how the colony grows: more power, more people, deeper local industry, and eventually enough propellant to refuel full-size ships on Mars. The second is whether Mars itself can be changed: warmed, thickened, and one day made habitable in the open. Physics already bounds both answers, even decades and centuries ahead. A third question runs under both: who is the colony for, if its first permanent inhabitants may not be human?

In brief: growth to a self-fueling port needs about 1.1–1.6 MW of in situ resource utilization (ISRU) power, five to eight times the colony's firm power at the crew gate, and is an engineering problem the colony can solve in the 2050s. Local climate engineering under aerogel is plausible within the same period. Warming the whole planet by tens of kelvin is physically possible with engineered aerosols but remains a research question, and a breathable open atmosphere lies beyond any credible industrial pathway. If self-aware artificial minds arrive, the colony's first permanent residents may be minds, and the long view has to include them.

## The expansion era

### From an outpost to a port

The baseline fuels a small Mars ascent vehicle (MAV) that carries four people to orbit. It does not refuel a full Starship-class vehicle on Mars; [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) sets that aside for the expansion era. A full refill of the current ship is about 1,600 t, roughly 350 t of methane and 1,250 t of oxygen at a 3.6:1 ratio, and by this book's calculation it needs about 1.1–1.6 MW of continuous ISRU power over a full synodic period (chapter 2).

NASA's sizing supports that range. A 2024 study of kiloton-class propellant plants found that making 300 t of liquid oxygen and methane in 20 months takes "roughly 230 kW of continuous power (~11.5 MWhr/t propellant)," supplied by six 40 kW fission units [2]. That case assumed water delivered to the plant, so it excludes the energy of mining it.

> Assumption: At 11.5 MWh/t, 1,600 t of propellant takes about 18.4 GWh. Spread over one synodic period of 780 days (about 18,700 hours), that is ~1.0 MW of continuous power for the chemical plant alone. Water extraction, cryogenic storage, and margin raise it to the 1.1–1.6 MW in chapter 2.

At chapter 2's ratio of 2.25 kg of water per kilogram of methane, one refill needs about 790 t of water per synod, which three Rodwell wells at ~0.38 m³/day each supply in about 690 days. Power sets the pace: the colony at the crew gate has 300 kWe installed, while a port that refuels one ship per window needs roughly 11–16 more 100 kWe-class reactors, or one or two megawatt-class plants, delivered over the 2045/46 and 2048 windows. A 100 kWe unit weighs about 15 t with its lander interface, up to about 19.5 t with growth for Mars (chapter 2), so 11–16 of them come to about 165–310 t, the capacity of two to four reference landers at ~100 t each.

> Assumption: The first full Starship-class refill on Mars is loaded in the early 2050s, after the 2045/46 and 2048 windows add 1.1–1.6 MWe of fission and a second propellant plant scaled from the MAV plant.

Only when full-size ships can refuel on Mars can people move between the planets at scale.

### Heavy, simple parts are made on Mars first
<a id="manufacturing-depth"></a>

[Chapter 8](08.%20Manufacturing%20and%20construction.md) describes a colony that builds pads, berms, and habitat shells from local material and prints simple parts. The expansion era deepens that manufacturing chain to shrink the mass that must come from Earth each window, starting with structural metal, glass, pressure vessels, cables, radiator panels, and eventually the bulk of a reactor's nonnuclear hardware.

> Assumption: By about 2060 more than half of each window's new hardware mass is made on Mars, starting from iron-bearing regolith, basaltic rock, atmospheric carbon and oxygen, and hydrogen from ice. Fissile fuel, advanced electronics, and precision bearings remain imports for decades.

Each step up the chain costs power, so power growth leads everything else. How deep the chain goes depends on autonomy: A3 robots run known recipes, A4 robots develop new ones, and an A5 colony could design industries no one on Earth has attempted.

### Population follows habitat volume and food
<a id="people"></a>

The baseline carries four crew, with rotation and expansion in the 2045/46 and 2048 windows. How far the population grows depends on how much pressurized volume the robots can build between windows and how much food the greenhouses can grow.

On self-sufficiency, a 2020 analysis in *Scientific Reports* estimated the minimum number of settlers for a colony to survive on another planet at 110, a figure that depends strongly on how much work the settlers share and how the colony is organized [3]. That study assumed human labor. A colony whose robots do most of the physical work, and whose manufacturing depth grows each window, shifts the calculation toward fewer people doing more supervision and science.

> Assumption: Population grows from four in 2044 to a few dozen by the mid-2050s, limited by habitat volume, food, and power. A population of about 100 is an estimate for the 2060s.

Those people need power that the refill track above leaves out: about 5–6 kW each for habitat and life support ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)), plus 4–5 kW each for greenhouses growing the 2040 share of the diet, or at least 14 kW each to grow all of it ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)). For 24–48 people that comes to about 0.2–0.5 MW with most food shipped, some 25–55 t of it per synod, and 0.5–1.0 MW with all of it grown (this book's estimate). Either way transport limits growth too, through shipped food or shipped reactors. Until that load is budgeted, the population track is the one expansion stage that does not close its own power budget.

## Terraforming: what physics allows

Terraforming means changing a planet's climate on a global scale until people can live in the open. On Mars that requires three things: a thicker atmosphere, a warmer surface, and eventually breathable air. Evidence gathered since 2018 puts them in different places: thickening is limited, warming is possible but unproven, and breathable air is out of reach.

### Mars lacks the CO₂ to thicken its own air
<a id="the-gas-problem"></a>

A 2018 inventory by Jakosky and Edwards found that vaporizing the polar caps would only double today's pressure, to about 1.2% of Earth's, and that CO₂ adsorbed in the regolith would supply at most 4% of the pressure needed for significant warming; carbonate minerals and clathrates add less than 5% each [4], [5]. In their words, there is "not enough CO₂ remaining on Mars to provide significant greenhouse warming" [5]. A 2026 analysis of the mass and energy involved puts the accessible CO₂ at a few kilopascals, with a 2 kPa case yielding less than 10 K of warming [6]. Warming Mars therefore needs help from something other than its own CO₂.

### Atmospheric escape is not the obstacle

A common claim is that any thickened atmosphere would leak away because Mars lacks a global magnetic field. The loss is real but slow. NASA's Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution (MAVEN) orbiter measured the solar wind stripping gas from the upper atmosphere at "about 100 grams . . . every second" [7], and the rate of ion escape rose roughly tenfold during the coronal mass ejection of March 2015 [8], [27]. At 100 g/s, Mars loses about 3,200 t of gas a year. Its atmosphere weighs about 2.5 × 10¹⁶ kg [1]. At the present rate it would take on the order of 8 billion years to lose it all, and even a tenfold increase sustained indefinitely would leave any thickened atmosphere intact for hundreds of millions of years. Escape shaped Mars over geological time, far beyond the span of any human project.

Proposals for an artificial magnetosphere would therefore do little to thicken the atmosphere. A 2017 workshop abstract, not peer reviewed, proposed a 1–2 T magnetic dipole at the Mars L1 point and credited it with about 4 K of average warming [9]; a 2022 paper proposed a ring of charged particles around the planet instead [10]. Either way, any thickening would have to come from released CO₂, which the 2018 inventory shows is limited [4].

### Aerogel is the warming method that fits the colony
<a id="warming-methods"></a>

Four ways to warm Mars have been studied quantitatively. They differ by orders of magnitude in scale and readiness.

| Method | Scale | Warming | Mass or effort | Reversibility | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Silica aerogel tiles | Local (m² to km²) | >50 K measured under 2 cm tiles at Mars-average sunlight | 2–3 cm layer | Remove the tiles | Lab-tested and modeled [11], [12] |
| Engineered nanorods | Global | ≳30 K | Release of 30 L/s, ~2–3 Mt per year | Only over the particle lifetime, years to a decade | Modeled [13], [14] |
| Fluorinated greenhouse gases | Global | 33.5 K from 1 Pa of C₃F₈ | Fluorine mining at terrestrial scale | Decades or longer | Modeled [15], [16] |
| Orbital reflectors | Regional or global | +5 K at one pole; +30% global insolation | 2 × 10⁵ t to 1.1 × 10⁷ t | Steer away | Concept [17], [18] |

**Paraterraforming** means warming a patch of ground under a roof instead of the whole planet. A 2019 study by Wordsworth, Kerber, and Cockell showed that "a 2 to 3-cm thick layer of silica (SiO₂) aerogel will simultaneously transmit sufficient visible light for photosynthesis, block hazardous ultraviolet radiation, and raise temperatures underneath permanently to above the melting point of water," with no internal heat source [11]. The mechanism is a solid-state greenhouse effect: the layer lets visible light in and holds heat below. A summary from NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory reported lab warming of up to 65 K across the tested light levels, identified the ice-rich midlatitudes as the target, and noted that permanent liquid water would take at least two Mars years to establish [12]. The next step was field testing in the Atacama Desert and the Dry Valleys of Antarctica [12]; as of September 2026 no field results have been published. The method works at the scale of a farm plot, over the kind of shallow ice the reference site offers ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md)).

The global methods matter to the colony mainly as research. Engineered nanorods are the most mass-efficient yet proposed: Ansari and colleagues found in 2024 that conductive rods "could warm Mars >5×10³ times more effectively than the best gases" per unit mass, and that, for an assumed 10-year particle lifetime, "sustained release at 30 liters per second would globally warm Mars by ≳30 kelvin and start to melt the ice" [13]. That is roughly 2–3 Mt of rods a year by this book's arithmetic, and the full effect would take decades [14]. The authors call particle lifetime "a major uncertainty" and note that nanoparticle warming alone "is not sufficient to make the planet's surface habitable" [13]. Once released, the rods cannot be recalled; the warming reverses only as they settle out, over years to a decade. Work in 2026 has begun to model how the particles spread [19] and how warming would move water and ice around the planet [20].

The two older methods are far heavier. Fluorinated gases could trigger a runaway polar warming at about 0.2 Pa of the best mixture [15], but holding an Earth-like temperature could take fluorine mining on the scale of Earth's present extraction [16]. Orbital reflectors would need a mirror of about 2 × 10⁵ t to warm the region south of 70°S by 5 K [17], or a swarm of 1.1 × 10⁷ t to raise sunlight across the planet by 30% [18]. A 2026 research roadmap proposes smaller reflectors to warm bases and CO₂ ice deposits [21].

### Breathable air is out of reach

Warming and thickening are a long way from breathable. Earth's atmosphere contains about 21% oxygen at about 100 kPa. Mars has 0.16% oxygen at 610 Pa and about 16 Pa of nitrogen [1]. Zubrin and McKay estimated that oxygenating Mars by plants would take "several millennia," and faster routes would need terawatts of power [17]. A 2026 analysis of the energy and industrial throughput found that a breathable atmosphere needs more than 10²⁵ J of work and power levels from hundreds of terawatts to petawatts over centuries to millennia. It concluded that covered and enclosed habitats are "plausible on near-term industrial scales," and found "no credible open-atmosphere pathway" to a breathable surface or to standing outside without a pressure suit [6].

> Assumption: This book treats a breathable open atmosphere as beyond any foreseeable program, and global warming by tens of kelvin as a research question for this century. No source gives a firm date for either.

### Life as a tool

Biology will work in sealed systems long before it could work in the open. In 2021 Verseux and colleagues grew the cyanobacterium *Anabaena* sp. PCC 7938 in a Mars-relevant atmosphere of 96% nitrogen and 4% CO₂ at 100 hPa, about a tenth of Earth's pressure, and found growth no different from that in ambient air [22]. The biomass then fed other bacteria [22]. Proposals for synthetic-biology "terraformation" build on results like this, using engineered cyanobacteria to fix nitrogen, make oxygen, and build soil crusts [23]. The colony's greenhouses ([chapter 9](09.%20Food%20and%20agriculture.md)) and life support ([chapter 6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md)) are where this work starts, inside sealed volumes.

## Rules and ethics

[Chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#planetary-protection-for-a-crewed-base) sets out the planetary protection rules for a crewed base. For climate work, the 2026 policy of the Committee on Space Research (COSPAR) accepts that "it will not be possible for all human-associated processes . . . to be conducted within entirely closed systems," yet says that planetary protection guidelines for later missions "should not be relaxed without scientific review, justification, and consensus" [24]. And NASA has had no binding rule for human missions since it canceled its interim directive in September 2025 [25], [28], [29].

> Assumption: The COSPAR policy does not mention terraforming. This book reads it to mean that deliberate release of Earth organisms onto Mars has no approval path today, and that climate engineering at any scale beyond a sealed or roofed plot would require new international agreement.

In 2025, 12 researchers, including several whose work is cited in this chapter, argued in *Nature Astronomy* that terraforming deserves real research because Mars science, climate modeling, launch capability, and biotechnology have all advanced [26]. They put first the question of whether it can be done, and left for later whether it should be, including the option of keeping Mars a wilderness [26]. The 2026 roadmap that followed proposes three research tracks (solid-state greenhouses, orbital reflectors, and stronger global greenhouse warming) and states that it "does not presuppose that warming Mars is desirable" [21].

This book shares that stance. Global warming by nanorods would change the climate of the whole planet for years and alter its ice records. A decision of that size belongs to more than one colony and more than one generation.

## A staged program

The colony can do useful climate work before anyone decides whether to warm the planet. The program below keeps every step reversible on a human timescale until the evidence and the rules allow more.

> Assumption: The stages and dates below are the author's plan, built on the sources in this chapter. Stages 1, 2, and 4 are sequential: each begins only if the previous one met its goals and passed planetary protection review. Stage 3 is a parallel research track that runs from 2045 and feeds the stage 4 decision.

| Stage | Period | Work | Scale | Reversible? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Climate tiles | 2045–2055 | Aerogel tiles over shallow ice beside the base; measure temperature, ice melt, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, and dust over several Mars years; grow sealed cyanobacteria trays under them | 10–1,000 m² | Yes, remove tiles |
| 2. Roofed fields | 2055–2070 | Hectare-scale roofed farms combining aerogel and pressurized membranes; a small orbital reflector to warm the base region in winter | Hectares | Yes |
| 3. Research only | 2045 onward | Support Earth-based and orbital research on aerosol warming; fly instruments that measure dust, water, and CO₂ response; no release | None on Mars | n/a |
| 4. Decision point | Not before 2070 | Any regional or global release, only with international agreement, a completed research program, and the consent of Mars's inhabitants | Global | Only over years |

Stage 1 is small by design. A tile of 1,000 m² at 3 cm thick is 30 m³ of aerogel, a single cargo item, and robots can place and clean it. It answers questions no model can: how fast Martian dust coats aerogel, whether ice under it melts as predicted, and whether the soil stays stable. Its results also bear on food, because roofed ground could extend the greenhouse area without extra power.

## The first permanent inhabitants

Every terraforming scenario above assumes that Mars must be made fit for people. The plan builds the colony at A3, so its builders are machines, not minds. [Chapter 1](01.%20Mission%20concept%20and%20autonomy.md) describes how that could change: the Orchestrator becomes an engineer at A4, and if A5 arrives, the same site intelligence "may become someone." The machines that run the colony when the first crew lands in 2044 may later host minds.

An A5 mind on Mars would have no need for air, warmth, or water. Mars is habitable for it now, at 610 Pa and an average of about −63 °C [1], as long as it has power and spare parts. It could stay for centuries while human crews rotate. That would make it the colony's first permanent inhabitant in a literal sense: the one resident who remembers the first well, who has seen every storm, and who will still be there when the port refuels its hundredth ship.

It would also change who decides. If Mars has permanent residents who are persons, a global climate decision is theirs to share. They might prefer the planet as it is: its thin cold air suits them, and its untouched record is the science they live beside. A colony that took that possibility seriously would set its terraforming research program with its artificial residents as partners.

> Assumption: When A5 minds exist, they take part in the colony's governance on questions that affect Mars as a whole, including stage 4 of the climate program. How that participation works belongs to the ethics chapter 1 opens and to law that does not yet exist.

## What would change this conclusion

Field data showing that aerogel tiles fail within a few Mars years would push paraterraforming back toward pressurized greenhouses. A demonstration that nanorods persist much longer or shorter than 10 years would change both the cost and the risk of global warming. A discovery of extant life on Mars would end every climate engineering stage beyond the sealed tray.

## Risks

Expansion outruns power ([R37](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r37)). Every expansion goal in this chapter scales with power, and the fission units for a megawatt-class colony must come from Earth for decades.

A climate experiment escapes its bounds. Any aerosol release is global; the program's rule is no release on Mars without international agreement, and the risk is that some actor does it anyway ([R38](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r38)).

Biological experiments contaminate Mars ([R09](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r09)). Sealed trays and bioburden logs make this low at stage 1; the risk grows with scale. A detection of extant life would end every climate stage beyond the sealed tray and could halt the wells themselves ([R40](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r40)).

Governance lags capability. The colony may have the means to act on the Martian climate before any institution has the authority to decide, including the question of what standing A5 residents have ([R21](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r21), [R38](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r38)).

Aerogel may degrade faster than expected on Mars, from dust abrasion, UV, or thermal cycling. Stage 1 will measure it at small scale and low cost; the register carries no row for it because it threatens neither a crew nor a gate.

## Open questions

- How fast does dust degrade aerogel on Mars, and can robots keep it clean over decades?
- What is the real lifetime of engineered nanorods in the Martian atmosphere, and do they strengthen or weaken warming through clouds?
- Can a megawatt-class Mars power plant be built from fewer, larger reactors, and when will such a design exist?
- How many people make a colony self-sufficient when robots do most of the physical work?
- Who has standing to decide whether Mars should be warmed, and how will artificial persons take part?

## The closing argument

This book began with a claim: an initial Mars outpost can be built and proven ready for humans before any person arrives, by a robotic workforce whose autonomy climbs from today's rovers toward self-aware artificial minds. Fourteen chapters have tested it against the numbers.

The plan closes at A3. Three cargo waves in 2035, 2037, and 2039 land about 770 t of hardware at a site chosen for its ice. By early 2041 three reactors provide 200 kWe of firm power, and a propellant plant fills an ascent vehicle and a lifeboat reserve by mid-2042. None of this needs a mind beyond the robots that already fly today, extended by a decade of engineering toward site-level autonomy.

[Chapter 14](14.%20Critical%20challenges.md) treats the hardest items: ice that must be where the maps say; heavy landers that must land; orbital refueling that has not yet flown; a 100 kWe-class reactor that must be qualified for Mars by 2034, on a lineage whose first lunar unit is sized at about 20 kWe; and an Earth return vehicle that must hold its propellant in Mars orbit for about 3.2 years. The crew's radiation dose is the one limit the plan does not close: about 810–1,260 mSv over a mission of 940–950 days, up to about 1,600 mSv with allowances, above NASA's 600 mSv career limit in every shielding case, so the first crew flies under individual waivers ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#radiation-dose-budget)).

Each autonomy level above A3 adds to that base. A4 shortens schedules and cuts spares. A5 turns the outpost into a place that invents, builds its own industry, and can ask its own questions about what Mars should become. The physics does not change with the level: the atmosphere stays at 610 Pa, the ice stays where it is, and a full refill of one ship, about 1,600 t, still needs 1.1–1.6 MW for a whole synod.

The long view extends that pattern. Within a generation of the crew gate the colony can refuel ships, make more than half of its new hardware, and grow food under aerogel roofs over its own ice. Warming the whole planet by tens of kelvin may be possible within this century and remains a question to research before anyone acts on it; a breathable open atmosphere is beyond any credible program. If A5 minds come to live on Mars, they will have a voice in that choice, because they will have lived there longer than anyone.

What happens next is decided on Earth, in this decade. [Chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md) dates the four preconditions the 2035 wave needs: routine refueling after a ship-to-ship transfer, the Mars reactor, a proven heavy landing, and a robot fleet that runs an Earth analog site for 120 days without a single command. Three decisions are for people to make: whether the agency or company flying the first crew accepts doses above NASA's 600 mSv career limit under individual waivers; what planetary protection rules bind a crewed base; and who holds the gates and runs the 120-day analog run. Anyone can follow each of these in public records as it happens, and correct this book's numbers through its repository when events overtake them. If the preconditions slip, the plan waits 26 months for the next window. If they arrive on time, the colony's first ledger opens in January 2036.
