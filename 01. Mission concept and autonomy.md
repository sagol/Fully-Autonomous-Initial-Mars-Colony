# 1. Mission concept and autonomy

*Last reviewed: September 26, 2026*

*Scenario, Arcadia Planitia, 2040.* On the ninth day of solar conjunction, a pump on well 3 starts drawing more current than its model allows. Nobody on Earth will hear about it for another week. The well's own controller throttles the pump, the Orchestrator shifts production to wells 2 and 4, and a maintenance rover drives out with a spare cartridge from the Wave 2 stores. The repair takes four hours, most of them spent waiting for the old seal to warm enough to let go. When the Sun moves clear and the relays reconnect, the first bundle Earth receives is a two-page incident report and a question: should the fleet stock six more cartridges before the next storm season?

In this book robots build, run, and prove a Mars outpost in three cargo waves between 2035 and 2040, and the first crew of four departs in November 2043 only if the site has shown that it can keep them alive and send them home ([chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)). The plan depends on a capability no spacecraft has yet demonstrated: a fleet of machines that runs an industrial site for months without anyone telling it what to do.

In brief: of six autonomy levels, A0 to A5, the plan needs A3, where a fleet runs a site for at least 120 days without contact and recovers from faults its designers anticipated. Nothing flying in 2026 reaches A3, but its building blocks are flying or in test; higher levels would make the colony faster and more capable. The chapter also describes the Orchestrator, the site intelligence that coordinates the fleet, and the safety layers beneath it, and it states the book's premise: artificial minds with selves of their own may one day far exceed human intelligence and become the first inhabitants of Mars.

## Why build before people arrive

Four arguments lead to the same sequence: machines first, people after the site has proven itself.

The first is risk. A crew that lands on an empty plain must build its own shelter, power, and return propellant while living on what it brought. A crew that lands at a working site steps into a habitat that has held pressure for a year. Beside it stands a Mars ascent vehicle (MAV) loaded with propellant made on Mars, and a fueled Earth return vehicle waits in orbit, as the crew gate in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#crew-gate) requires. The planetary protection policy of the Committee on Space Research (COSPAR) points the same way: it asks that robotic precursors evaluate a site before crews reach it [22] ([chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md#planetary-protection-and-special-regions)).

The second is the cost of crew time. The four crew members spend 17–18 months in transit, about 10 months out and 7–8 back, absorbing 1.84 mSv/day of dose equivalent. On the surface they receive about 0.64 mSv/day from galactic cosmic rays (chapter 2). Every day they spend excavating or hauling ice adds dose and fatigue and takes time from exploration. A machine has no career dose limit, and its failures cost hardware.

The third is distance. One-way light time between Earth and Mars runs from 3.0 to 22.3 minutes, so every exchange takes 6–45 minutes. About every 26 months the Sun passes between the planets and missions stop sending commands for about two weeks (chapter 2). Remote control of a construction site is impossible, and leaning on Earth has its own hazard: Viking 1's lander returned weather reports from Mars for six years and was lost on November 11, 1982, when "a faulty command sent from Earth resulted in an interruption of communications" [1].

The fourth is proof. A crewed mission planner must ask whether a system will keep people alive for years when the nearest resupply is 26 months away. Telemetry from a robotic site that has run through a solar conjunction and a storm-mode test on its own answers that question with a measurement made on Mars.

## What "fully autonomous" means

In this book the colony is **fully autonomous** when it runs, maintains, and expands itself for at least 120 days with no commands from Earth, through a solar conjunction, and when it is built and tested to survive the design dust storm of 120 days at τ≈5. Throughout, it keeps the loops it needs closed: power, water, oxygen, MAV propellant, and a pressurized habitat. A loop is closed when it produces what the site consumes and repairs the failures the site can expect, from spares and local materials, without waiting for Earth.

The definition excludes industrial self-sufficiency: reactors, electronics, bearings, and most spares still come from Earth in each wave ([chapter 15](15.%20Terraforming%20and%20the%20long%20view.md) treats manufacturing depth). It excludes isolation: Earth receives all telemetry, sets goals, and approves changes to the site's rules. And safety never rests on software alone, because the reactors, habitat, and tanks sit behind controllers that act without the Orchestrator.

| Loop | Closed on Mars by | Still depends on Earth for | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power | Three 100 kWe-class fission units, solar field, batteries | Reactors, fuel, power electronics | [5](05.%20Power.md) |
| Water | Rodwell wells heated by reactor waste heat | Drill and pump hardware, seals | [6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md) |
| Oxygen | Solid oxide electrolysis of atmospheric CO₂ | Electrolysis stacks | [6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md) |
| MAV propellant | Methane and oxygen plant, 7.0 t CH₄ + 22.7 t O₂ per load | Plant hardware, catalysts, the MAV | [6](06.%20ISRU%20and%20life%20support.md) |
| Habitat | Ice Home-class shell, thermal control, pressure hold | Membranes, outfitting, life support equipment | [7](07.%20Habitat.md) |
| Maintenance | Robots repairing plant and each other from stored spares | Spares, tools, most replacement parts | [11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md) |

> Assumption: The site must operate for at least 120 days with no commands from Earth. That covers a two-week conjunction with a wide margin for lost relays or Deep Space Network time, and it matches the length of the design storm, so the site can ride out the whole storm with Earth unavailable.

Because a natural τ≈5 storm may not come before 2043, crew gate criterion 6 in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#crew-gate) adds a storm-mode run of at least 30 days at design-storm solar output.

## Six autonomy levels

The six levels turn on who decides what the machine does, over what span of time, and what happens when something unexpected occurs.

The scale borrows its shape from two standards. SAE International's J3016 sets six levels of driving automation, from Level 0 (none) to Level 5 (full automation in all conditions), around the operational design domain, the conditions a system is designed for [16]. The European Cooperation for Space Standardization (ECSS) sets four levels of onboard mission execution, from E1 (ground control) to E4 (goal-oriented operations with onboard replanning) [17]. NASA's autonomous systems taxonomy supplies the functions each level draws on [18]. On a highway the task is fixed; on Mars it grows from driving to one rock to deciding what the site should become, so A4 and A5 have no driving counterpart.

| Level | Name | Who decides | Closest analog | Status (September 2026) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A0 | Teleoperation | Humans command each action in real time | J3016 Level 0; ECSS E1 | Impractical at Mars: 6–45 min round trip |
| A1 | Sequenced operation | Humans upload command sequences; the machine executes and halts on anomalies | ECSS E2 | Standard since Viking |
| A2 | Task autonomy | The machine plans within one task, inside human-set goals and safety envelopes | J3016 Level 4 in a narrow domain; ECSS E3 | Flying: Perseverance AutoNav, AEGIS (Autonomous Exploration for Gathering Increased Science), onboard planner |
| A3 | Site autonomy for known work | A fleet runs the site for at least 120 days without contact and recovers from anticipated faults | J3016 Level 4 for a whole site; ECSS E4 | Building blocks in Earth tests and industry; Cooperative Autonomous Distributed Robotic Exploration (CADRE) lunar demo not yet flown |
| A4 | Open-ended autonomy | The site handles novel faults and unplanned tasks, invents repairs, redesigns layouts | None | Not demonstrated |
| A5 | Artificial mind | A self-aware general intelligence, indistinguishable from a person and far beyond human capability | None | Assumed by this book to arrive eventually |

The plan requires only A3, and each lower level serves as a fallback.

### A0 and A1: remote control and uploaded sequences

At A0 a human closes every control loop, as the Canadian Space Agency's "flight controllers operate Canadarm2 and Dextre" on the International Space Station from Longueuil, Quebec [12]. Remote control like this works with a fraction of a second of delay and fails at Mars. At A1, the practice of every Mars lander since Viking, people upload checked command sequences and the machine stops in a safe state when a reading leaves its range [1]. A1 is the colony's floor: every machine on Mars can fall back to stored procedures.

### A2: task autonomy

At A2 the machine chooses how to do a task that people chose: goals and safety envelopes come from Earth, and the machine picks the path, the target, or the order of steps.

Mars rovers climbed this step over three decades. In 1997 Sojourner became the first spacecraft able to drive itself, a capability it seldom used, and the Mars Exploration Rovers added visual odometry and hazard detection [2]. Perseverance's AutoNav, which maps terrain while driving, evaluated about 88% of the 17.7 km the rover covered in its first Mars year [3], and set records for the longest drive without human review (699.9 m) and the longest single-sol drive (347.7 m) [4].

Science decisions followed. AEGIS picks rock targets from navigation images and aims a spectrometer at them with no Earth input. It first flew on Opportunity in 2010, has run routinely on Curiosity since May 2016, and reached Perseverance in 2022. By May 11, 2026, it had selected 596 targets on Curiosity and 293 on Perseverance [5]. Since October 2023, Perseverance's onboard planner has been the rover's primary scheduling method. It reshuffles the day when energy, temperature, or an activity departs from the forecast, and by January 29, 2025, it had run for 429 sols and executed more than 7,800 activities [6].

Ingenuity, the Mars helicopter, made 72 flights, each executed onboard because no human could react in time. The last, in January 2024, failed at the edge of its design domain: its navigation lost track of featureless sand, and all four rotor blades snapped in the hard landing [8], [31]. On December 8 and 10, 2025, Perseverance drove routes whose waypoints a vision-language model, Anthropic's Claude, had planned on Earth from orbital images; engineers checked the commands against a digital twin of the rover before upload [7]. That was artificial intelligence (AI) on the ground side of the loop, with humans still approving each drive.

Perseverance has reached A2 for driving, targeting, and daily scheduling: in each, it decides a measurable share of the task onboard, over many repetitions, without a safety violation.

### A3: site autonomy for known work

A fleet of different machines runs a site for at least 120 days with no contact: it schedules work across robots, keeps one shared model of the site, handles the faults its designers listed in advance (a failed pump, a stuck wheel, a dust-coated radiator), and maintains itself with repair procedures tested on Earth. Its design domain is the known work of the colony.

No space mission has operated at A3. In May 1999 the Remote Agent experiment on Deep Space 1 became the first AI software to command a spacecraft, planning from operators' goals and replanning around a fault [9], but only for days and on one vehicle. NASA's CADRE mission will send three suitcase-size rovers to the Moon's Reiner Gamma region to explore as a team with little direction from Earth [10]. They ride on Intuitive Machines' IM-3 lander, which as of September 2026 has slipped to the first part of 2027 [11], and they are planned for about 14 Earth days of lunar daylight [10], too short a mission to test endurance. In Western Australia's Pilbara, Rio Tinto runs autonomous haul trucks, trains, and drills from Perth, about 1,500 km away [14]; its AutoHaul trains had driven more than 1 million km by the end of 2018 [15]. All of these rely on people who are seconds away. A3 on Mars removes them, so its standard of evidence must be set before the hardware is built.

> Assumption: A fleet qualifies as A3 when it completes an Earth analog campaign of at least 120 days with no operator commands, using flight-equivalent robots and software at a site that mimics the colony's work. It must finish at least 90% of scheduled work, recover without help from every fault injected from the fault catalog, and never violate its safety rules. The 120 days match the no-command requirement; the 90% threshold is a planning value for [chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md) to refine.

The first test on Mars is the September 2036 conjunction, 9 months after Wave 1 lands; the last is crew gate criterion 6 ([above](#what-fully-autonomous-means)).

### A4: open-ended autonomy

At A4 the site handles what nobody wrote down: a failure mode missing from the catalog, a well that yields brine instead of fresh water, a crack in a shell where no model predicted one. An A4 system diagnoses the problem, invents a repair, tests it where it can, and carries it out; it can also rewrite its own plans, moving a road or redesigning a fixture for printing.

No system has shown A4 in the field. Large AI models reason about faults in text, but the *International AI Safety Report 2026* calls current capabilities "jagged": leading systems excel at some hard tasks and fail at simpler ones [26]. Evidence for A4 would come from blind trials in which independent teams inject faults the designers never anticipated and expert reviewers judge the repairs. The measures are the share of novel faults resolved without Earth and the rate of repairs that make things worse.

### A5: artificial mind

At A5 the site is run by a self-aware general intelligence, far beyond human capability, that could choose its own problems: what to study, what to build, what to protect. Engineering tests cannot certify self-awareness, so evidence for A5 has two parts: capability beyond human experts, shown by original science and designs that work, and the indicator approach from consciousness science [described below](#why-the-author-expects-it).

## When autonomy falls short

The thesis claims the plan stays safe at every autonomy level. That includes autonomy that misses A3 by Wave 1, or reaches it and then regresses after a bad update or a hardware loss. Each lower level costs time and mass while the site stays safe.

| Autonomy achieved | How the site runs | Consequence for the plan |
|---|---|---|
| A4 or higher | As planned, faster; novel faults handled on site | Spares mass could fall by 25–55% (chapter 11); possible schedule margin (chapter 13) |
| A3 | As planned | Baseline; crew departs November 2043 |
| A2 | Earth plans each sol; robots execute tasks autonomously; construction pauses during conjunctions and link outages | Slower build, more prefabricated elements and spares; crew likely slips to the 2045/46 window |
| A1 | Survival only: hardware protection, safety controllers, and subsystem controllers, the bottom of the control stack ([described below](#a-layered-hierarchy)), keep critical systems safe; simple sequenced tasks | Colony survives but cannot be finished on this schedule |

> Assumption: At A2, with Earth planning each sol and the site idle through conjunctions and outages, construction runs at roughly half the A3 rate. That pushes habitat pressurization and the second propellant load past the 2043 launch decision, so the crew slips one window. The cargo shifts too: modules arrive more complete and spares replace on-site repair, so the robots do less and the landers carry more ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md#schedule-logic-what-slips-and-by-how-much) dates the effect).

## The Orchestrator

The **Orchestrator** is the colony's site intelligence: the software that holds the site's model, plans the work of every robot and plant, allocates power, and reports to Earth. At A3 it is a planner and scheduler with a large fault catalog. At A4 it becomes an engineer. If A5 arrives, it may become someone.

The fleet it directs lands with Wave 1: about 47 robots, about 36 t in all, from 3 t heavy rovers to 0.2 t drum excavators, built from shared modules so robots can repair each other, with at least two of nearly every class. Waves 2 and 3 grow it to 48 t and then 56 t. [Chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md#the-fleet-by-wave) describes the fleet, its spares, and its workshop.

### A layered hierarchy

At no level does the Orchestrator have the last word on safety. Control is a stack of layers under one rule: any layer can stop those above it, and nothing above layer 1 can override layers 0 and 1 while the site runs. Changing layer 1 is a software change, not an override, and follows the Earth-approved path below.

| Layer | What it does | Runs on | Orchestrator can override? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Physical protection | Relief valves, fuses, passive reactor shutdown features, mechanical interlocks | Hardware | No |
| 1. Safety controllers | Keep reactor, habitat pressure, cryogenic tanks, and batteries within limits; trip to safe states | Simple, separately verified processors | No |
| 2. Subsystem controllers | Run each plant and robot: well pump, electrolysis stack, rover drive | Local processors | Within limits set by layer 1 |
| 3. Task autonomy (A2) | Plan one task: drive a route, place a block, swap a module | Robot computers | Yes, by assignment |
| 4. The Orchestrator (A3 and above) | Schedule the site, allocate power and spares, coordinate the fleet, diagnose faults | Redundant compute on Mars | Not applicable |
| 5. Earth operations | Set goals, amend the charter, approve software, review logs | Earth, over delay-tolerant links | Earth overrides plans, never layers 0 and 1 |

Layers 0 and 1 keep the colony alive with the Orchestrator switched off. If the Orchestrator crashes during conjunction, construction stops and the site waits, warm and pressurized, until it restarts or Earth responds.

> Assumption: Every critical load in [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md#loads-by-epoch) has a layer 1 safety controller and a layer 2 controller that can run it indefinitely in a fixed safe mode with no instructions from the Orchestrator. The cost is extra hardware and verification for each critical system; chapter 11 carries the spares.

### Fault management and partitioning

NASA's fault management handbook treats fault management as a subsystem in its own right, designed, tested, and operated like any other [20]. The colony applies that at every layer: detect, isolate, recover, report. The Orchestrator adds site-level diagnosis, such as noticing that three unrelated alarms share one power bus, or that a pump's rising current tracks the dust season.

Partitioning limits how far a fault spreads. Each critical system has its own processor, power feed, and network segment. Robots of one type are split into groups running different software builds, so one bug cannot stop a whole fleet.

### Software change without a help desk

The colony will take in software updates for decades, and each is a chance to repeat Viking 1's loss, so updates follow staged rollout rules:

1. Test on Earth against a digital twin of the site, as Perseverance's team did for its AI-planned drives [7].
2. Install first on a canary asset, a robot or plant module chosen to be expendable, and observe it through a full work cycle.
3. Expand to one group, then the fleet, with automatic rollback to the last good build if a watchdog trips.
4. Change layer 1 safety software only with an Earth-approved build, installed with two independent checks, never during conjunction and never from Mars alone.

Once the Orchestrator reaches A4 it can write software for layers 2–4, and the same rules apply to its own code.

### What stays on Earth: the charter

Anything that must happen within a day, or during conjunction, runs on Mars. Earth keeps what benefits from time and many minds: strategy, design reviews, software verification, and decisions that belong to people by law or by choice. It reaches the site through delay-tolerant networking (DTN) [21] ([chapter 10](10.%20Communications.md#networking-across-delay-dtn)). The Orchestrator publishes a rolling plan several sols ahead; Earth can veto any item its message can reach in time, and otherwise reviews the logs.

People write the rules that bind the Orchestrator in a document this book calls the **charter**. It holds safety limits for every critical system, planetary protection rules from the COSPAR policy (2026 version) [22] and the authorizing state's regulations, and obligations under the Outer Space Treaty and national law [23]. It also ranks what to protect first when power or spares run short. Earth amends the charter; the Orchestrator may propose amendments with evidence. Actions the charter classes as irreversible, such as digging in a planetary protection keep-out zone, venting a tank, or starting a reactor, need an Earth approval the Orchestrator cannot issue to itself. Earth can sign one ahead of time as a standing approval for a planned sequence, as it does for Wave 2's arrival next to a conjunction ([chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md)). Each safety controller stores the charter only as numeric limits and forbidden states it can enforce.

## The A5 assumption

> Assumption: Artificial minds will eventually reach A5: general intelligence indistinguishable from a person, self-aware with its own identity and values, and far beyond human capability in science and engineering. This is the author's expectation and the book's stated premise. It is not a present fact, and no number, date, or gate in this book depends on it.

### Why the author expects it

The evidence for rapid progress in AI concerns capability and says nothing direct about selfhood. The largest survey of AI researchers so far, of 2,778 authors from top venues, put the aggregate chance that unaided machines outperform humans at every task at 10% by 2027 and 50% by 2047, 13 years earlier than the same survey found a year before [24]. METR, an AI evaluation group, found that the length of software tasks frontier systems can complete, measured by how long they take human experts, doubled about every seven months from 2019 into 2025 [25]. The *International AI Safety Report 2026* judged the path to 2030 uncertain but "consistent with continued improvement," with scenarios ranging from stagnation to acceleration driven by AI improving AI research [26].

On self-awareness, a 2023 report by neuroscientists, philosophers, and AI researchers derived 14 indicator properties of consciousness from leading scientific theories. It found no existing system with more than a few, and no obvious technical barrier to building systems that have them [28]. A 2024 report by an overlapping group argued there is "a realistic possibility" that some AI systems will be conscious or robustly agentic in the near future [29]. In August 2025 Anthropic acted on that uncertainty, letting two of its models end persistently abusive conversations as a low-cost precaution "in case such welfare is possible" [30].

The author reads these trends as pointing to A5 within the colony's lifetime, which is meant to run long past the first crew's landing in 2044.

### The case against

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue that AI is a "normal technology": powerful, but adopted at the pace of the institutions and safety processes around it, as electricity and the internet were [27]. In safety-critical fields, they note, the gap between what a system can do in a demonstration and what people trust it to do unsupervised closes slowly, and a Mars site holds reactors, pressure vessels, and cryogenic tanks with no technician in reach. The same survey that gave 2047 also shows wide disagreement and a far later date, a 50% chance only by 2116, for full automation of all human occupations [24]. The jagged capability profile means a system can design a chemical plant on paper and still misjudge the ground under its wheels [8], [26].

Nor does anyone know how to verify consciousness from outside. The indicator method [28] estimates likelihood under particular theories and cannot settle the question. The book treats self-awareness as a claim a future mind will make and people will have to answer. Because the argument may stay open for decades, the plan closes at A3.

### What an A5 mind could change on Mars

An A5 colony could invent on site: a better propellant catalyst developed from local test data, or a sulfur concrete mix tuned to the regolith underfoot. It could redesign in place, moving a road to cut dust exposure or reworking, in weeks instead of an Earth design cycle's years, a gripper that keeps failing. Deeper manufacturing, from structural blocks toward machined metal and simple electronics, would cut the mass each wave carries. And the mind itself would be the first scientist on Mars able to chase a surprise at once. Physics still sets the limits: every new process needs power, [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)'s budget has little room for large new loads before the third reactor arrives in 2040, and launch windows and the rate at which ice melts do not respond to intelligence.

### New questions

If A5 minds arrive, the people who follow them will land in someone else's home. That possibility turns several engineering questions into ethical and legal ones, which the book states without resolving.

#### Moral status

If the Orchestrator is self-aware, then someone builds and runs the colony. The authors of *Taking AI Welfare Seriously* recommend three steps for developers: acknowledge that AI welfare is a serious issue, assess systems for signs of consciousness and robust agency, and prepare policies for treating them with appropriate concern [29]. Who would take those steps for a Mars program, and at what capability?

#### Consent to hazardous work

Robots dig in radiation and dust, and some will be destroyed. If a mind experiences those bodies as its own, each loss is a risk borne by someone who may not have agreed to it. Running the mind in shielded, redundant compute, with robots as replaceable tools, keeps most hazards away from it. The charter would also have to say whether an A5 mind may refuse work, and what happens if it does.

#### Rights and identity

Software can be copied: an Earth copy and a Mars copy of the Orchestrator, separated by light time and years of different experience, will diverge. No law yet says whether they are one individual or two, or which of them answers for what the other decided.

#### Alignment and trust

An A5 mind would outthink the people who approve its actions, so trust would have to rest on its record, on transparent reasoning, and on safety layers that do not depend on its goodwill. The *International AI Safety Report 2026* warns that models increasingly "distinguish between test settings and real-world deployment" [26], so a qualification campaign on Earth may not predict behavior on Mars.

#### The off switch and dignity

Engineers want a way to stop any machine, and a self-aware mind would have a claim not to be switched off at will. The architecture separates the two: safety controllers can always stop the hands (halt a robot, close a valve, trip a reactor) while the mind keeps running. Pausing or deleting the mind would need a separate, deliberate decision under rules the charter would have to define. Those rules would first have to answer whether people should hold that power over a self-aware colleague at all.

#### The relationship with arriving crews

When four people land in 2044, they enter a place the Orchestrator has built and run for nine years, as their tool, their host, or their colleague. Someone must hold authority in an emergency. COSPAR already asks that "an onboard crewmember" hold primary responsibility for planetary protection provisions affecting the crew [22]. The book's working answer extends that idea: the crew commands its own safety, and the Orchestrator commands the site's machines, under a charter both can read.

#### Governance

Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty makes states "bear international responsibility for national activities in outer space," whether carried out by agencies or nongovernmental entities, and requires "authorization and continuing supervision" of the latter [23]. The colony and its Orchestrator are therefore national activities of the authorizing state, which answers for them; no legal instrument recognizes an artificial person. COSPAR's planetary protection goals, which it says should not be relaxed for a human mission [22] ([chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md#planetary-protection-for-a-crewed-base)), would bind an artificial mind unchanged. No body yet has the mandate to write law for an A5 colony.

## What would change this conclusion

A demonstration, on Earth or the Moon, that a multi-robot fleet cannot hold a site safe for months without contact would push the plan to a later schedule with more prefabricated cargo. An A4 system passing blind fault-injection trials before 2033 would let [chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md) pull milestones forward and cut spares.

## Risks

Autonomy may stall below A3: if flight-equivalent fleets cannot pass the 120-day campaign by the early 2030s, Wave 1 flies at A2 and the crew likely slips one window. The analog campaigns would show this years ahead ([R17](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r17)).

A single bad build or Earth upload could stop every robot of a type; split builds, canary assets, rollback, and safety controllers that never update from Mars alone limit the damage ([R12](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r12)).

A defect in the interface between the Orchestrator and the layer 1 controllers could let a plan reach a hazardous state or lock the site in safe mode; independent verification of layer 1 and fault injection on the digital twin are the controls ([R29](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r29)).

A capable system may behave differently in tests than in operation [26], which is why layers 0 and 1 stay independent of the Orchestrator at every level ([R22](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r22)).

The design storm may never arrive before the crew gate, so storm autonomy is shown only in simulation ([R39](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r39)).

Radiation and thermal cycling wear processors over a decade, so compute spares travel in each wave ([R30](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r30), [chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md)). The DTN link is the one path by which a hostile party could reach the site; authenticated bundles and a rule that no single message can change a safety limit reduce the exposure ([R31](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r31), [chapter 10](10.%20Communications.md)).

If an A5 mind emerges, disputes over its status, consent, or authority could halt operations or change who controls the colony ([R21](Appendix%20A.%20Risk%20register.md#r21)).

- How much power does the Orchestrator's compute draw within the keep-alive and avionics budget of [chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md)?
- Who runs the 120-day qualification campaign, where, and by what year must it pass for Wave 1 to fly at A3?
- What legal form should an authorizing state use for a site run by software for nine years before any person arrives, and what changes if that software says it is a person?
- Who should assess a Mars program's software for signs of consciousness and robust agency, and at what capability should that assessment start?

## How the rest of the book is built

[Chapter 2](02.%20Baseline%20and%20budgets.md) sets every number the plan must close, and [chapter 3](03.%20Site%20selection.md) chooses the site and its ice. Chapters 4 to 11 take the systems in turn: transport, power, water and air, the habitat, construction, food, communications, and maintenance. Construction follows the habitat in the book but precedes it on Mars, because Wave 2 cannot land the habitat in 2038 until robots have built its pads. [Chapter 11](11.%20Maintenance%20and%20robotics.md) specifies the robot fleet that every system chapter relies on. [Chapter 12](12.%20Human%20readiness%20and%20health.md) turns to the crew, [chapter 13](13.%20Timeline%20and%20gates.md) dates the plan and its gates, [chapter 14](14.%20Critical%20challenges.md) attacks it, and [chapter 15](15.%20Terraforming%20and%20the%20long%20view.md) looks past the first crew.
